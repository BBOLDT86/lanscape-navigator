The money · race by race · Kentucky

Kentucky's Senate money was spent on the May primary, not November

Billionaires, crypto firms and groups that don't name their donors spent tens of millions to choose Mitch McConnell's successor before May. Andy Barr won the seat by 12 points, after a general election in which only one side bought much advertising.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 10, 2026. 17 sources. All the states › · How Kentucky voted ›

Kentucky had its first open U.S. Senate seat in decades this year because Mitch McConnell retired. Most of the money in the race was spent before the May 19 Republican primary. On Nov. 3, Republican Rep. Andy Barr beat Democrat Charles Booker 784,869 to 612,357, a margin of 12.1 points, according to AP results published by The New York Times. Donald Trump carried the state by 30.6 points in 2024.

Kentucky did not elect a governor this year. The state holds those elections in odd-numbered years, and the next one is in 2027. The Watch has not finished its own reading of the Federal Election Commission's Kentucky data, so the federal dollar figures below come from news reports on FEC filings and are attributed to those reports.

Where the money came from

In April, Louisville Public Media reported that Senate candidates vying for the GOP nomination in Kentucky and their aligned super PACs have already spent a staggering $48 million in the primary race1. Most of it came from super PACs backing businessman Nate Morris. Fight for Kentucky PAC reported raising and spending more than $14 million just in the first three months of this year, with $10 million of that coming from Elon Musk1. The Kentucky Lantern reported that Konstantin Sokolov, the head of the Chicago-based private equity firm IJS Investments2, gave the group $500,000. Win it Back PAC, which is entirely funded by Pennsylvania finance billionaire Jeff Yass1, reported spending $5.4 million through March to attack Barr.

Barr's main outside ally was Keep America Great PAC, a super PAC. It reported raising and spending roughly $11.5 million through March, with about $10 million of that directed to ads boosting Barr and attacking Morris1. Its largest disclosed gifts were $1.3 million from cryptocurrency company Foris DAX, as well as $1.1 million from Defend Us Inc., a 501(c)(4) dark money group that does not have to disclose its donors3. "Dark money" is the term for exactly this kind of gift: the people who paid for it are never named. Trackers built on FEC filings list Defend American Jobs, the crypto industry's super PAC for Republican primaries, as spending $7.2M4 for Barr. In early April, KY PAC entered the Kentucky airwaves by reporting $980,000 spent on ads attacking Daniel Cameron.1

The general election was one-sided. An analysis of outside spending published by the Arkansas Advocate found that conservatives have spent $46 million there, compared with just $196,000 for liberals, but the vast majority of that money went toward the contested GOP primary5. After the primary, Super PAC American Mission, which gets its money from the artificial intelligence industry, has spent more than $880k supporting Barr6. The candidates' own money was just as uneven. Through Oct. 14, Barr had raised $12.54 million and Booker $1.94 million, according to FEC figures compiled by Legis1 (Barr raised $12.54 million in total receipts and disbursed $14.68 million... Booker raised $1.94 million in total receipts7). The biggest Democratic outside group in the state went to a House race instead: Gov. Andy Beshear's super PAC In This Together has spent $500,000 on ads supporting Democrat Zach Dembo in his competitive race for the central Kentucky congressional district8.

The ads

The primary ads were mostly attacks. Keep America Great's ad, set to air statewide9 as part of a $2.5 million buy, appear[ed] to highlight a 2021 contribution that Morris made to a PAC supporting Nikki Haley10. Fight for Kentucky ads have been a mix of ones boosting Morris as a Trump ally and attacking Barr as insufficiently anti-immigration1. Win It Back's "Amnesty Andy" spot cost nearly $750,000 on the spot, airing in Lexington, Evansville, Louisville and Bowling Green, according to AdImpact11, as reported by Axios. Barr replied, "I never have and never will support amnesty."11

Barr's own primary ads focused on cultural issues, including an ad declaring "It's not a sin to be white" and another where he cheersed to Immigration and Customs Enforcement6. In the fall his campaign changed tone. On Oct. 1 it released a spot featuring retired teacher and proud democrat, Dena, in support of Andy Barr12. The campaign said it was part of a seven figure ad buy across broadcast television and digital streaming services12.

Booker had little money for television. A reporter on KET's "Comment on Kentucky" said in October that Booker has not had an ad on TV since August and currently has no buys planned for October13, so his campaign relied mostly on social media. According to a Booker press release cited by Legis1, his Oct. 28 ad accused Barr of taking Wall Street campaign money and backing tariffs, the Iran war, and data centers7.

Did it work?

The primary money did not go the way its biggest spenders wanted. Louisville Public Media found that pro-Cameron spending in the race only making up less than 3% of the total spending in the race — and pro-Morris spending making up 57%1, yet polls showed Morris in third place. On May 1, Trump endorsed Barr and urged businessman Nate Morris, a Barr rival, to drop out of the race and take an ambassadorship. Morris agreed and promptly joined Trump in backing Barr.14 Barr then won the primary with 60.50%15 to Cameron's 30.82%. NBC News put the outcome down to Trump, not the advertising: it is President Donald Trump whose sought-after endorsement decided the race16. Musk's $10 million went to a candidate who was not on the ballot in May.

In the general election, the side that spent far more won. But Barr's 12.1-point margin was 18.5 points smaller than Trump's in 2024. The lopsided spending did not stop that shift toward Democrats, and the filings cannot show whether it made the shift smaller. The crypto and AI industries now have a senator they spent money to elect.
