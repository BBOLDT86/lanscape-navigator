Kansas Republicans Outspent Democrats for Governor; Democrats Outspent Them for Senate

National Republican groups spent more than twice what Democrats did on the governor's race, which is still uncalled. In the Senate race, which set a Kansas spending record, Democrats spent more and their candidate won.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 8, 2026. 18 sources. All the states › · How Kansas voted ›

Kansas voters saw two very different money fights before Nov. 3. In the U.S. Senate race, Democrats and their allies spent more and won. Adam Hamilton, a Methodist pastor, beat Sen. Roger Marshall 561,143 votes to 505,558, according to the Associated Press count published by The New York Times. That is a 5.1-point margin in a state President Donald Trump carried by 16 points in 2024. In the governor's race, national Republicans spent more than twice as much as Democrats on ads, and the race is still open. Democrat Cindy Holscher leads Republican Ty Masterson 551,250 to 543,612, and AP has not called it.

The Watch's read of Federal Election Commission data for Kansas wasn't ready when this was published. The Senate dollar figures below come from ad trackers and news reports, credited as they appear. The governor's race is a state race, so its money is reported to Kansas, not to the FEC.

Where the money came from

In the governor's race, most of the money came from the two national parties' governors' groups. The Republican Governors Association's Right Direction PAC laid down an ad buy of about $6.9 million from the end of the primary through the general election1. By late September, the Sunflower State Journal reported, the DGA had booked about $2.3 million in air time for Holscher, against $11.6 million the Republican Governors Association had reserved for Masterson2.

The Democratic Governors Association sent its money through the Kansas Values Institute. That is a social welfare group that's not required to disclose its donors3, which is what "dark money" means as a legal term: a 501(c)(4) nonprofit that doesn't have to say who funds it. It reserved $2.755 million in ad time3. As of Election Day, the Sunflower State Journal reported that national Republican groups spent more than $10 million, much of it on negative advertising targeting Holscher, while the Democratic Governor's Association spent nearly $4 million in support of Holscher4.

The two candidates raised similar amounts. Kansas Public Radio reported that Holscher raised $2.34 million from late July through Oct. 22, and Masterson raised roughly $2 million during the same period4. The Democratic primary had its own money story. Gov. Laura Kelly's Middle of the Road PAC turned out to be a funder of Heartland Priorities, a social welfare group that ran ads and developed a website opposing5 Holscher. The report showed that Middle of the Road gave $230,0005 to the group.

The Senate race cost far more. The AP, citing the ad tracker AdImpact, reported that Democrats committed $65 million to television and digital advertising, Republicans about $45 million, and the total, around $110 million, was easily a record for any race in Kansas6. In the third quarter, Hamilton reported raising $9.9 million through his campaign account7. Marshall raised $866,000. Among outside groups, the Senate Leadership Fund was the most significant spender for Marshall, spending at least $11 million in the state after it redirected money from North Carolina8. A Kansas-based super PAC, the People over Politics Action Fund, was the largest spender against Marshall, spending more than $11 million.8 Greg Orman, who ran for the Senate as an independent in 2014, contributed $50,000 to the anti-Marshall PAC in July as one of its first donors8. Lower Costs Now, a Democratic super PAC9, spent nearly $6 million against Marshall and $4.5 for Hamilton9. On the Republican side, Main Street Fund, a Republican-affiliated super PAC formed on Kansas' primary election day, spent nearly $4.6 million against Hamilton8.

The ads

The Republican Governors Association's first ad, "Crushing," began airing across Kansas starting Thursday, August 6th10. It said Holscher had opposed capping property taxes10. Later ads said she wanted to turn Kansas into California. A political scientist who reviewed the ads for KAKE-TV called some of their claims false, including "Holscher voted to spend your tax dollars on benefits for illegals"11. He also pointed out that California actually has a strict cap on property taxes11.

The Kansas Values Institute aired a clip in which Masterson appears to say "The Brownback plan, which by the way, I wrote."12 That refers to former Gov. Sam Brownback's income tax cuts. Masterson said the ad perpetrated a "lie"12 by cutting his words short. Another Kansas Values Institute ad called Masterson "big tech's biggest fan"11 over data centers. The same reviewer noted that Holscher also voted in favor of11 the bill. Masterson's own 30-second ad, "Kansas values," was part of a seven-figure television and digital program13. It shows a teacher, a union member and Democrats who say they will vote for him.

In the Senate race, Marshall and national Republicans looked to target Hamilton's faith as "woke," running ads that splice quotes from Hamilton's sermons14. Hamilton's side went after Marshall's medical career. Hamilton hit Marshall over a New York Times report that Marshall's former medical practice sued more than 700 former patients over unpaid bills15.

Did it work?

In the Senate race, the side that spent more won. The AP pointed to other reasons as well. With only a nominal primary challenge, Marshall's campaign didn't start its ads until late August and his fundraising lagged.16 The AP also described the result as a sign of how far unhappiness with President Donald Trump has spread16.

In the governor's race, the side that spent less is ahead. KSN reported that as of Friday evening, Holscher is leading Masterson by about 7,600 votes.17 The election is under a close-race audit, as required by Kansas law, because the margin between Holscher and Masterson is less than 1%.17 Holscher has declared victory, and Kelly has called her "Governor-elect." Masterson has not conceded. He said "the margin is narrow enough that Kansas law calls for another look."18 Until the count is final, the race is undecided, and so is the question of what the Republican spending advantage bought.
