Moore Outspent Cox Many Times Over, Then Spent Late on Redistricting

Gov. Wes Moore had about 48 times Dan Cox's cash heading into the fall. He won a second term easily and spent his closing ad money on Question 3, which passed even though polls had shown it losing.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 10, 2026. 22 sources. All the states › · How Maryland voted ›

The Federal Election Commission figures The Watch reads showed no outside spending in Maryland's congressional races. So the money story here comes from state filings and the local press. It is mostly about a governor who had far more money than his race needed and found another use for it.

Where the money came from

Gov. Wes Moore, a Democrat, raised his money early. By June, his campaign committee, Wes Moore for Maryland, reported raising almost $13.8 million, close to the total of the next nine largest committees in the state combined, according to the Maryland State Board of Elections.1 His running mate, Lt. Gov. Aruna Miller, raised an additional $1.7 million1. Dan Cox, the former Frederick County delegate Moore beat in 2022, had raised $74,699 and had $30,345 in the bank.1

The gap stayed wide after the June 23 primary. In the next state filings, as Fox 5 D.C. reported, Moore's campaign ended the reporting period with nearly $6.5 million in cash, compared with approximately $136,000 for Cox2. Cox also reported $147,889 in outstanding obligations2, which was more than he had in the bank.

A second, separate pot of money went to a ballot question. Question 3 clarifies that certain constitutional requirements for drawing legislative districts apply to Maryland Senate and House of Delegates districts, not congressional districts.3 In practice, that frees the Democratic legislature to redraw the congressional map. The committee backing it, Fight Back MD, received $2,280,017.94 in donations4 between Aug. 19 and Oct. 18, including nearly $1.4 million from national Democratic political action committees.5 The other side had far less. One opposing group, Protect Maryland Votes, has been advocating against Question 3 with a budget of about $200,000 so far5, The Baltimore Banner reported.

The opposition money that drew the most attention went through Truth for Maryland, a PAC formed in October. Its filings showed one funder, the Progressive Prosperity Caucus, which donated $325,000.6 Because the Caucus is a nonprofit, it is not required to disclose its donors6. That arrangement is what "dark money" means as a legal term: the people who actually paid are never named. The nonprofit lists an Omaha, Nebraska address7, and according to the Banner, Democrats in other states believe it is a front for Republicans.6 The PAC's chair, Republican strategist Andrew Brightwell, said in a statement that the group was "proud of the progressive support we have received in this fight"6.

The ads

Moore's reelection ads ran almost everywhere. His campaign is airing its TV ads on every Baltimore news station but one: Fox 45, Sinclair Broadcast Group's flagship.8 In D.C., the campaign did not buy time on WJLA, the Sinclair station there. All told, it spent more than $1 million to air its ads on stations across Maryland, according to Federal Communications Commission records.8 Moore is in a public feud with David Smith, Sinclair's executive chairman, who owns The Baltimore Sun and has sued Moore for defamation.

In the spring, Moore's campaign also put money into the Republican primary. Its video ads call Cox "too conservative for Maryland" for being aligned with President Donald Trump9, and they ran on Fox News and Facebook.

By mid-October Moore's own race was not in doubt, so he moved his ad money. The Washington Post reported that Moore switched roughly $750,000 in campaign ads10 in the D.C. market to promote Question 3; NBC News put the figure at $700,000. The new ad featured state Democrats, among them Rep. Steny Hoyer11. Next came former President Barack Obama, who recorded a 30-second TV ad urging Marylanders to vote yes. It aired in the Baltimore and Washington television markets and on digital platforms, and Moore's campaign paid for it.12 The first buy was $500,000.

Fight Back MD spent $550,000 on TV ads urging voters to vote in favor of Question 3, according to AdImpact13, in October alone. Its own filing for Oct. 5–18 showed roughly $112,000 on television ads, $100,000 on digital advertising and $166,000 on direct mail.14

Truth for Maryland borrowed Moore's slogan and colors. Its ads on streaming TV services encourage voters to vote "yes for Wes" but also "against Question 3."15 Its filing reported nearly $294,200 in spending on videos, online ads and mail7. Moore's lawyers sent the group a cease-and-desist letter. State elections officials warned Truth for Maryland that the group could face a "potential" violation of campaign finance laws.5 Republicans spent on the question as well: The Maryland Republican Party also has been sending out mailers against Question 3.5

Did it work?

Moore won by 1,278,118 votes to 647,110, a 31.5-point margin, in the AP count published by The New York Times. That is 2.7 points better than the Democratic presidential margin in Maryland in 2024. The Associated Press called the race for Moore one minute after the polls closed16, though Cox said he would wait for the full count. The side that spent far more won, but the result looks a lot like the rematch's 2022 version. In October, UMBC pollster Mileah Kromer said the numbers looked as you would expect, given the demographic characteristics of the state and the disproportionate fundraising numbers17.

Question 3 makes a stronger case that money mattered. An October poll showed 53% of likely Maryland voters planned to vote against Question 3 while 25% would support it.18 The measure then passed 60.0% to 40.0%, 1,076,264 votes to 716,67919, with 98% of precincts in. Reports disagree on how much Moore put behind it. The Banner said more than $1.2 million on advertising20; Salisbury station WMDT reported $4 million on ads supporting the ballot question21. A caveat: the UMBC survey was conducted before the groups backing and opposing the ballot question began spending heavily13. Opponents blamed the loss on Democratic tactics. A Protect Maryland Votes adviser called it "the predictable result when the dominant political party does everything in its power, at every step, to deceive Maryland voters."22
