The money · race by race · Hawaii

Hawaii's Green outraised Cordery nine to one, then won by a record

No national group spent money on Hawaii's race for governor. Gov. Josh Green's $3.9 million campaign, along with a carpenters-funded super PAC that backed his running mate, far outspent a Republican campaign that raised $430,800. The Democrats won by 45 points.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 11, 2026. 14 sources. All the states › · How Hawaii voted ›

Hawaii's biggest race this year drew no national money. The Federal Election Commission figures The Watch uses show no independent expenditures in the state's federal races this fall. The governor's race is a state contest, and its money is reported to the Hawaii Campaign Spending Commission. Gov. Josh Green, a Democrat, beat Republican Gary Cordery 278,652 votes to 104,926, a margin of 45.3 points in the Associated Press count. Hawaii voted Democratic by 23.1 points in the 2024 presidential race, so Green ran 22.2 points ahead of that result.

Where the money came from

Green raised $3.9 million in the current election cycle and still had $1.3 million in cash on hand1, according to state filings reported by Honolulu Civil Beat. Cordery brought in $430,800 over the same cycle, spent nearly all of it, and had a surplus of just $3,7001. That works out to roughly nine dollars for every one.

Green's donors included well-known local executives such as Paul Kosasa of ABC Stores, Scott Seu of Hawaiian Electric Industries, Arnold Martines of Central Pacific Bank and Patrick Sullivan of Oceanit1. Earlier filings showed bank employees among his top donors, including First Hawaiian Bank ($19,289) and Central Pacific Bank ($8,000)2. Former Bank of Hawaii CEO Peter Ho gave $6,000, the maximum amount allowed in gubernatorial races. Green received 17 such donations.2 Civil Beat described Cordery's donors as largely unknown1. His running mate, Daniel Anthony, brought in only $24,7143. University of Hawaii political scientist Colin Moore put it plainly: "Hawaii is a dominant Democratic state and most of the wealthy interests support Democrats."4

The ticket's biggest outside money went to the lieutenant governor's race. Pacific Resource Partnership, a consortium made up of the 6,000-member Hawaiʻi carpenters union and the more than 250 contractors that employ them5, runs a super PAC called For A Better Tomorrow. Its money comes from the Hawaii Carpenters Market Recovery Program Fund1. As of mid-October, the super PAC had reported raising $8.3 million this election and spending $10.7 million1. Before the August primary it put more than $2 million on Kawakami's behalf1, backing Kauai Mayor Derek Kawakami, who became Green's running mate. Kawakami's own campaign raised $1.3 million, and the governor himself has contributed nearly $70,000 to his running mate for media production services1.

The ads

In the last week of the campaign, Green's ads were running all over Hawaiʻi's four main TV stations, cable networks, Facebook and Instagram. "Trusted Caring Leadership," one ad declares.1 His campaign also bought a front-page ad in the Honolulu Star-Advertiser and spent more than $400,000 from Sept. 27 to Oct. 191. Both candidates named the cost of living as their top issue. Green touted tax cuts he worked on with the Legislature and their doubling of the earned income tax credit and food tax credit.6

Cordery's campaign was harder to find. Civil Beat noted that voters might have heard a Cordery spot on KWME radio1 or seen one of his boosted posts on Meta. He is a general contractor, and his pitch was that his own children and grandchildren had left Hawaii because they could not afford to live there. He promised to eliminate the general excise tax on food and medicine6. Green rebuffed repeated calls to debate7 him.

For A Better Tomorrow paid KGMB, KITV and KIKU to air television commercials beginning March 248. Its 30-second spot use Kawakami's own words from his state of the county address9. Kawakami's campaign said it did not coordinate with outside groups. In the fall, the super PAC spent more than $50,000 in the past month for TV and cable ads1 for Kawakami, though the race was not competitive.

The only super PAC money Civil Beat reported in a federal race came in the August primary, before the period the FEC general-election figures cover. Future Progress, a Washington-based super PAC, reported spending $75,000 on digital advertising10 for state Sen. Jarrett Keohokalole, who was challenging U.S. Rep. Ed Case. Its donors were what campaign-finance writers call "dark money" in that window: the super PAC won't be required to disclose its donors until after voters have cast their ballots10. Filings released in October showed that the shipping company Matson had paid for the ads.

Did it work

The side that spent more won, and by a wide margin. Civil Beat reported that Green won by the largest margin of victory in state history7. It concluded that Cordery's money was not nearly enough to counter a flood of advertising on behalf of the Democrats7. Reported margins differed on election night. The Star-Advertiser reported 50 percentage points11 from early returns, while Civil Beat's Wednesday figures showed a margin of nearly 46 percentage points7. The AP count used here is 45.3.

Money was probably not the only reason. Green entered the general election with a 64% approval rating among registered Hawaii voters, according to a Morning Consult ranking11. In early October, the prediction market Kalshi gave Cordery a scant 3% chance of winning12. In July, before the primary, Moore said Cordery's depleted war chest will limit his ability to compete in the general election4.

The money seemed to matter more in the primary. Kawakami beat state Rep. Della Au Belatti, 62.2% of the vote to Belatti's 24.5%13. Belatti said "The odds were long that we were going to win this because of the late entry, the $2 million that clearly was spent"13. In the fall, much of the money went to a race whose result was never in doubt. Longtime strategist Chuck Freedman said that big donors "scratch their heads when the Green campaign reaches out for more money"14 for a race without serious competition.
