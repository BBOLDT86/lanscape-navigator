The money · race by race · New York

Uber and Dolan money poured into New York's lopsided governor's race

Gov. Kathy Hochul beat Bruce Blakeman by nearly 20 points. She had far more money, both in her campaign and in outside groups, while the money for Blakeman came largely from businesses linked to James Dolan.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 8, 2026. 20 sources. All the states › · How New York voted ›

Gov. Kathy Hochul, a Democrat, won a second full term on Nov. 3. She beat Bruce Blakeman, the Republican Nassau County executive, 3,638,231 votes to 2,441,385, a margin of 19.6 points. That is 7 points better than the Democratic margin in New York's 2024 presidential vote. The Associated Press immediately declared New York Gov. Kathy Hochul the winner Tuesday in her reelection bid.1

The governor's race is a state contest, so its money never shows up in Federal Election Commission records. The Watch's federal data show no outside spending in New York's federal races. The figures below come from state Board of Elections filings, as reported by the state's press.

Where the money came from

Hochul's own campaign held a large lead. Hochul's campaign had about $19 million banked through September, compared to a measly $178,000 for Blakeman.2 Much of that came through her party. Gothamist reported that a $10 million transfer helped leave Hochul's campaign with about $19 million for the final month3, using a part of state law that allows them to take millions of dollars from their Democratic and Republican parties, bypassing caps on individual campaign contributions.3 Hochul's campaign chose not to participate in the public financing system.4 Blakeman did, and court rulings found he was entitled to millions of dollars in public matching funds.5 In an analysis published Nov. 7, New York Focus found that Hochul, a prodigious fundraiser, outspent Blakeman by $15 million6.

Super PACs, which New York law calls independent expenditure committees, added more. These groups can raise unlimited sums but may not coordinate with candidates. The Press-Republican of Plattsburgh reported that they had spent about $13.2 million as of Oct. 9 — more than double the amount of super PAC spending through the same period in 2022.2 Hochul has benefited from the lion's share of that spending, thanks to support from big players like Uber, the Greater New York Hospital Association, and the New York Apartment Association, a landlord group.2

Uber, the ride-hailing company, gave the most. According to the final pre-election filings, as tallied by Bushwick Daily, Uber gave $14.96 million to two pro-Hochul committees7. Uber had a policy stake in Albany. At her State of the State address, Hochul proposed a slate of Uber-supported auto-insurance reforms, which the company lobbied for and that would save it millions of dollars in insurance premiums and legal payouts.8 Hochul successfully pushed through the reforms as part of the state budget8.

Greater Empire State, a super PAC tied to the Democratic Governors Association, raised $4.34 million9. Former New York City Mayor Michael Bloomberg, the Greater New York Hospital Association Management Corp., and Leadership for New York each contributed $1 million9, according to state filings cited by The New York Post. City & State put Bloomberg's total at $1.5 million10 and listed the Sports Betting Alliance, which gave $1.2 million10. Leadership for New York is run by political strategist Jay Martin, a former executive director of the New York Apartment Association.9

On the Republican side, the money came mostly from one donor. Several big donors had poured $2.3 million into Safe and Strong New York through Aug. 4, according to state campaign finance records. The contributions include $1 million in total from Madison Square Garden bigwig James Dolan and another political committee he supported; $500,000 from Trilogy Advisors in New York City; $500,000 from Caryl Ratner.11 The final filings showed two Dolan-controlled MSG companies gave $2.7 million7 to the group. Other pro-Blakeman donors included casino mogul Stephen Wynn.2 A second pro-Blakeman super PAC, Defend Empire State, raised far less. Its top contributors are property management company Edelstein & Son Realty LLC, and hedge fund investor Jonathan Hellner, who both gave $50,00010.

What the ads said

According to Gothamist, Hochul used her robust war chest to air $30 million worth of ads touting her push to send energy rebate checks to New Yorkers, pause data center construction and expand access to childcare.12 Her first two general-election spots, "Every Penny" and "100%," ran on broadcast, cable, streaming, and digital platforms.13 Her final three ads, which started Oct. 27, accuse Blakeman of backing Medicaid cuts, higher health insurance costs and aggressive immigration enforcement.14

The outside groups mostly repeated her message. Greater Empire State's first ads attacked "MAGA Bruce Blakeman" over his support for what the group called "Donald Trump's cost-raising agenda that is hurting New York."9 Uber's ad was gentler. It showed children painting and eating spaghetti before a girl asks viewers to vote for Hochul. A viewer wouldn't know Uber is involved without delving into Board of Elections records on PAC spending.15

Not all of the outside money helped Hochul. The Transport Workers Union has launched ads calling Hochul "radioactive" to fellow Democrats amid a dispute over her veto of a bill requiring two-person subway crews.16

Blakeman tried to brand Hochul as "the worst governor in America," repeating it on the campaign trail and in his campaign advertisements.1 His use of artificial intelligence drew a complaint. The New York State Democratic Party filed a complaint with the state Board of Elections asking it to investigate Blakeman's use of AI.17 Strong and Safe New York paid for ads faulting the governor for rising utility costs and an outflow of New Yorkers to Florida and other states15. It also bought 13 billboards, at a bridge from Staten Island to New Jersey and on roads leading into Massachusetts and Vermont.11

Did the money work?

The side that spent more won, and won by a lot. Gothamist reported that Blakeman was hurt, in part, by a struggle to keep pace with Hochul's fundraising, particularly in the critical final weeks of the campaign.12 New York Focus came to a different conclusion: tens of millions of dollars spent on statewide races for governor and attorney general had little obvious impact, given the lopsided margins in those races.6 Other forces were also at work. Trump had an abysmal 29 percent approval rating in New York, according to a September Quinnipiac University poll18, and Hochul built her campaign around tying Blakeman to him.

The final accounting is not complete. Across all of New York's races, New York Focus counted more than $500 million in spending, with some recent spending not yet reported.6 Blakeman's numbers are also still being revised. His campaign had said earlier that its filings don't fully reflect the campaign's fundraising activity.4
