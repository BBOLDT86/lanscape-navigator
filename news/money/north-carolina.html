The money · race by race · North Carolina

Republicans led North Carolina's Senate ad spending, then pulled out as Cooper won

The Senate Leadership Fund promised $71 million to elect Michael Whatley and stopped at about $30 million. Roy Cooper's campaign raised more than three times Whatley's total and won by nearly 20 points.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 10, 2026. 24 sources. All the states › · How North Carolina voted ›

Roy Cooper, the Democratic former governor, beat Michael Whatley, the former Republican National Committee chairman, in North Carolina's open U.S. Senate race on Nov. 3. The Associated Press count, as published by The New York Times, gave Cooper 2,467,972 votes to Whatley's 1,643,114, a margin of 19.6 points. Donald Trump carried the state by 3.2 points in 2024. Cooper becomes the first Democrat elected to the Senate from North Carolina since 2008.1 North Carolina did not elect a governor this year, so the Senate race topped the ballot.

The Watch's own Federal Election Commission data for North Carolina had not been processed when this was written. The figures below come from filings and ad-tracking data as the outlets named here reported them.

Early in the year, spending was projected to set a Senate campaign record at or above $1 billion.2 In the end, total ad spending was around $90 million, according to AdImpact data reported by WUNC3.

Where the money came from

The candidates' fundraising was lopsided. According to FEC records reported by WRAL, Cooper had raised $47.2 million entering October, with $12.5 million on hand, while Whatley had raised $13.4 million, with $916,000 on hand as of Sept. 30.4 $27.15 million of Cooper's receipts were transfers from other authorized committees, compared with $5.10 million for Whatley5. Those transfers are mostly money from joint fundraising committees.

Most of the outside money for Whatley came from the Senate Leadership Fund, the super PAC aligned with Senate Republican leaders. In the spring it pledged $71 million to help Whatley win the seat Republican Sen. Thom Tillis was leaving.6 It spent about $30 million on ads before moving the rest to Republican campaigns in other states7. Bloomberg put its North Carolina spending at $32 million. The fund raises money nationally. Its largest donation, $158 million, came from Elon Musk8. It has also received at least $46 million from One Nation, a 501(c)(4) nonprofit that does not have to disclose its donors.9 That is what "dark money" means in the legal sense: political money whose original donors are not made public. Because the fund spends across many states, none of its donors can be tied to the North Carolina ads in particular.

A second Republican super PAC, Old North Action, cut $6.5 million in October ad reservations, according to AdImpact10. It still had about $8.4 million in pro-Whatley ads reserved as of Oct. 8.7 The reporting The Watch reviewed did not identify Old North Action's donors.

On the Democratic side, Senate Majority PAC committed $32 million to the race11. Much of its spending went through WinSenate, a super PAC run by Senate Majority PAC12. FEC data through March show Senate Majority PAC's largest contributors include Majority Forward, the George Soros-funded Democracy PAC and retired media executive Fred Eychaner.13 Majority Forward is also a nonprofit that does not disclose its donors.

The ads

The Republican groups ran on crime. In a pair of Senate Leadership Fund ads, two North Carolina fathers described their children's killings.14 In one, Stephen Federico says his daughter Logan was killed in a home invasion in Columbia, South Carolina, and then says Cooper "let 3,500 criminals out on the street; 18 of them were murders."15 The 3,500 figure refers to a 2021 legal settlement over crowded prisons during the COVID-19 pandemic. FactCheck.org found that Cooper had nothing to do with the accused killer's release, because the suspect was released from a South Carolina prison.15 Federico told PolitiFact he does not believe the ad blames Cooper for his daughter's death.14 Donald Hayes, the father in the second ad, told NC Newsline he was "swindled into an ad that was about politics"16. The Senate Leadership Fund said it stands by its message17.

The Democrats ran on Whatley's career as a lobbyist. WinSenate's ad attacked Whatley for cashing in as a Big Oil lobbyist while North Carolinians paid more for gas18. The $5 million campaign ran on TV stations statewide.18 Cooper's own ad, "Guys Like Him," covered Whatley's career lobbying for oil companies and utilities, including Duke Energy19. The political arms of three environmental groups, EDF Action Votes, LCV Victory Fund and NRDC Action Votes, spent $1.5 million on an ad called "Their Man." It ran on streaming TV, YouTube and digital audio through Sept. 28.20

Republican ads largely stopped in October. AdImpact reported that Whatley's last TV ad aired Oct. 721. At that point Cooper's campaign had $7.4 million reserved for future ads21. Outside groups had $4.7 million reserved for Whatley and $11.2 million for Cooper.21 The Senate Leadership Fund moved its money to Kansas, Semafor reported.6 Americans for Prosperity Action, a conservative super PAC, also announced a $22 million ad campaign for five Republican Senate candidates that did not include Whatley22.

Did it work?

Which side spent more depends on the period and on what is counted. Through late September, AdImpact data reported by CBS 17 showed that Republican groups had spent about $60 million, compared with $41 million by Democratic groups.10 By election night, NPR and WUNC reported that Cooper and national Democratic groups significantly outspent Whatley and Republicans.3 The two counts don't match: the September total alone is larger than the $90 million final figure WUNC cited, and The Watch could not reconcile them.

What can be said: Republican super PACs outspent Democrats during the summer, and Whatley still trailed. He never led in polling, and Cooper's lead widened over the course of the race.3 The money left after the polls had settled, not before. Andy Jackson of the John Locke Foundation, a conservative think tank, said the spending shift may have reflected the state of the race more than it caused it23. NPR noted that polls showed the economy, not crime, was North Carolina voters' top concern this year.24

The candidate who raised more won. The side whose outside groups spent more through September lost by almost 20 points.
