West Virginia: Capito's cash edge was vast, but her winning margin halved

Sen. Shelley Moore Capito and two super PACs, one tied to AI-industry investors, heavily outspent Democrat Rachel Fetty Anderson. Capito won a third term by 20 points. That is less than half her 2020 margin.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 9, 2026. 16 sources. All the states › · How West Virginia voted ›

Republican Shelley Moore Capito won election Tuesday to the U.S. Senate representing West Virginia, defeating Democrat Rachel Fetty Anderson.1 The Associated Press count, as carried by The New York Times, gave Capito 266,067 votes and Fetty Anderson 172,922, a margin of 20.4 points. Donald Trump carried the state by 41.9 points in 2024.

Two notes on sources. First, the Federal Election Commission figures The Watch uses had not yet been loaded for West Virginia. Second, West Virginia did not elect a governor this year. Patrick Morrisey has been governor since 2025.2 The Senate race was the top of the ballot. The dollar figures below come from campaign reports as covered by the Charleston Gazette-Mail and from trackers that compile FEC filings. Each is attributed.

Where the money came from

According to the Gazette-Mail, Capito's campaign reported roughly $3.07 million on hand after securing about $6.75 million in net contributions3 this cycle, in a report filed Oct. 22. It took some $3.55 million from individuals and $3.35 million from political committees.3 The campaign reported receiving $511,225 via political action committee conduit through the American Israel Public Affairs Committee3. In other words, the PAC of the American Israel Public Affairs Committee, a pro-Israel lobbying group, collected individual donations and passed them on to the campaign. Most of those individual donors were from out of state3, according to Capito's own reports. She also took money from committees tied to data centers and fossil fuel industries as well as state miners and farmers3.

Fetty Anderson's campaign raised $164,5584 through Oct. 14, according to an FEC-based summary by the site Legis1. She had $35,6814 in cash on hand. She had told volunteers that her campaign chose to run without corporate financing and industry financing5.

Two super PACs spent money supporting Capito. None of the trackers reviewed for this story showed meaningful outside money for Fetty Anderson. Legis1's FEC record listed just $368 in total outside spending supporting Democratic candidates4. The first super PAC, Take Me Home WV Action, spent $337,5856 before the May primary, according to the tracker Votelog. Votelog shows another $49,9756 paid in October and $49,1416 in September to a vendor, LandmarksCM LLC. No press reporting found for this story identified the group's donors. The second super PAC, American Mission, reported two transactions totaling $210,193.20 supporting Capito on Sept. 234. American Mission is the Republican arm of Leading the Future, a super PAC network focused on lobbying for policies friendly to the artificial intelligence industry.7 Leading the Future's largest disclosed contributors included venture capital company Andreessen Horowitz, which contributed $25 million, and OpenAI president Greg Brockman and his wife8. The Brockmans gave a combined $25 million. Leading the Future gave $5 million each9 to American Mission and to two other affiliates.

The ads

Take Me Home WV Action ran TV spots for Capito, and its message changed over time. Gazette-Mail columnist Phil Kabler described its first spot, from late 2025. That ad called Capito "The leader President Trump trusts to fight for West Virginia"10 and noted she had his full endorsement. The 30-second spot featured four photographs of Capito with Trump.10 The group's fall spot dropped Trump entirely. It called Capito "the independent, caring voice we need in Washington."10 Kabler wrote that Capito's own campaign spots, at least the ones I've heard, also ignore Trump.10

Capito began her campaign differently. She announced with a video that touts her support for President Donald Trump and his agenda.11 The video also promoted the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Trump's tax and spending law.

According to Votelog, American Mission's West Virginia money went to a $202,6936 TV and radio buy and $7,5006 in production costs. Both were paid to Summit Ridge Media Group. No reporting found for this story described what the West Virginia ad said. In another race, The Christian Science Monitor found that an American Mission ad for a Georgia congressman makes no mention of artificial12 intelligence.

No paid TV campaign by Fetty Anderson turned up in the coverage reviewed. She relied on door-knocking. On the eve of the election she was hitting the road for Nicholas County to continue door-knocking efforts13. Her pitch, as described by the Parkersburg News and Sentinel, painted Capito as someone disconnected from the average working-class voter14 and backed by special interests, including AIPAC.

Did it work?

The side with the money won easily, as expected in a race the forecasters rated safe for Republicans. The money did not stop a large swing toward the Democrat, though. Capito's 20.4-point margin was less than half of Trump's in 2024. In 2020 Capito won a second term against Democrat Paula Jean Swearengin by a margin of 43.3%15. Her seat stayed Republican even as Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress nationally.4

The outside money was small next to Capito's own campaign spending. It cannot be shown whether it added to her margin or held off a bigger swing. For American Mission's backers, the West Virginia race was a safe bet. Reporting on the national results, WIRED found that Leading the Future saved the bulk of its contributions for candidates already expected to win16.
