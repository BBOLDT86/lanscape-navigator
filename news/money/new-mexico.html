Haaland Outspent Hull More Than Seven to One in New Mexico

Deb Haaland's record campaign fund and a $2.5 million ad push by a Democratic Governors Association-backed committee faced no reported Republican outside group. She won by 14.7 points. The state's Senate race swung harder with little outside money.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 9, 2026. 23 sources. All the states › · How New Mexico voted ›

Deb Haaland, the former U.S. interior secretary, beat Republican Gregg Hull, the former mayor of Rio Rancho, 416,176 votes to 309,436 on Nov. 3. That is a 14.7-point margin in the Associated Press count. She will be New Mexico's next governor and the first Native American woman elected governor in U.S. history.1 The money in the race flowed overwhelmingly one way.

The Watch had not finished reading the Federal Election Commission data for New Mexico's federal races when this was published. The data it did have show no outside spending in the U.S. Senate race, which Sen. Ben Ray Luján won 458,756 to 266,434 over Republican Larry Marker. This report follows the governor's race, a state contest whose campaigns report to the New Mexico secretary of state.

Where the money came from

Haaland's campaign broke state records. In the last reports before Election Day, the Albuquerque Journal found she had spent nearly $19.6 million since launching her campaign in February 2025 and had received about $20.5 million in contributions.2 A Journal analysis found that 65% of her money came from out of state. Her donors included Native American tribes, musicians and Oracle America, the Texas-based software company behind the controversial Project Jupiter data center campus in southern New Mexico.2 A lot of it came in small amounts. In one fall reporting period, Source NM counted roughly $1.9 million from a little more than 34,000 donations, an average of about $55.3

Hull had far less. By the Journal's count he spent slightly more than $2.6 million on his campaign.2 Filings showed 99% of his donors came from New Mexico.4 Among those who gave him the maximum were the Merrion Oil & Gas Corporation PAC, Brantley Farms and Delaware Ranch5, along with Chase Oil Corporation and three Melloy Brothers companies.6

The largest single source of outside money was Affordable NM, a state political committee that registered with the secretary of state in May.7 Because it spends independently of the candidates, it is not subject to the state's $12,400 per-person limit on donations to a gubernatorial candidate.8 The Santa Fe New Mexican reported that it received $1.58 million from the Democratic Governors Association in eight contributions9 and that the phone number on its registration rings at the association's Washington headquarters.10 Its other donors included Energy Transfer LP, a Dallas-based natural gas pipeline company with ties to Project Jupiter, which gave $250,000.11 Walmart heir Jim C. Walton gave $125,000, and a PAC affiliated with the United Food and Commercial Workers union gave $87,600.8 Kentucky Gov. Andy Beshear's super PAC, In This Together, gave $25,000.12 The state Republican Party called the Energy Transfer gift a "huge conflict of interest" for Haaland.8

Nothing came in on the other side. In October, the pollster Brian Sanderoff said no conservative-leaning PAC had yet come to Hull's rescue.13

The ads

Haaland opened the general election in late August with a six-figure TV ad campaign.14 The 30-second ad ran on network stations statewide and on digital platforms, and it did not mention Hull.14 Her later expense reports included several payments of more than $300,000 each to Sage Media, a Washington firm that specializes in progressive campaign advertising.3

Most of the attack ads came from Affordable NM. Its first ad, "Red Carpet," said that Hull rolled out the red carpet for Trump's rally and left taxpayers with the $240,000 bill.15 The 30-second spot aired on network stations across New Mexico.7 Hull answered that the city spent the money on security because of social media threats that never materialized.16 The Journal noted that the ad left out that Hull, at the same debate, defended his decision not to endorse Trump.16 The committee also bought time during the KOB-TV debate Haaland skipped. An Affordable NM attack ad ran in the first commercial break.17 In its final report, it listed more than $2 million in spending, mostly on ads against Hull, plus nearly $137,000 for polling.10 The governors association put the committee's ad total at $2.5 million.

The Democratic Party of New Mexico ran its own 30-second digital ad, "New Mexico's Trump," on Hull's statements about cutting SNAP and Medicaid and ending universal child care.18

Hull promised to run a positive campaign. He said he had turned down money from donors who wanted him to go negative against Haaland.19 A 60-second ad on his social media accounts promoted his record as mayor.14 His campaign said its ads would run on streaming services, cable and broadcast TV.20 State filings compiled by Transparency USA list his largest payees as Red Surge, at $750,755, and Peakland Strategies, at $619,126.21

Did the money work?

The side that spent more won, and by more than the polls suggested. In October, the Journal Poll had Haaland at 49% and Hull at 42%.13 She won by 14.7 points, 8.7 points better than the Democratic margin in the 2024 presidential race.

The Senate race complicates that picture. Luján won by 26.5 points, 20.5 points better than the 2024 margin, and the FEC data contain no outside spending there. The governor's race also fell on a good night for Democrats across the country.22 That suggests the national climate did much of the work in both races.

Hull's campaign argued it won more voters per dollar19, and the simple arithmetic backs that up. His roughly $2.6 million comes to about $8 per vote received. Haaland's $19.6 million comes to about $47 per vote. Both totals include primary spending, and her primary against Sam Bregman was the more expensive one. Readers can decide whether the extra spending bought the margin or just padded a win the year was already delivering.
