Cotton-linked super PAC spent millions defining Shoffner; Cotton won by 12

Outside money in Arkansas's Senate race went almost entirely to one side, mostly through a super PAC run by Sen. Tom Cotton's allies. Cotton won easily, but his margin was far below Donald Trump's in 2024.

Sen. Tom Cotton won a third term on Nov. 3, defeating Democrat Hallie Shoffner 480,627 votes to 375,647, according to Associated Press results published by The New York Times. That is an 11.9-point margin in a state Donald Trump carried by 30.6 points in 2024. In the governor's race, Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders beat Democratic state Sen. Fred Love by 15.3 points.

The outside money in the Senate race was one-sided. Federal Election Commission figures show $4.1 million in independent expenditures. All of it helped Cotton, and none of it helped Shoffner. That works out to about $4.67 for every vote cast.

Where the money came from

Almost all of it came from America One. The Arkansas Democrat-Gazette reported that this super PAC notes two joint fundraising representatives in its current statement of organization: the Cotton Victory committee and the Cotton Majority Committee.1 The FEC figures credit America One with $3.2 million against Shoffner and $665,000 for Cotton. Local reporting counted more. By Election Day, the Democrat-Gazette's tally showed America One had directed $4.5 million on media opposing Shoffner2.

America One reported $6,552,560.003 in total receipts from January 2025 through Oct. 14, 2026. Its best-known funder is a nonprofit. The Democrat-Gazette reported that America One Policies, a 501(c)(4) nonprofit organization, contributed $450,000 to the super PAC4 in the third quarter alone. Groups organized as 501(c)(4)s do not have to name their donors. That is what "dark money" means in the legal sense: political money whose original donors are not disclosed. Talk Business & Politics reported in 2019 that Republican supporters of U.S. Sen. Tom Cotton, R-Ark., are forming a 501(c)(4) to promote conservative policies5. Cotton's own fundraising committee also sent money to the super PAC: $36,889 to America One4 in the third quarter.

The second-largest outside spender was Arkansans for Democracy and Justice. The committee registered with the FEC last December, listing an address in Mountain Brook, Ala., in its statement of organization. Its first contribution came from America One in the form of a $200,000 donation dated Jan. 8.1 The FEC data show it spent $180,000 against Shoffner. Two law-enforcement PACs added $46,000 and $37,000 for Cotton.

The candidates' own money was also lopsided. Cotton's campaign raised $14.5 million and Shoffner's $4.0 million. In late September, Punchbowl News cited AdImpact data showing that Cotton and his allies have spent $13.6 million, while Shoffner and Democrats have spent just $208,703.6

The governor's race was even more uneven. According to the secretary of state's filings, through Oct. 24, Sanders spent a total of $5.7 million on television advertising, direct mail and other advertising... In contrast, Love spent $60,181 on other advertising7 over the same period.

What the ads said

America One's spring TV ad starred a man identified only as "Marcus from Harvey." The Arkansas Times found that he is Republican state Rep. Marcus Richmond, and that the ad attacked Shoffner's contributions to "Democrats behind the Green New Deal."8 Contracts with America One are on file at KLRT-TV in Little Rock. America One paid $713,069 on July 24 to Full Reach Media Group LLC in Arlington, Va., for media production and media placement opposing Hallie Shoffner.9 Its later ads focused on her comments on migrant workers and opposition toward the Trump administration's controversial immigration enforcement agenda.2 Arkansans for Democracy and Justice relied on text messages. One sent Jan. 17 called Shoffner "a radical lunatic who couldn't win a general election in our state if her life depended on it"1.

Cotton's campaign took up the same theme. In September, Cotton's team began running TV ads quoting Shoffner saying in a podcast interview that Arkansas farmers would "trade five Americans for just one" undocumented immigrant worker.10 Shoffner answered that "Tom Cotton is going to clip up everything that I say, and he's going to mischaracterize it"10.

Shoffner's own advertising arrived late and on a much smaller scale. In mid-October she announced two new ads that her campaign said are part of a $1.1 million buy. They include a 30-second spot featuring former Democratic Gov. Mike Beebe supporting Shoffner's bid.11

In the governor's race, Sanders ran an ad about Medicaid work requirements featuring a fictional man named "Brad." It aired statewide as part of a six-figure ad buy12 and is drawing criticism from the NAACP Arkansas State Conference and sparking online debate over its portrayal of a white man receiving public assistance.13

Google's political ad library is less revealing. Its largest "political" advertiser in Arkansas was Kalshi Inc., a prediction market, at $963,000. Americans for Prosperity Action spent $237,000 in the state, the National Republican Senatorial Committee (NRSC) $86,000 and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee (DSCC) $82,000. The report does not show what those ads said in Arkansas.

Did it work?

The side with more money won both races, by wide margins. Polling moved in Cotton's direction once the ads began. Talk Business & Politics had Shoffner leading the senator 47.4% to 44%14 in August. By October, the same poll had Cotton with 50% and Shoffner with 41%.14 Roby Brock, the editor-in-chief of Talk Business, said of the advertising: "Clearly, this has had some impact in the numbers."15

The results also cut the other way. Cotton's margin was 18.8 points smaller than Trump's in 2024. In the governor's race, where outside groups reported no federal-style spending and Love spent almost nothing on advertising, the margin still shrank by 15.3 points. Shoffner was outspent heavily and still ran 3.4 points closer than Love. Punchbowl News raised the possibility in September that because Shoffner is airing so few of her own ads, Cotton is likely raising voters' awareness of her candidacy.6 The money bought Cotton a large share of the airtime and a comfortable win. Whether it changed the outcome is for the reader to judge.
