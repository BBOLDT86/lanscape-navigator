The money · race by race · Louisiana

Landry-tied super PAC backed Letlow; Davis, with little outside help, nearly won

Most of the outside money in Louisiana's Senate race was spent in the spring, when Julia Letlow ousted Sen. Bill Cassidy. In the fall, Republicans spent late, Democrats barely at all, and Letlow won by 1.8 points.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 13, 2026. 15 sources. All the states › · How Louisiana voted ›

Julia Letlow, a Republican congresswoman from Monroe, won Louisiana's open U.S. Senate seat on Nov. 3. She got 745,963 votes and Jamie Davis, a Democrat, got 720,106. Davis is a farmer and a former member of the Tensas Parish Police Jury. Donald Trump carried Louisiana by 22 points in 2024, and Letlow won by 1.8. The Associated Press did not call the race for Letlow until 99% of votes had been counted.1

The Watch had not finished reading the Federal Election Commission's data for Louisiana when this went to press. The outside-spending figures below come from FEC filings as reported by the press and by sites that track FEC data, and each is attributed. Louisiana does not elect a governor this year. Democrats are already looking at a slate of Republican statewide elected officials next year, including Gov.2 Jeff Landry.

Where the money came from

Most of the outside money was spent before the general election. It came in the Republican primary, when Letlow, with Trump's endorsement, ran against the incumbent, Bill Cassidy. By mid-May, the Accountability Project, a super PAC aligned with Gov. Jeff Landry, put $6 million behind the effort to elect Letlow, while the Louisiana Freedom Fund was on pace to eclipse $10 million to keep Cassidy in office.3 Cassidy's campaign and Louisiana Freedom Fund, a super PAC backing Cassidy, spent nearly $22 million on ads, according to AdImpact.4 That super PAC's FEC registration lists the Cassidy Leadership Fund as a "joint fundraising representative."5

Over the whole cycle, FEC data compiled by Votehound show the Accountability Project with $9,522,1936 in independent expenditures supporting Letlow. The Advocate described it as a super PAC funded by Trump and Landry7. One large source was another super PAC, Hope in Action PAC. According to Legis1's review of the filings, Hope in Action gave about $2.86 million to the Accountability Project, and Frank Scott Moran contributed $250,000, while the energy company Commonwealth LNG LLC contributed $250,000.8

The candidates raised similar amounts in the general election. Letlow's campaign has collected far more money than Davis', with $7 million compared to his $5 million.9 Davis, though, had more cash on hand than Letlow heading into the final weeks.10 Outside money arrived late. The Lens reported, using Capitol Hill Access data, that nearly three-quarters of the $1.75 million spent in October by outside groups in support of Letlow came between October 26th and 31st11, most of it from the Accountability Project. The Republican Senate leadership's super PAC, the Senate Leadership Fund, showed up last. The Lens put its spending at $8,427 on "text messaging."11 VoteLog's later tally of FEC filings, which runs through Election Day, lists a $144,98712 Senate Leadership Fund payment on Nov. 2 for texts and phone calls.

The Democratic side had almost no outside money. VoteLog counts $2,798,284 benefiting Letlow12 after the June primary runoffs and $128,619 for Davis. All of the Davis money came from Black Voters Matter Action PAC, which spent it on phone banking. Davis received essentially no money from national Democratic donors and groups, such as the Democratic Senate Campaign Committee2, the Louisiana Illuminator reported.

The ads

The primary ads were rough. Cassidy's side accus[ed] her of failing to properly disclose her stock trading while in office and pursuing DEI policies as an official at a Louisiana university.13 Letlow's super PAC spent most of its money on the third candidate, state Treasurer John Fleming. Accountability Project, spent about 10 times more in ads targeting Fleming than it did targeting Cassidy over the course of the race, according to AdImpact.4

In the general election, Davis picked up the stock-trading attack. On Oct. 14 his campaign released two ads. Fox 8 reported that one of them said Letlow had signed a nondisclosure agreement with Meta about a Richland Parish data center. Letlow then buys stock in profitable companies building the data center, failing to disclose her shady purchases for over a year.14 Letlow has played down those investments, claiming she was unaware her financial advisers were buying the stocks in question.1 Letlow's own campaign made a $900,000 television advertising purchase right before the Oct. 15 Federal Election Commission reporting deadline9.

The Accountability Project's ad against Davis drew the most attention. A $400,000 attack ad began airing across the state11 in the last week of October. In it, Davis is depicted by an AI rendering, lumbering slowly towards the screen11, while text on screen lists policy positions it says he holds. WAFB said the ad attempts to align Davis with extreme left-wing policies.15 The Advocate reported that many of his supporters called it a racist trope.7

Did it work?

The side that spent more won, by 25,857 votes. Whether the late money made the difference is disputed. The pollster John Couvillon told The Lens the ad came too late: "why else would they do that ad against Jamie Davis at the very last minute after 500K (people) had voted?"11 Trump also stepped in late. Trump had no involvement in the Louisiana race until he staged a "tele-rally" for Letlow on the eve of the election1.

Turnout favored Davis. Early-voting figures showed 34% participation from Black voters, a new high for a congressional midterm election in Louisiana, and 17,000 more Democrats than Republicans casting ballots statewide.1 Davis received 720,106 votes, the most ever for a Black candidate in a statewide election in Louisiana1. Even so, the outside spending, the money from Landry's network and Trump's endorsement all backed the winner.
