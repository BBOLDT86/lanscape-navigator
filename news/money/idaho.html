The money · race by race · Idaho

Idaho's incumbents outspent their challengers and won, by narrower margins than Trump

Jim Risch and Brad Little had far more money than their opponents and both won easily. Very little outside money came in, and the largest late Democratic ad buy was for an abortion-rights measure that failed.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 7, 2026. 21 sources. All the states › · How Idaho voted ›

Idaho's 2026 races were paid for mostly by the candidates' own campaigns. The Federal Election Commission data The Watch used shows no outside spending in the state's federal races. The governor's race and a ballot measure are covered by state filings.

Where the money came from

Sen. Jim Risch, the Republican incumbent, went into his race against independent Todd Achilles with a large money lead. The Achilles campaign this election cycle has spent more than $1.7 million, and Risch's campaign has spent over $4.7 million, according to federal campaign finance data1, the Idaho Capital Sun reported. Risch has also pulled in $1.4M from PACs2. That includes $62,000 from the National Republican Senatorial Committee, the Senate Republicans' party committee, and $71,600 from the 2026 Senators Classic Committee PAC3. About $1.2 million of Achilles' money came from individual donors, and Achilles is his own top individual contributor, having given $33,500 to his campaign in contributions and a loan2.

In September, Risch mailed Idahoans a fundraising letter signed by Vice President JD Vance. It claimed Democrats were "prepared to pour millions of dollars into Idaho"4 to beat Risch. That money never showed up in the FEC data. The only outside spending anyone has reported was small. A summary of filings by Legis1, a legislative-tracking service, lists three payments from Forward Party Victory Fund Inc., totaling $105,000, with transaction dates from October 14 to October 215. It also lists a $14,800 transaction in support of Achilles5 from United Independents Campaign Fund.

In the governor's race, Gov. Brad Little's campaign said it had raised over $2.4 million. This breaks the fundraising record for a statewide candidate reelection6. According to Sunshine, the Idaho secretary of state's campaign-finance database, Little raised almost $1.2 million from people, compared to nearly $1.1 million from companies7. Idaho Education News reported that the Idaho Automobile Dealers Association, the Idaho Association of Realtors and the Idaho Association of Bankers each floated Little maxed-out $10,000 donations8. Hospitals and agricultural companies were also among his top donors. Little's opponents were Democrat Terri Pickens, a Boise lawyer, who has raised nearly $250,0009, and independent John Stegner, a retired Idaho Supreme Court justice, who had raised $516,52010 by early October.

The largest late flow of money on the Democratic side went to a ballot measure, not a candidate. Idahoans United for Women and Families, the committee behind Proposition 1, the abortion-rights initiative, gave the Idaho Democratic Party $620,500 earlier this month11, Boise State Public Radio reported in late October.

The ads

Risch attacked Achilles' party history. "Don't let two-faced Todd Achilles fool Idaho voters," warned a mailer from the Risch campaign, citing the fact that the independent candidate previously served as a Democrat12 in the Idaho House. Risch also outspent Achilles online. His campaign has spent more than $193,000 on social media ads through Meta1, compared with $18,700 on Meta ads1 for Achilles. Near the end, a former chief justice backing Achilles told the Idaho Statesman that Risch had "started running hot and heavy on television ads"13.

Achilles focused on Risch's age and his refusal to debate. His statewide billboard buy, placed through the agency 116 & West, included one that facetiously congratulates Risch for being the "oldest and richest" senator in Idaho's history14. One of those billboards stood on land owned by the Risch family and was taken down after a family member asked.

Little skipped the debates and relied on paid media. Idaho Education News described a current TV ad touting Idaho's first-in-the-nation ban on transgender athletes15. A KIVI-TV fact check of another Little ad on household costs noted that the governor did not cut the six percent sales tax on groceries16. Pickens is running traditional TV ads16, along with social-media videos on public lands and a proposed data center.

The most disputed ad of the campaign ran under Pickens' name. The Democratic Party bought $371,246 worth of TV ads at a discounted rate through the Democratic nominee for governor, Terri Pickens11. Those ads were about Prop 1 and said they were paid for by the Idaho Democrats and "authorized" by Pickens, but did not mention Idahoans United17. Broadcasters must give candidates their lowest rates; issue groups don't get that discount. Opponents of Prop 1 sued, saying the arrangement was meant to get the cheaper rate. The secretary of state's office said Idahoans United, the state Democratic Party and Terri Pickens violated no campaign finance reporting laws11, and a district judge denies request in Prop 1 campaign finance case18.

Did it work?

In both top races, the side that spent more won. According to AP results, Risch beat Achilles 401,788 to 238,352, a margin of 23 points. Little beat Pickens 407,263 to 230,770, a margin of 24.7 points. Stegner got 7.6%19, according to the Idaho Press.

The margins were also much smaller than Donald Trump's 36.5-point win in Idaho in 2024: 13.5 points smaller in the Senate race and 11.8 points smaller for governor. These figures alone can't say how much of that change came from the ads and how much from the national mood. Asked whether the national results were a rebuke of the president, Little said "it's got the air of that."20

The Democratic money that went through Pickens' campaign did not win either. Prop 1 failed: it lost by about 56% of votes21.
