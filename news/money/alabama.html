Crypto Money Carried Barry Moore Through Alabama's Senate Primary

A crypto industry super PAC spent $12.2 million for Barry Moore before the June runoff, and no outside group spent for his Democratic opponent. In the governor's race, Tommy Tuberville outraised Doug Jones by about $11 million.

Outside groups reported spending $22.6 million on Alabama's 2026 Senate race to the Federal Election Commission, or $13.46 for every vote cast in November. Almost none of it went to the general election. Every committee on the FEC list spent for or against one of three Republicans: Rep. Barry Moore (listed under his legal name, Felix Barry Moore), Jared Hudson, a former Navy SEAL, and Attorney General Steve Marshall. None spent for or against the Democratic nominee, Everett Wess. The tally puts $3.1 million on the "Democratic side," but that is money spent against Hudson.

Moore's own campaign raised $4.6 million. Outside groups spent $16.5 million to support him. On Nov. 3 he beat Wess 917,338 to 759,321, according to AP results, a margin of 9.4 points.

Where the money came from

The biggest spender was Defend American Jobs, which put $12.2 million behind Moore. It is one of three affiliated super PACs in the Fairshake network; it spends in Republican primaries, and Protect Progress spends in Democratic ones.1 Mother Jones reported that Defend American Jobs is almost entirely funded by another crypto super PAC, called Fairshake2, and that almost all of Fairshake's funding comes from just three sources: the crypto companies Ripple and Coinbase and the Silicon Valley venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz2. After the runoff, a Fairshake spokesperson said the PAC had made its "biggest spend of the cycle"3 on Moore. Fellowship PAC, which Mother Jones called another pro-Moore crypto group2, added $350,000.

The second-largest spender was the Alabama Freedom Fund, a super PAC bankrolled by Club for Growth Action, a conservative advocacy group that endorsed Moore4. It spent $3.1 million for Moore and $2.4 million against Hudson. The Senate Conservatives Fund put in $386,000 and American Mission $330,000.

The other candidates got far less help. Alabama Conservatives, a super PAC, spent $1.9 million for Hudson. Hudson's campaign said the group raised $428,000 in the first quarter of the year5. American Values First spent $646,000 for Marshall and the same amount against Hudson, and Alabama Strong PAC spent $391,000 for Marshall. The FEC data block lists no donors for these committees, and the reporting reviewed for this story did not name them.

The governor's race is a state race, so its money is reported to the state, not the FEC. In filings analyzed by WBRC, Tuberville had raised about $18.2 million since May 20256 and Jones about $7.1 million. Tuberville relied on large checks: donors who gave $5,000 or more supplied 87% of the money, and nearly 40 donors gave $100,000 or more each.7 Jones relied on small ones. WBRC found that of Jones' 97,919 individual contributions, 91,857 were $100 or less.8

What the ads said

The crypto money never asked voters about crypto. Defend American Jobs' first buy, $5 million over five weeks, featured a new spot highlighting President Trump's endorsement of Moore, running on broadcast television, Fox News, and streaming platforms across the state.9 The Alabama Freedom Fund also ran on the endorsement. Its $1.25 million buy across TV, streaming, and radio in Birmingham and Huntsville4 included "Mother," an ad that called Marshall a former "soft-on-crime" Democrat. American Values First ran an ad in the same two markets attacking Hudson for his stance on the filibuster and the SAVE America Act10.

The runoff turned on Moore's military record. Alabama Conservatives paid for "Stolen Valor," and the group said the buy totals $500,000 and it will air in the Huntsville and Birmingham markets.11 The ad quoted a 2020 Moore campaign spot in which he said he had been "in those combat boots." Moore served in the Alabama Army National Guard from 1988 to 1991 and in the Army's Individual Ready Reserve from 1991 to 1997. He was never deployed to combat.12 His campaign said he did wear combat boots to train.11 AL.com reported that the station WVTM pulled the ad13. The Alabama Freedom Fund answered with "Knows It," an ad bought to match the placement of the Alabama Conservatives ad14.

On Google, the biggest political advertisers reaching Alabama were not Alabama campaigns. The prediction market Kalshi spent $1.8 million, the group ONE NATION $598,000, MAGA Inc. $242,000 and Georgia Sen. Jon Ossoff's campaign $232,000.

In the governor's race, the money went mostly to two ad firms. WBRC found that Tuberville sent $8.6 million to one Washington, D.C., company, Go Big Media7, and that Jones' campaign has paid Lever Communications $3.23 million.8 Jones's first ad said that "Data center developers are getting big tax breaks from Montgomery, and Tommy Tuberville is taking their money,"15 and pointed to a $25,000 contribution from Applied Digital Corporation. Tuberville's ads promised to send in the National Guard against crime and went after Jones over the CHOOSE Act, the state's school-choice program. His closing spot, "Difference," ran on digital, broadcast and television across the state16.

Did it work?

In the Senate race, the side with more outside money won every round. In the May 19 primary, Moore led Hudson 39%-26%17. He then won the runoff with 55.8% of the vote to Hudson's 44.2%3. The runoff did not look settled beforehand: the final two surveys found Moore ahead by 9 points (co/efficient) or Hudson ahead by 5 points (Strategy Management).17 The money was not the only advantage Moore had, either. Trump's endorsement was what most of the pro-Moore ads were built around.

In November there was no outside money in the Senate race, and Moore won by far less than the state's recent record. His 9.4-point margin compares with Trump's 30.6 points in 2024. Alabama Daily News noted that Katie Britt beat her Democratic opponent by nearly 36 points in 202218.

The governor's race followed the same pattern. Tuberville raised about $11 million more than Jones and won, 873,332 to 806,270, by 4.0 points. That is a 26.6-point move toward the Democrats from the 2024 presidential result. In both races, the side that spent more won.
