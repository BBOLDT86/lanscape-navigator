Alabama: Tuberville spent twice what Jones did and won by four points

Tommy Tuberville raised about $18 million under Alabama's no-limit rules, most of it in large checks. Doug Jones raised about $7 million, mostly in small gifts. The bigger budget won, but by the closest margin since 2002.

Alabama's most expensive general-election race this fall was not a federal one. Tommy Tuberville, the state's senior U.S. senator, ran for governor against former Sen. Doug Jones. Because it was a state race, the paper trail runs through the Alabama secretary of state's filings, not the Federal Election Commission. Tuberville won 873,332 votes to 806,270, according to the Associated Press results published by The New York Times. That is a four-point margin in a state Donald Trump carried by 30.6 points in 2024.

The federal race was quieter. The FEC data available to The Watch shows no outside spending in the general election for the U.S. Senate, where Rep. Barry Moore, a Republican, beat Everett Wess, a Democrat, by 9.4 points. The big money in that race came earlier. Defend American Jobs, a Republican affiliate of crypto super PAC Fairshake,1 announced a $5 million primary buy, and reported spending $7.4 million on media to support Republican Barry Moore ahead of his May 19 Alabama primary, and an additional $4.7 million before2 the June runoff. In the general election the two candidates were badly mismatched on money: through the end of June, Wess raised slightly less than $4,000 in campaign funds compared to Moore, who has received almost $2.5 million3, the Alabama Reflector reported from FEC records.

Where the money came from

By the WBRC-TV analysis of state filings through Nov. 2, Tuberville raised about $18.2 million since May 20254, and Jones raised about $7.1 million after entering the race on Nov. 24, 2025. Alabama has no limit on how much an individual, company or political action committee can give to a state candidate.5

The two campaigns were paid for very differently. Donors who gave $5,000 or more to Tuberville's campaign account for 87% of his money, and about 40 of them gave $100,000 or more each.6 For Jones, about 40% came in gifts of $100 or less. No Jones donor has given $100,000 or more.6

Several of Tuberville's biggest donors do business with the state. NaphCare, a Birmingham-based prison health care company, gave Tuberville's campaign $100,000 on May 27, 2025, the day he announced his candidacy5, and its founder, Jim McLane, sits on the campaign's finance committee. Kiewit, Massman and Traylor Bros., partners in a joint venture ALDOT paid $131.1 million in fiscal 2026, gave a combined $100,000.5 The law firm Beasley Allen contributed $125,0007. Its founder, Jere Beasley, a former Democratic lieutenant governor, put the gift down to a 20-year friendship with Tuberville. Tuberville also took $25,000 from Applied Digital Corporation, a Dallas-based data center development company, and a $100,000 donation from VGW Luckyland, a sweepstakes company8. Business groups came in late: four PACs gave $350,000 in cash6 in the final reports, and ProgressPAC, the Business Council of Alabama's political arm, also paid $229,883 for a mailer and advertising supporting Tuberville6. His campaign chair, Jordan Doufexis, said the campaign had "the financial support of more than 17,000 people."9

Jones's money was local. Jefferson County donations exceed $1.9 million, or 49.1% of all itemized Alabama giving.10

The ads

The two candidates did minimal TV advertising during the primary.11 Jones opened the fall campaign with an ad on data centers. In it he says "Data center developers are getting big tax breaks from Montgomery, and Tommy Tuberville is taking their money"11, and it ran on broadcast and cable.11 A later Jones spot says Tuberville is "wrong" on data centers and highlights his past remarks about gas prices.12

Tuberville's first ad promised to get rid of the state sales tax on groceries. Next he went after Jones for opposing the CHOOSE Act, Alabama's education savings account program.13 His crime ad drew the most attention. Calling Montgomery a "war zone" and Mobile and Birmingham "dangerous,"14 he promised to send in the National Guard on his first day. Yellowhammer News reported the campaign was sending his three latest ads to television, cable and digital screens across the state.15 Mobile's mayor called the plan unnecessary. In an Oct. 2 op-ed, Tuberville omitted the Azalea City. The column also appeared to pull back on a vow to send in armed servicemen "on day one."16 His closing ad, "Difference," contrast[ed] himself with Democrat Doug Jones on taxes, education and public safety. However, Tuberville never mentions him by name.17

Each campaign ran its ads through one main firm. Of Tuberville's roughly $15.1 million in spending, about 57% of that has gone to Go Big Media LLC of Washington, D.C.4 Jones's campaign has paid Lever Communications $3.23 million10, a Maryland firm. The spending came late: Tuberville's campaign spent about $362,000 a month from January through August 2026, then $4.1 million in September and $5.9 million in October.6

The only outside effort that turned up in the press was a text message. Mobile's Lagniappe reported that texts promoting a nonexistent Doug Jones Pride rally appear to have been commissioned by a PAC with ties to Tommy Tuberville.18 The message did not disclose which organization was linked to the communication.18

Did the money buy the seat?

The side that spent more won. Tuberville outspent Jones by roughly $15.1 million to $6.6 million. But the money did not deliver a blowout. In their 2020 Senate race, Tuberville earned 60.1% of the vote to Jones' 39.7%19, even though Jones, then the incumbent, outraised and outspent Tuberville sometimes by a factor of more than 3-to-1.20 This year the spending gap was reversed and the gap in votes shrank sharply. The race was the closest race for governor since 200219, and Jones's total is the highest vote total of any Democratic gubernatorial nominee in Alabama history.19 Alabama Daily News found that Jones increased the Democratic vote share in every county by at least 10 percentage points21 compared with 2022.

The Senate race is a useful comparison. Wess had almost no money, and his race moved 21.2 points toward Democrats from the 2024 presidential margin. The governor's race, where Jones spent millions, moved 26.6 points. Republicans held both seats. How much of the narrower margins came from the money, and how much from the national mood, is for the reader to judge.
