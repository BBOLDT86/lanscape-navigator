The money · race by race · Ohio

Ohio Republicans outspent Democrats on ads and lost both top races

Crypto PACs, a billionaire candidate's loans and a $10 million check from a Pennsylvania investor helped Republicans outspend Democrats in Ohio's Senate and governor's races. Sherrod Brown and Amy Acton won anyway, by wide margins.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 7, 2026. 18 sources. All the states › · How Ohio voted ›

By one tracker's count, Ohio had the most expensive Senate race in the country this year, and its governor's race set state fundraising records. In both races, Republicans and the groups backing them spent more on advertising. In both, the Democrat won.

The Watch had not finished reading the Federal Election Commission's Ohio data when this was published, so the federal dollar figures below come from published reporting on those filings. The governor's race is a state contest, and the candidates report to the Ohio secretary of state.

Where the money came from

Sherrod Brown, the former three-term Democratic senator, ran in the special election against Jon Husted, the Republican who was appointed to JD Vance's seat. Brown had far more campaign money: Brown's campaign raised more than $68 million, compared to Husted's $20 million.1 Outside groups narrowed that gap. A recent report from the advertising tracking firm AdImpact has Ohio as the most expensive Senate race in the country, with outside groups spending more than $325 million.2 By Oct. 20, the Ohio Capital Journal counted $113.5 million attacking Brown2 and $69.5 million attacking Husted. Democrats' primary Senate Super PAC WinSenate and an associated group called Duty and Country PAC accounted for $44.2 million of that total.2 Counting all ad spending, The New York Times last week reported that Democrats paid for about $208 million in ad spending on the race versus Republicans' $288 million.1

A large share of the Republican-side money came from the cryptocurrency industry. In September, the crypto super PAC Fairshake announced a $30 million campaign against Brown. Signal Ohio reported that Ripple Labs has given $48 million to the PAC, and Coinbase, a cryptocurrency exchange, gave it $25 million, according to Federal Election Commission records.3 Fairshake's ads ran under the name of Defend American Jobs, one of its affiliated super PACs. Crypto trade outlets reported that the group's Ohio media spending reached $46.6 million4 by mid-October. Digital Freedom Fund, a super PAC established by Gemini co-founders Tyler and Cameron Winklevoss4, spent more than $5.9 million. The AI industry also bought airtime: Build American AI, a nonprofit linked to the AI industry's Leading the Future super PAC network, spent at least $2.54 million on Ohio ads touting data centers' economic benefits, according to an investigation by DeSmog.5 Build American AI is a 501(c)(4), a type of nonprofit that does not have to disclose its donors. Axios has called it a "dark money" offshoot of the super PAC.

In the governor's race, the Republican nominee, Vivek Ramaswamy, paid for much of his own campaign. His final pre-election report, covering more than four months, showed his campaign spent $51.1 million. Ramaswamy also reported making another $5 million loan to his campaign. That brings its total outstanding debt to $41.3 million.6 Acton raised more in that period, $3.9 million — more than double Ramaswamy's take — while spending $26.2 million.6 V-PAC, the super PAC supporting Ramaswamy, raised more than $18 million in its most recent quarterly report. A single donor, Pennsylvania billionaire Jeff Yass wrote Ramaswamy a check for $10 million7. AdImpact reported earlier this month that Republican aligned groups have spent or reserved more than $100 million in ads in the governor's race, compared with about $40 million from Democratic groups.2

The ads

The Senate Leadership Fund, the main super PAC for Senate Republicans, ran ads featuring Soren Aldaco. In one, "Sherrod Brown supports sex changes for minors, irreversible surgery on kids like me," Aldaco says.8 The Ohio Capital Journal found that she isn't from Ohio and didn't have surgery until she was an adult.8 CNN counted at least three deceptive ads attacking Brown on gender issues9. Brown answered with an ad noting that the surgeries are already illegal for minors in Ohio. The crypto money took a different approach. Signal Ohio reported that in the first two Defend American Jobs ads, neither ad mentions cryptocurrency. One is a positive ad about Republican Sen. Jon Husted's biography.3

On the Democratic side, WinSenate tied Husted to the FirstEnergy bribery scandal in ads that would air on television stations throughout the state as well as on digital streaming platforms.10 FactCheck.org found that the individual statements in the ads are mostly accurate; it's the juxtaposition of them that wrongly suggests Husted has been accused of wrongdoing in the scandal.11 Brown's own ads focused on data centers. In one he named Wood County and said "Jon Husted gave away $2.5 billion to these out of state corporations and billionaires."12 Build American AI's ads made the opposite case, saying data centers have brought more than $40 billion in investments and created over 95,000 jobs in Ohio.13

The governor's race started with a lopsided air war. Between May 15 and the end of June, the Republican's campaign and V-PAC: Victors, Not Victims, a super PAC dedicated to electing him, have spent $9 million on ads14, while Acton spent $100,000. In October, Ramaswamy's campaign put out a one-minute ad in which Acton's estranged mother, Donna Arthur, accused her of lying about her childhood. Axios reported a $4 million statewide campaign that began streaming Tuesday night.15 After criticism from both parties, including from Gov. Mike DeWine, the campaign stopped running the ad after reportedly spending only a quarter of the $4 million it originally pledged16.

Did it work?

Brown won 2,301,042 votes to Husted's 2,077,748, a margin of 4.9 points. Acton beat Ramaswamy 2,404,178 to 2,039,730, a margin of 7.9 points. Donald Trump carried Ohio by 11.2 points in 2024. In both races, the side with more ad money lost.

Spending was not the only factor, and the evidence about what decided the races is mixed. In the governor's race, Republicans' own polling, according to Axios, indicated that the ad was indeed a game-changer — just not the type Ramaswamy's campaign hoped it would be.17 Other analysts note that Acton was already ahead before the ad ran. In the Senate race, the National Republican Senatorial Committee even acknowledged that Husted's ties to the industry were a problem, writing in an August memo that "more than any other thing in this race, data centers are the anchor hanging around Husted's neck."18 Republicans lost seats in other states the same night. Two years earlier, the cryptocurrency industry spent $40 million on ads attacking him late in his losing campaign1. This year the industry spent more against him, and he won.
