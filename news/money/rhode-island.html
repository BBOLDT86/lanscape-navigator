The money · race by race · Rhode Island

Foulkes outspent a sitting governor, then an independent, and won both races

Helena Foulkes spent about $6.8 million and got help from her party, unions and a clean-energy super PAC. That was more than all of her opponents combined. She beat Gov. Dan McKee by 24 points and independent Ken Block by 30.

The federal money that poured into other states mostly passed Rhode Island by. The Federal Election Commission data The Watch uses had not yet been read in for this state, and it showed no committee spending independently for or against any federal candidate. Sen. Jack Reed, a Democrat, beat Republican Raymond McKay 240,069 to 105,346. That is a 37.5-point margin in a state that voted Democratic by 13.9 points for president in 2024.

The spending happened in the race for governor, which is a state race. Its money is reported to the R.I. Board of Elections, not the FEC.

Where the money came from

Helena Buonanno Foulkes reported about $6.8 million in spending through Oct. 26 since the start of last year1, according to a WPRI analysis of state filings. That was about 69% of all the money spent by candidates for governor this cycle.1 Foulkes is a former president of CVS Pharmacy. Counting only what candidates spent from their own accounts, the total of about $9.9 million was the least amount in nearly two decades.2 McKee was second at nearly $2.6 million through early October.2 Independent Ken Block spent about $368,000, and Republican nominee Aaron Guckian about $139,000.2 The figures are preliminary, and final reports are due in December.1

Before the September primary, outside money mostly helped McKee. A Section 527 political group, Working Rhode Island Forward, was expected to be funded by McKee-supporting labor unions and their allies3. WPRI reported that the Democratic Governors Association and the Laborers union directed a combined $225,0004 to it for digital ads and mailers. On the other side, UNITE HERE spent $100,000 in July to support Foulkes and Providence mayoral challenger David Morales.5

The largest single outside effort came against McKee. Invest in America's Future PAC, a state-level committee, received $550,000 from a federal super PAC called the Invest in Tomorrow Coalition PAC4. That super PAC is funded by solar developers and Silicon Valley entrepreneurs5, and its largest donor is tech billionaire Chris Larsen, with a $6 million contribution6. Rhode Island Current reported that none of the donors are based in Rhode Island.6 The group said it objected to McKee's budget plan, which would have delayed the state's transition to 100% renewable energy by nearly two decades and slashed energy-efficiency spending by more than 20%.7

After the primary, the money that had backed McKee moved to Foulkes. The Democratic Governors Association deposited $500,000 with an affiliated group in Rhode Island to support her campaign8. In mid-October, no money had been spent by that group9. An arm of the Laborers' International Union of North America planned to spend $500,000 to support Foulkes10, according to WPRI. The state Democratic Party also paid for mail and digital ads for her (details below).

The ads

Foulkes's first television ad, "Care," started running May 30. In it she addresses the audience directly, reframing her healthcare past favorably11, and promises bridge inspectors to prevent another Washington Bridge "fiasco." Her campaign spent just over $95,000 to air it 359 times11 on Providence stations through June 8, according to FCC filings. McKee's opening ad claimed Foulkes jacked up insulin prices and wanted to cut Medicaid.12 By late August, Foulkes had outspent McKee more than three-to-one on TV, $2.2 million to $670,000, according to AdImpact13, as reported by WJAR.

In the last week before the primary, Invest in America's Future announced a broadcast and digital buy for two 30-second attack ads, one focused on the ILO contracting scandal and another on the Washington Bridge.4 One ends with the line "Dan McKee works for insiders, not for you"5. In the same week, McKee's digital-only spot featured a father whose son died of a drug overdose, renewing his criticism of Foulkes over the opioid crisis.4

In the general election, Foulkes's main opponent was Block, not the Republican. The state Democratic Party paid for a series of mailers linking Block to Republican viewpoints14. One reads: "If Ken Block walks like a duck and quacks like a duck, he must be a Republican…duck."15 Block filed complaints over whether the $228,000 worth of mailers is considered a print advertisement and subject to a $25,000 spending cap16, and over $90,000 the state Democratic Party spent on digital ads for Foulkes.16 The Foulkes campaign pointed to a Board of Elections advisory opinion four years earlier saying mailers don't count toward the aggregate limit.17 Block spent over $127,000 on TV ads during the general election18. In mid-October, Foulkes booked about $150,000 in TV time for one week, while Block's most recent orders totaled about $25,000.10 Guckian never appeared on broadcast networks, though he aired commercials on NESN during the Red Sox home opener18.

Did it work?

In both races, the side that spent more won. In the primary, Foulkes won 62% to 38%19 and won all 39 cities and towns.20 The money's effect is hard to separate from other causes, though. When the super PAC went on the air, McKee already trailed Foulkes by 15 points4 in a WPRI/Emerson poll. NHPR attributed the result in large part to voter dissatisfaction over McKee's response to the Washington Bridge crisis19.

On Nov. 3, Foulkes received 57.9%, or 207,216 votes; Block 27.9%, or 99,785; Guckian 13.2%, or 47,36121. The Watch's data compares Foulkes only with Guckian, a 44.5-point margin. Because an independent came second, that number overstates any statewide swing; her margin over Block was 30 points. A WPRI poll in mid-October had shown the gap at 9 points, down from 27 in August.21 In his concession, Block compared his own campaign spending, about $400,000, to the millions the Foulkes campaign spent.22 Foulkes said she needed the money because it was hard to challenge an incumbent governor.23
