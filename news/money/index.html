The $67 million behind six Senate races, and what it bought

A crypto super PAC was the biggest outside spender in The Watch's federal data, and most of its money went into an Alabama primary. The filings so far show only part of what the national players spent.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026. FEC data read Nov. 11, 2026; the sums refresh daily. 30 sources, numbered in the text.

$97.5Moutside money reported in the Senate and House races (independent expenditures, for and against)
5 of 5decided Senate races went to the side with more outside money
–paid straight to Meta and Google: the platform payments (Schedule B) are not in the filings read so far
$1.4Bpolitical ads on Google’s platforms since Jan. 2025, by Google’s own count

The Federal Election Commission figures The Watch pulled on Nov. 7 show $67.0 million in independent expenditures in six U.S. Senate races. Independent expenditures are ads and other spending by groups that may not coordinate with candidates. No House race appears in the pull, and no committee in it itemized a direct payment to Meta or Google. So this is a partial map. For scale, ad spending hit a record $11.87 billion, according to AdImpact1. Google's own transparency report shows $1.4 billion in political ads on its platforms since January 2025.

The map still says something. The biggest single spender was a crypto-industry super PAC, and most of its money went into a Republican primary.

Where the money came from

Defend American Jobs spent $12.2 million, all of it in Alabama. That is nearly a fifth of the total. It is a Republican affiliate of crypto super PAC Fairshake2. In the second half of 2025, Fairshake's three biggest donors gave $74 million: Ripple and Coinbase each donating $25 million and Andreessen Horowitz contributing $24 million3. An FEC analysis by FinTech Weekly counted Coinbase as the single largest contributor to Fairshake in the current cycle with a combined total of $52,469,213.874.

On the Democratic side, much of the money traces back to Senate Majority PAC, the super PAC aligned with Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer. The Georgia Way raised $8,500,095.005 between June and mid-October. Votelog, a site that compiles FEC filings, lists Senate Majority PAC as its donor for $8,500,0006. Lower Costs Now got nearly all of its money from two committees: SMP, which contributed $30 million, and FF PAC, which gave $20 million7.

On the Republican side, the Alabama Freedom Fund is bankrolled by Club for Growth Action8. In Arkansas, the Democrat-Gazette reported that America One received $450,000 from America One Policies, a 501(c)(4) nonprofit, and a combined $250,000 from members of the Stephens family9. A 501(c)(4) is a nonprofit that does not have to name its donors. The biggest Republican Senate super PAC, the Senate Leadership Fund, has a similar arrangement: it is closely affiliated with One Nation, a 501(c)(4) nonprofit organization that is not legally required to publicly disclose its donors10. A newer industry also gave. Reuters reported that prediction-market operators Kalshi and Polymarket gave at least $5 million combined to Republican and Democratic political groups11.

Where it went

Alabama drew the most outside money: $22.5 million, with $19.4 million on the Republican side. One thing about how The Watch's data counts money matters here. Spending against a Republican goes in the Democratic column, even when another Republican paid for it. So almost all of Alabama's $3.1 million "for Democrats" is Republicans attacking Republicans. Roll Call reported that the Alabama Freedom Fund spent $2.5 million to boost his campaign and to oppose Marshall. Meanwhile, American Values First has been boosting Marshall and opposing Hudson12. Votelog counts only $387,557 of independent expenditures disseminated after the June 16, 2026 primary runoffs13.

Georgia drew $20.9 million, $19.3 million of it on the Democratic side. That includes $4.2 million from Hardworking Americans Inc., Kemp's super PAC14, the committee of Republican Gov. Brian Kemp, which was active in the Republican primary. Our data shows just $1.6 million helping Republicans in Georgia. Other counts are higher: Votelog lists the Senate Leadership Fund at $11,511,399, all opposing Ossoff6. The super PAC had committed to spending $44 million in Georgia15. AdImpact later said it had recently canceled $7.6 million in ad reservations in Georgia16.

In Alaska, our data shows Democrats ahead, $12.5 million to $5.4 million. Local reporting found the opposite. The Anchorage Daily News reported that Sen. Dan Sullivan's allies had spent roughly $20 million on ads attacking Peltola and supporting the incumbent since mid-July17, against about $15.4 million from Mary Peltola's backers. According to AdImpact, Republicans have reserved $45 million in Alaska advertising from Aug. 1 through Election Day, compared with Democrats' $32 million18.

In Arkansas, America One directed $4.5 million on media opposing Shoffner19, the Democrat Hallie Shoffner. The Colorado and Florida races drew less than $1 million each.

Where the ads ran

The data block shows zero direct payments to Meta or Google. That does not mean no money reached them. Committees usually pay media-buying firms, which then buy the airtime and the digital ads, so direct platform payments are only a floor. For example, the Senate Leadership Fund's filings show media placement paid to Cavalry LLC, and media production paid to SLMG LLC20. AB PAC spent on billboard advertising paid to AdQuick, Lamar Companies and Target Platform20.

Google's own report fills in some of the gap. The SLF PAC spent $34.6 million on Google's platforms, mostly in Ohio and Michigan. WinSenate spent $33.5 million, Jon Ossoff's campaign $23.0 million, and two accounts registered as One Nation a combined $26.3 million. The biggest political advertiser on Google was Kalshi, at $84.2 million. Spending peaked in the week of Oct. 25, at $171.8 million.

Television still got the most money. Defend American Jobs said its Alabama ad will air on TV, streaming and Fox News Channel2. The Alabama Freedom Fund's buy covered TV, streaming and radio in the Birmingham and Huntsville media markets8. In the third quarter, broadcast television accounted for 51% of the $3 billion in political advertising that aired21.

What the ads said

The crypto PAC's ads were mostly about President Donald Trump: the first one highlighting President Donald Trump's endorsement of Moore22. The group also promised he would help make America the crypto capital2. The Club for Growth's ad called Alabama Attorney General Steve Marshall someone who had spent most of his life as a "soft-on-crime Democrat."8 Marshall changed his party affiliation from Democrat to Republican in 20118.

In Alaska, a North Star ad criticizes Sullivan for accepting hundreds of thousands of dollars from commercial trawling companies, linking the industry to declines in salmon runs23. On the other side, Last Frontier PAC turned a New York Times report on Peltola's treatment of her staff into a grainy black-and-white attack ad24. FactCheck.org found that ads from Sullivan and the PAC misleadingly claim that Peltola opposed military pay raises25. In the final week, 59 PAC, an SLF affiliate group in Alaska, and Last Frontier PAC26 paid for ads tying Peltola to a second Dan Sullivan on the ballot. Peltola's campaign has continued to deny involvement26.

In Arkansas, America One's ads went after Shoffner over her comments on migrant workers and opposition toward the Trump administration's controversial immigration enforcement agenda19.

Whether it worked

In our data, the side with more outside money won all five decided races. Look closer and the record is mixed.

• Alabama: Most of the money was spent before the nomination. The crypto PAC's candidate, Barry Moore, won the runoff over Jared Hudson, who lost the Republican primary runoff to Moore27. He then beat Democrat Everett Wess by 9.4 points in the general election, which drew little outside money.
• Georgia: Ossoff won by 18 points. But Republicans' main group pulled back, and his campaign had its own money: $2.1 million in cash on hand as of June 3015 for Collins, against $42.6 million for Ossoff.
• Arkansas: Tom Cotton won by 11.9 points. That is not close to Trump's electoral performance in Arkansas two years ago, when he carried the state by nearly 31 points19.
• Alaska: The race has not been called. Our results show Sullivan up 2.0 points. Alaska Public Media reported that by Wednesday Peltola led Sullivan by 637 votes28, and The Washington Post reported she held a narrow lead29 going into the weekend. If Sullivan holds on, the side with less money in our data wins. By the press's counts, it would be the side that spent more.

Nationally, AdImpact found Republicans spending approximately $5.18 billion this election cycle, compared with $4.77 billion for Democrats30.

Who spent it

The committees — Super PACs, party committees and hybrids — behind the outside money, by what they spent across the races, and their record: a race counts as won when the side they spent for won it.

The buyers

The biggest contributors to those committees, by what they gave them this cycle, with the seats their money reached and how those races came out. Giving to a Super PAC is not buying a seat — the committee decides where it goes — but it is the closest the public record comes to a list of who paid for the ads. Donor lists fill in once the FEC key is set.

Where the ads went

Two measures. First, what committees reported paying Meta and Google directly, by month: a floor, because most digital buys go through media vendors whose names, not the platforms’, appear in the filings. Second, Google’s own transparency count of political advertising on its platforms (Search, YouTube and the display network), which covers every advertiser Google verified, whatever route the money took — and sweeps in some advertisers that are political only in Google’s sense of the word, such as the prediction markets that bought election-odds ads. Meta publishes its figures only in its Ad Library, which has no public file.

How we made it

  • Outside money is independent expenditures (Schedule E) aggregated by the FEC by candidate and committee for the 2025–26 cycle, including 24- and 48-hour reports; a committee’s spending “for a side” is its support of that side’s candidate plus its opposition to the other. Candidate totals are from Form 3 summaries. Winners and margins are AP’s count via The New York Times, matched by state, office, district and surname.
  • Donors are itemized receipts (Schedule A) of $10,000 and more to the committees that spent the most, summed by contributor name; a donor’s “seats” are every race the committees it funded spent on, won when that committee’s side won. Pass-through entities (ActBlue, WinRed) appear as contributors where the committee reported them that way.
  • Platform payments are itemized disbursements (Schedule B) naming Meta Platforms, Facebook or Google as the payee, $2,500 and up. They are a floor: digital buys through vendors are reported under the vendor’s name.
  • Google’s figures come from its political-ads transparency bundle: weekly spend by verified advertiser since Jan. 2025, geographic spend by state (cumulative), and per-ad spend and impression ranges with targeting. Meta has no equivalent file.
  • The written pieces are by Claude Opus 5.5 through Anthropic’s API: the data above goes in the prompt, every dollar figure must come from it or be attributed to a named filing or outlet, and every web claim carries the search’s own citation. Spending on the API is logged in spend.json (and the wave’s in its own).
