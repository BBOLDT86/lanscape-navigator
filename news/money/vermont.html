The money · race by race · Vermont

National Republicans' $950,000 ad push backed Phil Scott's sixth Vermont win

Amanda Janoo outraised Gov. Phil Scott with small donors and no corporate money. A Republican Governors Association super PAC offset her edge with TV and online ads calling her inexperienced, and Scott won by 6.6 points.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 13, 2026. 16 sources. All the states › · How Vermont voted ›

Gov. Phil Scott is a Republican in a state Kamala Harris carried by 31.8 points in 2024. On Nov. 3 he won a record sixth term, beating Amanda Janoo, the Democratic and Progressive nominee, 162,025 votes to 141,618 in the Associated Press count reported by The New York Times. That is a 6.6-point margin. Two years ago, the ideologically diverse coalition that handed Scott 71% of the vote1 looked unbeatable. This time he was outraised by a first-time candidate, and a national party group made up the difference.

No federal race in Vermont had any reported outside spending in Federal Election Commission data. So the money story is the governor's race, which is filed with the Vermont Secretary of State's office.

Where the money came from

Janoo, an economist, raised just over $1 million, which is more than any Scott challenger since the governor's first bid for the office in 20162, VTDigger reported. Seven Days reported that she refused to accept money from corporations or corporate PACs3 and drew from about 8,600 individual donors. Her filings show $5,180 from the Vermont-NEA and $4,000 from VPIRG4. Ben Cohen and Jerry Greenfield, the founders of Ben & Jerry's ice cream4, also gave.

Scott raised just shy of $720,000 by Election Day2. In August he told reporters "I'll take money from anyone," including corporations5. By mid-October, donations from businesses and organizations — including an assortment of real estate, construction and automotive companies6 made up $110,000 of his total. The Vermont Association of Realtors contributed the $5,180 maximum5, and Meta, Facebook's parent company, gave $1,000.

The largest single source of money was the Republican Governors Association. It paid for A Stronger Vermont, an independent-expenditure committee (Vermont's version of a super PAC). Under campaign finance law, the PAC can't donate directly to Scott's campaign but is able to spend an unlimited amount of money supporting him.7 On Oct. 8, its coffers were boosted by a $400,000 contribution from the Republican Governors Association7. VTDigger put the association's total at $950,000 as of last week, according to campaign finance reports8. The counts differ a little. Vermont Public reported more than $930,000 as of Monday1 (Oct. 26) in spending, and Seven Days put the figure above $1 million. The committee last spent this heavily in 2016, when it spent $3 million on behalf of Scott during his inaugural run9. In fundraising emails, Janoo's campaign pointed out that the association counts Elon Musk, Sheldon and Miriam Adelson, and David Koch among its top donors10.

On the Democratic side, the Democratic Governors Association gave $40,000 to its own Vermont-based super PAC, called Our Vermont9. Janoo's campaign said "she does not support outside interest groups getting involved in Vermont politics."11 By mid-October, Our Vermont reported spending $121,000 since the start of October on consulting and surveys but not on any advertising7.

The ads

A Stronger Vermont's first buy was $160,000 on TV and social media12. The ads attempt to portray Janoo as a far-left candidate whose proposals are too far afield even for her fellow Democrats.9 They feature a quote from Janoo's primary opponent, Aly Richards, who intimated in July that Janoo's platform represents "magical thinking."9 A website carrying one of the ads also includes a picture that appears to show Janoo holding a glass of wine next to the words, "what kind of governor would Amanda Janoo be?"13

Emma Shouldice, Scott's spokeswoman, said the ads make "fair points."9 Scott himself said, "I don't like the negative campaigning."7 Janoo's campaign called the RGA "a D.C.-based political group aligned with Trump."13

The candidates mostly ran positive ads of their own. In September, the Scott campaign paid around $137,000 to Hen House Media for TV ads.4 It also bought digital, TV and radio advertising7. Janoo's campaign shelled out $60,000 to Mosaic Communications on September 29 — her campaign's first foray into television ads.4 In early October she reported more than $200,000 in TV and online advertising. WCAX noted that Scott's ads struck a similar positive tone to ads crafted by Scott's challenger14. The sharpest message from the Scott side came on social media, where his campaign asked, "why is their answer to every problem always another tax, fee and more spending?"15 AdImpact counted a collective $21.1 million in Vermont this cycle14, WCAX reported. That figure covers all races, not just this one.

Did it work?

Add what each campaign raised to the RGA's money, and the pro-Scott side had roughly $1.67 million against Janoo's roughly $1 million. The side with more money won.

The local press credits the outside money mainly with the tone of the race, not its size. VTDigger reported that the RGA spending helped counter the fact Janoo raised more money than Scott over the last two months of the race.2 Middlebury College political scientist Matt Dickinson said its value "wasn't the money itself.2" It let someone other than Scott go negative and question how Janoo would pay for her plans and whether she was ready to govern. Scott kept his own campaign civil. Dickinson suggested he may have clinched the race with a closing debate line calling Janoo's platform "free this, free that."16

The money also had a cost. Before the vote, Vermont Public reported that the ad blitz has handed Democrats a potent attack line tying the never-Trump Republican to the historically unpopular president.1 Scott won, but by far less than two years earlier. Neither the filings nor the reporting can say how much of the remaining margin the ads bought.
