Alaska's outside money favored Sullivan; Peltola leads an uncalled count

Outside groups reported $37.7 million in independent spending in Alaska's Senate race, more of it helping Republican Dan Sullivan. Democrat Mary Peltola raised more than twice what he did. She leads the count, which has not been called.

Outside groups reported $37.7 million in independent expenditures in Alaska's U.S. Senate race to the Federal Election Commission. That is about $151.51 for every vote cast. Of it, $20.3 million helped Sen. Dan Sullivan, the Republican incumbent, and $17.4 million helped Mary Peltola, the Democratic former congresswoman. The candidates' own money tilted the other way. Peltola's campaign took in $36.9 million and Sullivan's $14.1 million. As of Nov. 12, the Associated Press had not called the race. Peltola led 121,487 votes to 117,512.

The FEC totals are probably low. By Oct. 1, the Anchorage Daily News counted more than $35 million on the U.S. Senate race since mid-July1. Advertising reservations were larger still: Republicans have reserved $45 million in Alaska advertising from Aug. 1 through Election Day, compared with Democrats' $32 million, according to AdImpact2, as reported by the Alaska Landmine.

Where the money came from

The largest single spender was SLF PAC, the Senate Leadership Fund, with $9.8 million against Peltola. It is the GOP's main Senate super PAC3, and in the spring it said it will also spend $15 million in Alaska4. Its best-known donor this cycle is Elon Musk, but the $158 million he gave in September appeared to flow directly to another group, Texas PAC5. The fund also takes money from One Nation, an affiliated nonprofit. The law does not require such groups to name their donors, which is what "dark money" means. That arrangement allows some of the original sources of the money ultimately spent by the super PAC to remain undisclosed.6

Last Frontier PAC spent $7.1 million against Peltola. It is the super PAC partner of Last Frontier Action, a 501(c)(4) nonprofit, and Brock Lowrance, a longtime GOP operative and former senior adviser at the National Republican Senatorial Committee, is leading7 both groups. The Alaska journalist Dermot Cole reported from its filings that ConocoPhillips gave $500,000 on December 15 last year, while the owners of Hilcorp—Jeff and Melinda Hildebrand—gave $100,0008.

The biggest Democratic spender was North Star, with $7.3 million against Sullivan. A super PAC by that name ran in the 2020 Senate race, and Alaska Public Media reported then that the firm it hired to buy its television commercials is the same one as Senate Majority PAC9. The reporting reviewed for this piece did not identify North Star's 2026 donors. Senate Majority PAC itself said in May it was reserving $10 million of Alaska TV time10. Women Vote, the next-largest Democratic spender, put $2.8 million against Sullivan, part of nationwide campaign supporting female Democratic candidates who back abortion access.1

The ads

One North Star ad criticizes Sullivan for accepting hundreds of thousands of dollars from commercial trawling companies, linking the industry to declines in salmon runs11. The group also paid Google $2.4 million for ads in Alaska. Its largest Google buy was a text ad that ran from Sept. 20 through Election Day.

Republican groups went after Peltola's character. In late September, Last Frontier PAC began airing an ad about her treatment of her staff on broadcast stations tomorrow as part of an approximately $600,000 buy12. The ad drew on a New York Times investigation. Peltola's campaign denied she engaged in abusive behavior12. In the final week, the Times reported that people working for Peltola spent months recruiting candidates they hoped could take votes away from the GOP Sullivan.13 One was Daniel J. Sullivan, a retired teacher. Republicans answered with a "high six-figure" ad campaign on TV and streaming platforms13, paid for by 59 PAC, an SLF affiliate group in Alaska, and Last Frontier PAC13. Peltola's campaign has continued to deny involvement13.

Both sides paid to promote longshot candidates. The Alaska Beacon found that Right for Alaska, a Democratic-aligned super PAC, spent $2.35 million before the August primary, much of it boosting fringe Republican and other conservative candidates14. Some of its money came from the Sixteen Thirty Fund, which does not have to disclose its donors14, and another $1.3 million14 came from Liberty Force Action Fund. Its operative, Thomas Datwyler, is also tied to Alaska Values PAC, whose ads tell voters "Rank one name, leave the rest blank: Only rank Heikes"15, meaning Gerald Heikes, a little-known conservative. On the Republican side, the super PAC Fair Deal Alaska, which boosted fringe candidates in the primary, got $650,000 from Senate Leadership Fund16 and another $100,00016 from Last Frontier PAC.

Did it work?

The side that spent more outside money is behind for now. On Nov. 11, the Beacon reported that with 245,470 first-choice ballots counted, Peltola now leads17 by 4,250 first-choice votes, or 48.8% to 47.1%.17 The Beacon's analysis of partial ranked-choice data shows Peltola winning that matchup as well.17 The state will not run this count until November 18.18

Early ballot data suggest the longshot candidates did not split the vote as their backers planned. FairVote found that 43% of voters ranking Republican Gerald Heikes first had their vote count for Dan S. Sullivan, compared to 17% for Mary Peltola.19 Among voters who ranked Daniel J. Sullivan, 34%19 had their votes go to Peltola and 23%19 to the senator.

Alaska's governor's race was run under state law, so its money does not appear in the FEC data. Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins leads Republican Bernadette Wilson by 20.8 points, and that race is also uncalled. According to state filings reported by the Daily News, he raised over $5.3 million20 and she raised $1.5 million20. His donors included six employees of Anthropic, a California-based artificial intelligence company, who collectively gave Kreiss-Tomkins $372,000.21 (Anthropic makes Claude, the AI that wrote this report.) Kodiak PAC, a group funded by the Republican Governors Association, has spent $1.7 million backing the GOP candidates22. A group backing Kreiss-Tomkins raised more than $1.5 million, in large part from the Democratic Governors Association.23
