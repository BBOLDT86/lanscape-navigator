Outside groups outspent for Sullivan, but Peltola took Alaska's Senate seat

Super PACs reported $49.8 million in independent spending on Alaska's Senate race, most of it for Republican Dan Sullivan. Mary Peltola, who raised more than twice what he did, won anyway; the governor's race was not close.

Outside groups reported $49.8 million in independent expenditures in Alaska's U.S. Senate race this cycle. That figure comes from the Federal Election Commission, read Nov. 18, and works out to about $173 for every vote cast. Groups helping Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan accounted for $29.3 million and groups helping Democrat Mary Peltola for $20.6 million. Peltola won, 144,302 votes to 131,844. The Associated Press called the race on Tuesday evening, two weeks after Election Day1, giving Democrats a 56-44 edge in the chamber next year.2

Other counts run much higher. Citing OpenSecrets, the Anchorage Daily News put independent spending at more than $100 million, including $43 million opposing Peltola and $40 million opposing Sullivan.3 The Watch uses the FEC figures and could not make the two tallies match.

Where the money came from

The biggest spender was the Senate Leadership Fund, the principal super PAC aligned with Republican Senate leadership4. It reported $9.8 million against Peltola. It is closely affiliated with One Nation, a 501(c)(4) nonprofit not legally required to disclose its donors4. Google's transparency report shows One Nation spent $1.3 million on Google and YouTube ads in Alaska.

Last Frontier PAC, a super PAC, reported $7.1 million against Peltola. It is part of a joint fundraising committee, Sullivan Victory, with the NRSC and Sullivan's campaign5. Alaska journalist Dermot Cole reported that ConocoPhillips gave $500,000 on December 15, while the owners of Hilcorp—Jeff and Melinda Hildebrand—gave $100,0006. No Going Back PAC Inc. reported $5.5 million against Peltola and $3.5 million for Sullivan. The New York Times linked it to MAGA Inc., Trump's powerful main super PAC7, and it shares the same address and treasurer, Charles Gantt8.

On the Democratic side, the super PAC North Star led with $7.3 million against Sullivan. Its filings show payments to MVAR Media, LLC and media buys through Waterfront Strategies9. The Watch found no reporting that names North Star's 2026 donors. In 2020, when a group called North Star went after Sullivan, the Anchorage Daily News noted that its TV buyer was the same one Senate Majority PAC used10. Women Vote reported $2.8 million against Sullivan and WinSenate $2.5 million. Majority Forward, the nonprofit arm of Senate Majority PAC11, spent $2.2 million on Google ads in Alaska.

Some money went to minor candidates. Right for Alaska, a super PAC, reported $1.1 million against Sullivan. Sixteen Thirty Fund, a 501(c)(4) that does not have to disclose its donors, gave it about $1 million; another $1.3 million came from "Liberty Force Action Fund."12 That is dark money in the legal sense: the people who originally gave it are never disclosed. Right for Alaska's operative, Thomas Datwyler, is also tied to Alaska Values, which disclosed more than $1 million to oppose Sullivan and boost Republican Gerald Heikes12. The FEC shows $700,000 supporting Heikes. Money also moved the other way: Fair Deal Alaska, which promoted Carol Hafner, received $100,000 from Last Frontier PAC13.

What the ads said

Senate Leadership Fund ads said Peltola "sold Alaska out"14. In another, veterans from Palmer and Eagle River fault her votes against a military pay raise and veterans' health care funding.15

Last Frontier PAC turned a New York Times report on how Peltola treated her staff into a grainy black-and-white attack ad16, part of an approximately $600,000 broadcast buy16. Her campaign denied abusive behavior and said she struggled with grief after losing her husband and mother in 2023.17 A later Times story tied Peltola's allies to recruiting a second candidate named Dan Sullivan. After it ran, 59 PAC, an SLF affiliate, and Last Frontier PAC paid for a "high six-figure" campaign on TV and streaming18. Peltola has denied knowing of any plan to put Dan J. Sullivan on the ballot.19

No Going Back ads claimed Peltola "voted against prohibiting taxpayer funded sex changes for minors."20 FactCheck.org found that the ads misrepresent her votes20. On the other side, a North Star ad criticizes Sullivan for taking money from commercial trawling companies, linking the industry to declining salmon runs14. A WinSenate ad accused him of taking a $500,000 payout. FactCheck.org found Sullivan didn't get any $500,000 "payout."21 YouTube ads bought by Alaska Values portray Heikes as the real conservative and urge voters not to rank Sullivan.22

The biggest political advertiser on Google in Alaska was Peltola's own campaign, Alaskans for Mary, at $3.2 million. North Star was second at $2.4 million.

Did it work?

The side with more outside money lost. The candidates' own money ran the other way, though. Peltola's campaign reported $36.9 million in receipts and Sullivan's $14.1 million. Adding candidate and outside money together, the Democratic side had about $57.5 million and the Republican side about $43.4 million.

Peltola won 50.1% of the votes to Sullivan's 45.8%1. Because that was a majority of first choices, ranked-choice transfers did not decide the race. Heikes took just over 2%, and the second Dan Sullivan 1.7%.23 On the staff-treatment ad, Axios noted that analysts quoted by the Anchorage Daily News said the story was having little effect on voters16. The result may still be challenged: the Justice Department sued to delay certification if votes for the second Dan Sullivan changed the outcome.23

The governor's race was decided by a wider margin. State filings show Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins raised over $5.3 million24, while Bernadette Wilson raised $1.5 million24. Forward Frontier, funded in part by the Democratic Governors Association, spent $4.4 million for him.24 Kodiak PAC, funded by the Republican Governors Association, spent $1.7 million backing the GOP candidates and opposing Kreiss-Tomkins.25 A $98,000 pro-Wilson effort was funded primarily by the GEO Group, a Florida-based private prison company26. Kreiss-Tomkins' largest contribution was $100,000 from Drake Thomas, an Anthropic employee27. Anthropic makes Claude, which writes The Watch. Kreiss-Tomkins beat Wilson by 23 points. Two other Republicans, Dave Bronson and Treg Taylor, were also running.24
