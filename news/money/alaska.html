In Alaska, a nine-figure Senate fight comes down to 637 votes

Super PACs spent heavily for and against Mary Peltola and Dan Sullivan, including on long-shot spoiler candidates. The Senate race waits on a Nov. 18 ranked-choice count. The Democrat who outraised his rivals leads for governor.

Two days after the polls closed, Democrat Mary Peltola, a former congresswoman, had 102,962 votes and Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan had 102,325. The Associated Press has not called the race. If neither candidate has more than 50% of the vote on Nov. 18, the Division of Elections will tabulate ranked choices.1 Peltola's lead of 0.3 points comes in a state Donald Trump carried by 13.8 points in 2024.

Where the money came from

According to the Federal Election Commission, Peltola's campaign raised $36.9 million and Sullivan's raised $14.1 million. The FEC independent-expenditure data The Watch read on Nov. 8 counts $17.9 million in outside spending on the race: $12.5 million that helped Peltola and $5.4 million that helped Sullivan. That works out to $83.84 per vote cast. Those figures are incomplete. They show no Senate Leadership Fund spending against Peltola, for example. Other counts are far larger. The Anchorage Daily News, citing OpenSecrets, reported that independent groups spent a combined total of more than $100 million on the race — including $43 million opposing Peltola and $40 million opposing Sullivan2. In October, Alaska Public Media reported that independent groups backing Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan have outspent those campaigning for Democratic former Congresswoman Mary Peltola by nearly $7 million.3

The two parties' Senate super PACs set the scale. The Democratic-aligned Senate Majority PAC has planned roughly $11 million for the race, while the Republican Senate Leadership Fund has pledged about $15 million to back Sullivan.4 The Senate Leadership Fund is a super PAC, meaning it can raise and spend unlimited sums but may not coordinate with candidates. It is closely affiliated with One Nation, a 501(c)(4) nonprofit organization that is not legally required to publicly disclose its donors5. That arrangement is what the legal term "dark money" means. According to Google's transparency report, One Nation spent $1.3 million on Google ads in Alaska. Majority Forward Fund, a group allied with the Democrats, spent $2.2 million.

The largest single anti-Sullivan spender was North Star, a super PAC that reported $7.3 million against him in the FEC figures. The Daily News described it as the largest outside spender on Peltola's behalf. The Maryland-based group spends on Democratic candidates in what it describes as the "most critical races"6. On the Republican side, Last Frontier PAC reported $2.8 million against Peltola. The Daily Sitka Sentinel described it as a group supporting incumbent Sen. Sullivan that's funded by wealthy conservative donors7. Americans for Prosperity Action reported $987,000 for Sullivan and Club for Growth Action $689,000. Midnight Sun PAC reported $1.3 million for Peltola in the FEC figures. Its top donor is political nonprofit Midnight Sun Independence Fund, which is based in Washington, D.C.8

The governor's race, which is reported to the state's Alaska Public Offices Commission rather than the FEC, was lopsided. Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins, the Democrat, has raised more than the sum raised by the three Republicans in the race combined, according to campaign finance reports9. Early on, six employees of Anthropic, a California-based artificial intelligence company, who collectively gave Kreiss-Tomkins $372,00010 were among his top contributors. Forward Frontier, a group funded in part by the Democratic Governors Association, has spent $4.4 million backing Kreiss-Tomkins11. Kodiak PAC, a group funded by the Republican Governors Association, spent $1.7 million backing the Republican candidates and opposing Kreiss-Tomkins.

What the ads said

One North Star ad criticizes Sullivan for accepting hundreds of thousands of dollars from commercial trawling companies, linking the industry to declines in salmon runs6. Ads paid for by the Senate Leadership Fund said Peltola "sold Alaska out"12. Others claimed she was "just a liberal in Alaska clothes."12 No Going Back, a pro-Trump PAC, ran a different attack. According to AdImpact, it has spent more than $3.8 million to air the ads, which have 8.6 million impressions so far13. The ads said Peltola "wants our money working for they/them."13

Two late Republican ads drew on New York Times reporting. Last Frontier PAC bought an approximately $600,000 buy14 of broadcast time for an ad about allegations that Peltola mistreated her staff. Her campaign denied abusive behavior15. In the final week, the PAC joined 59 PAC, which CNN described as an affiliate of the Senate Leadership Fund in Alaska, in a "high six-figure" ad campaign on TV and streaming. The ads accused Peltola of recruiting a second, unrelated Dan Sullivan to the ballot. Peltola's campaign has continued to deny involvement16.

Both sides spent money promoting long-shot candidates. CNN reported that Right for Alaska spent more than $2.3 million in the days before the vote to attack Sen. Dan Sullivan and boost Heikes17, meaning Republican candidate Gerald Heikes. After the primary, a second group, Alaska Values PAC, ran YouTube ads, mailers and Facebook ads telling voters "Rank one name, leave the rest blank: Only rank Heikes"18. The Daily News reported that one group backing Heikes is heavily funded by progressive organization Sixteen Thirty Fund19. On the Republican side, Fair Deal Alaska spent about $800,000 before the primary, mostly on mail and text messages, promoting Democrat Carol Hafner and another long-shot candidate. Another $100,000 for Fair Deal Alaska came from Last Frontier PAC7.

Did it work?

The Senate race is not decided, so the answer is not yet in. Which side spent more depends on whose count you use. The FEC figures favor Peltola's allies. OpenSecrets has the two sides roughly even. The October reporting, and AdImpact's figures on advertising time booked ahead, favored Sullivan's. AdImpact found Republicans have reserved $45 million in Alaska advertising from Aug. 1 through Election Day, compared with Democrats' $32 million15. Peltola raised more than twice as much as Sullivan, and she leads by 637 votes.

The spoiler candidates may matter in the ranked-choice count. Heikes has 2.3% and Dan J. Sullivan 1.7%. Sullivan's campaign circulated a memo projecting a Sullivan victory20.

In the governor's race, the side that spent more is well ahead. Kreiss-Tomkins had 49.1% of first-choice votes to 29.2% for Republican Bernadette Wilson. In a preliminary ranked-choice tally by the voting-reform group FairVote, he defeats Bernadette Wilson 55-45%21. The AP has not called that race either. The state aims to certify results by Nov. 25.
