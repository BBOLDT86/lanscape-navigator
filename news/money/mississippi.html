The money · race by race · Mississippi

Viral beef ad, late PAC money put Mississippi's Senate seat in doubt

Democratic super PACs and the party's Senate committee came in late. In the final stretch, AdImpact data showed Cindy Hyde-Smith outgunned on the air. Scott Colom leads by 2,574 votes, and the Associated Press has not called the race.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read not yet. 22 sources. All the states › · How Mississippi voted ›

Four days after the polls closed, Mississippi was still counting ballots in its U.S. Senate race. The Associated Press tally shows Scott Colom, the Democratic district attorney for the 16th Judicial District, with 467,930 votes and Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith, the Republican incumbent, with 465,356. That is a lead of 2,574 votes, or 0.3 points, in a state Donald Trump carried by 22.9 points in 2024. The AP has not called the race, and Mississippi's Secretary of State Office confirmed to ABC News that the state has no formal recount process.1

The Federal Election Commission totals The Watch normally uses had not been loaded for Mississippi at press time. The dollar figures below come from filings as reported by the state press, national outlets and the ad-tracking firm AdImpact. Mississippi elects its governor in odd-numbered years, so this Senate race was the main contest on the ballot.

Where the money came from

Hyde-Smith had the larger campaign treasury. According to the Magnolia Tribune's reading of third-quarter FEC reports, her main campaign committee had raised $6.2 million this cycle2. Colom reported a cash on hand total of $992,000 after raising $1.32 million in the third quarter.2 Mississippi Today noted that over those three months Colom raised almost twice as much money as his Republican opponent3.

The national groups came in late. On Oct. 20 Senate Majority PAC, the super PAC aligned with Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer, announced that it would spend $1.2 million on a new television advertisement to boost Scott Colom4. Newsweek reported that it later invested another $1 million.5 The Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee, the party's Senate campaign arm, added $500,000 with Mississippi Democratic Senate candidate Scott Colom in a coordinated ad buy6, according to Semafor.

Two smaller Democratic-leaning super PACs also spent heavily. New Deal for All is the family-run super PAC of Florida attorney Steve Pajcic7, and Axios reported it put around $1.1 million7 into Mississippi. New Southern Majority, a super PAC launched by the Southern Poverty Law Center's political advocacy arm8, spent just under $1 million8 on messages aimed at about 200,000 Republican voters, according to Brannon Miller, whose firm, Chism Strategies, ran the effort.

On the Republican side, Senate Leadership Fund, the main super PAC for Senate Republicans, launched an ad campaign in the "low seven figures" in Mississippi9. Leading the Future is an AI-industry super PAC funded by Greg Brockman, president of OpenAI, and prominent venture capitalists Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz10. Mississippi Today reported it was spending $2 million to support Hyde-Smith and three other Republican Senate candidates. Hyde-Smith's camp disavowed10 the group.

The big Senate super PACs raise their money nationally. Elon Musk gave $158 million to the Senate Leadership Fund11. The fund has also taken money from One Nation, a registered 501(c)(4) organization12 that does not have to disclose its donors. That is what "dark money" means in its legal sense. Public Citizen lists Majority Forward, a similar nonprofit, as giving $102 million13 to Senate Majority PAC. None of the reporting ties a specific donor's money to Mississippi.

The ads

The ad that got the most attention cost Colom's campaign the least. It opens with a clip of Hyde-Smith answering a question about beef prices in a February interview with WLOX: "you have so many proteins to choose from."14 A string of Mississippians then reply that they would rather pick another senator. According to the Clarion Ledger, the video garnered 2.8 million views on social media in 36 hours.15

The paid TV campaigns followed. Senate Majority PAC's spot called Hyde-Smith a "'do nothing' senator who voted to kick people off Medicaid while pocketing thousands from special interests"16 and highlights his Christian faith.16 The ad Colom ran jointly with the DSCC will run statewide on TV and digital6. In the final week, the most-aired spot was New Deal for All's ad with Jena-Lisa Jones, who says Jeffrey Epstein abused her. It aired more than 1,200 times over the last week, costing the left-leaning New Deal for All super PAC over $329K, per AdImpact.17 In a statement, Hyde-Smith said a procedural vote on one amendment is not the same thing as saying the Epstein files should remain secret17.

Hyde-Smith's own ad called Colom the "transgender defender"17. It played over 2,800 times across Mississippi and Memphis, Tenn., television markets in the 30 days since its Sept. 10 debut, costing the senator's campaign over $854K, per AdImpact.18 Senate Leadership Fund's ad attacked Colom for support from Chuck Schumer, Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, D-N.Y., and George Soros19.

Did the money buy the seat?

By AdImpact's count, reported by the AP on Oct. 20, the race had seen only about $8 million in total spending, with Colom outspending Hyde-Smith nearly $4.6 million to $3.2 million4. That was before most of the late super PAC money landed. In the closing week, AdImpact data shows that only one of the eight most-aired spots portrays her positively.17 So far, the side that spent more on the air is ahead in a count that is not finished.

People who worked on or studied the race credit different things. Miller pointed to a facility in DeSoto that has drawn significant backlash from locals about persistent noise issues.8 Deborah Williams, the DeSoto County Democratic chair, gave more credit to organizers knocking on nearly 70,000 doors8. Aaron Hitefield, a political scientist at Mississippi State University, said Republicans seemed apathetic about Hyde-Smith20, but the data so far do not show Republicans switching to Colom. The independent candidate, Ty Pinkins, said he stayed in the race after realizing he could not win because he believed it would boost Colom's candidacy.21

Lawyers are now involved. The National Republican Senatorial Committee has asked all 82 county election commissions to separate out the affidavit ballots, document their disposition and preserve "all associated materials."22
