The money · race by race · Florida

Florida Republicans outspent Democrats and held both top seats, narrowly

Byron Donalds's operation raised more than six times what David Jolly raised, and a Ken Griffin-backed super PAC carried Ashley Moody's Senate attacks. Both Republicans won, by 1.3 and 2.9 points, in a state Trump carried by 13.

Florida's two statewide races on Nov. 3 were lopsided in money and close in votes. According to AP results, Republican Byron Donalds beat Democrat David Jolly for governor by 1.3 percentage points, 4,134,526 votes to 4,027,416. In the U.S. Senate special election, Republican Sen. Ashley Moody beat Democratic state Rep. Angie Nixon by 2.9 points. Both margins were more than 10 points narrower than Donald Trump's 13.1-point win in Florida in 2024.

Where the money came from

The governor's race is a state contest, so its money is reported to the state, not the Federal Election Commission. Records filed with the Division of Elections show that his Friends of Byron Donalds political committee combined with his campaign account to raise more than $173 million, spending more than $165 million of that through Oct. 29.1 Jolly, between his Florida 2026 committee and his campaign account, raised nearly $27 million and spent nearly $25 million through the same date.1

Donalds's money came in very large checks. The Miami Times reported that billionaires like Ken Griffin ($15 million) and Jeff Yass ($7.5 million)1 gave, along with GEO Group, the private prison operator, at $4.5 million and the Seminole Tribe at $6 million. Miami New Times, working from the committee's records, put GEO Group's total at $3.5 million2 and found that Club for Growth Action Florida2 gave $3.75 million. By mid-September, Jolly's political committee had received 18 donations of six figures or more. Donalds' committee reported more than 300.3 Jolly's biggest donor, hedge fund manager Donald Sussman, gave $1.5 million. National donors largely deployed resources elsewhere, though the Democratic Governors Association ultimately contributed $1.2 million.1 After the election, a Jolly adviser gave a different account: he said a reported $1 million donation to Jolly from the Democratic Governors Association ended up at about $200,000 because so many strings were attached.4

In the Senate race, FEC filings show Moody's campaign raised $14.5 million. Florida Politics reported that through Oct. 14, the Democrat raised $11.3 million5, and that Moody also collected $7.14 million in a victory fund and $3.26 million in a victory committee. The FEC independent-expenditure data The Watch read on Nov. 8 lists only $661,000 in outside spending: $625,000 for Moody, led by SLF PAC ($250,000) and Senate Conservatives Fund ($233,000), and $36,000 against her from Out of the Swamp PAC. That count misses the big general-election spenders that news outlets found in the filings. Florida Politics reported that the Stronger Safer Nation super PAC organized to help Moody spent $2.95 million boosting the Republican through ad reservations, as well as a massive $10.3 million thrashing Nixon.5 On the other side, American Bridge dumped $2.5 million in ad buys on Nixon's behalf, while New Deal For All, a Jacksonville-based PAC, spent upward of $6.48 million on ads pummeling Moody.5

Reuters reported that Stronger Safer Nation was backed by a $2.5 million donation in May from Citadel founder Ken Griffin and $6.7 million from a Florida state PAC when it closed in March6. The Tampa Bay Times named Paul Singer of Elliott Management as another major donor. According to Central Florida Public Media, all of New Deal for All's money came from a pair of donations last week from Stephen Pajcic III, the founder of a Jacksonville injury law firm.7 Pajcic is also a former Democratic state legislator.

What the ads said

Donalds's ads hit Jolly on costs. Donalds' Sept. 8 ad about Jolly's plan said the proposal would cost "$1,000, per Florida family, every single year,"8 calling it a "hurricane tax." PolitiFact found that the ad references a recent study but oversimplifies its findings. It didn't evaluate Jolly's plan, instead looking at a Florida House proposal from 2024.8 An ad from Friends of Byron Donalds, a political committee aligned with Donalds, says Jolly wants more taxes on tips, overtime and Social Security.9 WUFT noted that those are federal taxes, so the governor of Florida would have no control over the taxes Donalds refers to in the ad10. Online, Google's transparency report shows the Donalds campaign spending $8.1 million on Google and YouTube ads in Florida, and his PAC another $3.8 million. Jolly answered late: an early-October ad rebutted the suggestion Jolly supports a "hurricane tax" and called Donalds a "liar."3 According to AdImpact, Jolly and Democrats had purchased just $3 million of airtime in Florida3 by early October.

The Senate ads were about crime and ideology. Stronger Safer Nation's ad said Nixon wanted to defund police, and Moody, it continues, is married to a police officer and "locked up killers and rapists" as Florida's attorney general.11 NBC News reported that much of Moody's messaging and campaign ads focused on policies proposed by the Democratic Socialists of America more broadly, even as Nixon publicly distanced herself from them.12 New Deal for All's ads said Moody "cast the deciding vote to block the release of the Epstein files."13 PolitiFact said the claim missed context.

On Google's list of Florida's biggest "political" advertisers, the second-largest was not a campaign at all. Kalshi, a prediction market, spent $7.5 million on Google ads in the state.

Did it work?

In both races, the side with more money won. In the Senate race, it also won the outside-money count by either measure, the FEC figures above or the press tallies. Central Florida Public Media counted spending to advantage Moody totaled $27.1 million, compared to about $14.9 million for Nixon.7 Still, both Republicans won by far less than Trump did in 2024.

Jolly's campaign argued that the money gap decided the race. Late in the race, the campaign prepared negative TV ads, but the ads never aired. The campaign was out of money.4 His adviser said an additional $10 million for TV probably would have won it. A post-election account of the race credited something besides TV. It reported that just as instrumental down the stretch for Donalds was a sprawling turnout machine that in the waning hours of the race found the necessary votes.14 Jolly himself said "The money they spent in the field in South Florida was the most effective."14

In the Senate race, a pre-election poll reported by News4Jax found the attacks had a mixed effect: 25% said Republican messaging about Nixon's ties to the Democratic Socialists of America made them less likely to vote for her, while 12% said it made them more likely and 55% said it made no difference.15 Whether the Republican spending saved two seats or just failed to widen the gap is the reader's call. The money went overwhelmingly one way, and so did the results, by small margins.
