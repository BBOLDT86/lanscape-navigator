Florida Republicans Far Outspent Democrats and Held Both Statewide Seats, Narrowly

Byron Donalds spent about $166 million to David Jolly's $25 million and won by 1.3 points. Ashley Moody won by 2.9 points, helped by a super PAC that billionaire Ken Griffin helped fund.

Florida Democrats came closer in both statewide races on Nov. 3 than the state's recent history suggested. Sen. Ashley Moody, a Republican, was appointed to the US Senate in January 2025 after Marco Rubio became secretary of state1. She beat state Rep. Angela "Angie" Nixon 4,211,733 to 3,975,375, a margin of 2.9 points. Rep. Byron Donalds, a Republican, beat former Rep. David Jolly for governor by 1.3 points. Donald Trump carried Florida by 13.1 points in 2024, so both Democrats moved the state by 10 to 12 points. In both races the Republican side had far more money.

Where the money came from

The governor's race is a state race, so its numbers come from Florida's filings. Through Oct. 29, his campaign account and affiliated political committee had raised more than $173 million and spent more than $165 million combined, according to state filings.2 Jolly's campaign account and Florida 2026 political committee had raised nearly $27 million and spent nearly $25 million.2 By The Watch's arithmetic, that works out to about $20 for every vote cast for governor on Donalds's side and about $3 on Jolly's.

Donalds's committee, Friends of Byron Donalds, drew its largest checks from finance and gambling. Its largest individual donor is billionaire Ken Griffin, founder and CEO of Miami-based hedge fund Citadel, who has contributed $15 million3, the Miami New Times reported. Jeff Yass of Susquehanna International Group has contributed another $7.5 million3. The Seminole Tribe of Florida, which owns Hard Rock International and operates the state's dominant legal sports betting platform, has contributed $6 million3.

In the Senate race, Moody raised $14.5 million and Nixon $11.3 million, according to their FEC reports. Outside groups reported $11.0 million in independent expenditures: $7.9 million helped the Republican side and $3.1 million helped the Democratic side. That is about $1.33 per vote cast. The biggest outside spender was Stronger Safer Nation, a super PAC. In FEC data read Nov. 17, it had spent $6.3 million against Nixon and $879,000 for Moody. According to Reuters, the PAC was backed by a $2.5 million donation in May from Citadel founder Ken Griffin and $6.7 million from a Florida state PAC when it closed in March4. Its other donors include RAI Services, a subsidiary of the tobacco giant Reynolds American5, which gave $400,000. Press tallies of the PAC's spending run higher than the FEC figures used here: Florida Politics reported $2.95 million boosting the Republican through ad reservations, as well as a massive $10.3 million thrashing Nixon.6

On the Democratic side, the largest spender was New Deal For All, Inc., with $3.0 million against Moody in the FEC data. Florida Politics put its spending upward of $6.48 million6. According to Axios, Pajcic and his children, Michael and Helen, launched the New Deal for All super PAC this month to run ads in Florida, Kansas, Mississippi and South Carolina.7 Steve Pajcic is a Jacksonville lawyer and former Democratic state legislator. He told Axios he hasn't consulted Democratic Party leaders or any of the candidates he hopes to help.7

The ads

The ads paid for by Stronger Safer Nation went after Nixon on crime. "Radical Angie Nixon wants to defund our police and empty our prisons," the ad says.8 The Tampa Bay Times reported that it then said Moody is married to a police officer and "locked up killers and rapists" as Florida's attorney general.8 Other ads backing Moody tied Nixon to positions held by the Democratic Socialists of America, Folio Weekly found. Nixon has specifically said she does not agree with every policy position of the organization.9 AdImpact figures posted by reporter Kimberly Leonard showed that Stronger Safer Nation had spent $3M on negative ads against Nixon thru Oct 5. No pro-Nixon TV.10

New Deal for All ran two kinds of ads. One shows characters playing Moody and Uthmeier in a hospital stealing equipment from sick children11, a reference to the Hope Florida settlement money. Another, called "Protect," featured a woman who survived Jeffrey Epstein's abuse. It said Moody "cast the deciding vote to block the release of the Epstein files."12 PolitiFact rated the claim Mostly False13 and noted that Moody later joined her Senate colleagues to pass the measure by "unanimous consent"14.

In the governor's race, a Donalds ad from Sept. 8 says Jolly's plan would cost "$1,000, per Florida family, every single year,"15 calling it a "hurricane tax." Fact-checkers reported by WUSF concluded that Jolly's plan likely will not save homeowners money on insurance, but it also will not create a $1,000 hurricane tax outright16. An ad from Friends of Byron Donalds said Jolly wanted higher taxes on tips, overtime and Social Security, but Florida also has no individual state income tax, meaning a Florida governor doesn't control the federal income taxes referenced by the commercial.9 Online, Google's transparency report shows $8.1 million in Florida from Donalds's campaign and $3.8 million from his committee. The largest "political" advertiser on Google in the state was Kalshi, a prediction market, at $9.3 million.

Jolly answered late. According to AdImpact, reported by CNN, Jolly and Democrats had purchased just $3 million of airtime in Florida17 by early October. His closing ad, a seven-figure buy, opened with Jolly saying "I don't support a hurricane tax or socialism, and I never will"18.

Did it work?

Both races went to the side that spent more. In the Senate race, Republicans had about two and a half times as much outside money as Democrats. In the governor's race, Donalds outspent Jolly by roughly 6.7 to 1 and won by 105,376 votes. But the money did not stop a large swing toward Democrats.

Jolly's campaign blames the gap. "For whatever sets of reasons, there just wasn't an investment made," said Mark Riddle, Jolly's senior adviser.19 After the election, Jolly said that with national money, "without question we would have won."20 That is the losing campaign's claim, not a finding. CNN reported in October that national Democrats have prioritized other battlegrounds, including a costly fight over a Texas Senate seat17. Whether more money would have closed a 1.3-point gap is for the reader to judge.
