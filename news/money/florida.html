The money · race by race · Florida

Ken Griffin's money backed both Florida Republicans who narrowly held on

Republicans spent far more than Democrats in Florida's Senate and governor's races. A super PAC backed by Ken Griffin ran most of the outside ads in the Senate race. Both Republicans won by less than three points.

Republicans won both of Florida's statewide races on Nov. 3, by small margins, after spending much more than the Democrats. Sen. Ashley Moody beat state Rep. Angie Nixon 4,211,455 votes to 3,974,149, a margin of 2.9 points, according to the Associated Press count published by The New York Times. Rep. Byron Donalds beat former Rep. David Jolly for governor 4,135,701 to 4,029,462, a margin of 1.3 points. Donald Trump won the state by 13.1 points in 2024.

Where the money came from

In the Senate race, Federal Election Commission records read Nov. 10 show $7.9 million in independent expenditures for the Republican side and $69,000 for the Democratic side. Independent expenditures are paid for by outside groups that may not coordinate with the campaigns. Almost all of the Republican money came from one committee, Stronger Safer Nation. It reported spending $6.3 million against Nixon and $879,000 for Moody. Smaller amounts came from SLF PAC ($280,000), the Senate Conservatives Fund ($233,000) and Senate Conservatives Action ($87,000). On the candidates' own books, Moody's campaign took in $14.5 million and Nixon's $11.3 million.

Stronger Safer Nation is a super PAC established in 2025 with the stated purpose of supporting Republican Ashley Moody1. The Tampa Bay Times reported it has raised more than $12 million, with major donations from billionaires Ken Griffin, CEO of Citadel LLC, and Paul Elliott Singer, CEO of Elliott Management Corp.2 According to Reuters, Griffin donated $2.5 million to Stronger Safer Nation in May.3

News reports give bigger numbers than the FEC data used here. Florida Politics reported that Stronger Safer Nation spent $2.95 million boosting the Republican through ad reservations, as well as a massive $10.3 million thrashing Nixon4. It also reported that American Bridge dumped $2.5 million in ad buys on Nixon's behalf, while New Deal For All, a Jacksonville-based PAC, spent upward of $6.48 million on ads pummeling Moody.4 Neither of those Democratic groups shows up in the FEC independent-expenditure figures used for this story, and The Watch could not find out why.

New Deal for All is a super PAC that Pajcic and his children, Michael and Helen, launched5 in October. Steve Pajcic is a co-founder of the personal-injury law firm Pajcic & Pajcic6 and a former Democratic state legislator.

The governor's race is a state contest, so its money is reported to Florida, not the FEC. Donalds' campaign says he "raised over $174 million from 49,694 unique donors7." Post-election coverage put Jolly's total at the $27 million that Jolly raised8. A post-election review of the filings found that Donalds' committee, Friends of Byron Donalds, collected more than $155 million in contributions9. Miami New Times reported that Griffin has contributed $15 million to Donalds' gubernatorial effort10, and that Jeff Yass, the billionaire cofounder of Susquehanna International Group10, gave $7.5 million. The same report found that the Seminole Tribe of Florida, which owns Hard Rock and runs the state's main legal sports-betting platform, gave $6 million, and that a private prison company with immigration detention contracts gave $3.5 million. Online, Google's transparency report shows Donalds' campaign spent $8.1 million on Google ads in Florida and Friends of Byron Donalds PAC spent $3.8 million. The prediction market Kalshi spent $7.6 million in the state, but it was not advertising for any candidate.

What the ads said

Stronger Safer Nation's ads said Nixon was against the police. One of them said "Radical Angie Nixon wants to defund our police and empty our prisons"2 and called Moody a lawmaker who "locked up killers and rapists" as Florida's attorney general.2 Moody's own spots used the same theme. In one, NBC News reported, "Angie Nixon is actually a socialist," a narrator says11, while the ad lists Democratic Socialists of America positions such as abolishing the police, which Nixon has said she does not want to do. PolitiFact found that a Moody TV ad set in a diner mischaracterizes12 Nixon's position: she wants to repeal the 2025 tax law, but her objections are to its tax breaks for the very wealthy.

New Deal for All's ad "Protect" featured a survivor of Jeffrey Epstein's abuse. It said Moody "cast the deciding vote to block the release of the Epstein files," a claim PolitiFact rated Mostly False. According to Axios, the group's other Florida ads hit Moody and Attorney General James Uthmeier over a scandal involving $10 million from a Medicaid settlement5.

In the governor's race, Donalds repeated one attack over and over. His ad, widely played on broadcast, cable and digital platforms13, said Jolly's insurance plan would cost Florida families $1,000 a year in a "hurricane tax." PolitiFact said the ad references a recent study but oversimplifies its findings14. Jolly opened the general election with an ad called "Broken" about the cost of living, backed by an initial $1 million buy15 on broadcast and digital. Near the end, his senior adviser Mark Riddle said, the campaign had several attack ads ready. The ads never aired. The campaign was out of money.16

Did it work?

In both races, the side that spent more won. In the Senate race, outside spending in the FEC data came to 96 cents for every vote cast, almost all of it for Moody. But both Republicans won by far less than Trump did two years ago: about 10 points less in the Senate race and nearly 12 in the governor's race.

Each side reads the result differently. Donalds' campaign says its money paid for $80 million in campaign ads7, plus a $20 million get-out-the-vote program that hit 2.25 million doors7. Jolly's camp says a lack of money cost them the race. Riddle said "An extra $10 million for TV for David Jolly probably wins this race."16 He also said a $1 million pledge from the Democratic Governors Association ended up at about $200,000 because so many strings were attached.16 In the Senate race, NBC News reported that Moody relied on her significant fundraising advantage to flood the airwaves11. Nixon, who was outspent, still finished within three points. Whether the money decided either race, or only limited a Democratic swing, is for the reader to judge.
