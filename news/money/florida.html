The money · race by race · Florida

Donalds's $165 million and a Griffin-backed super PAC held Florida, narrowly

Republicans Byron Donalds and Ashley Moody outspent their Democratic opponents several times over and won Florida's governorship and Senate seat. They won by 1.3 and 2.9 points in a state Donald Trump carried by 13.1.

In Florida on Nov. 3, the side with more money won both statewide races. Republican Byron Donalds beat Democrat David Jolly for governor by 1.3 points, 4,134,526 votes to 4,027,416. Sen. Ashley Moody, a Republican, held her seat against Democratic state Rep. Angie Nixon by 2.9 points, 4,210,242 to 3,972,095, according to AP results published by The New York Times. Trump carried Florida by 13.1 points in 2024. The Federal Election Commission figures The Watch uses had not been processed for Florida by press time, and the governor's race is reported to the state, not the FEC. The dollar figures below therefore come from state filings and the outlets named.

Where the money came from

Florida Politics reviewed state Division of Elections filings and found that Donalds's Friends of Byron Donalds political committee and his campaign account raised more than $173 million and spent more than $165 million of it through Oct. 29.1 Jolly's Florida 2026 committee and his campaign account raised nearly $27 million and spent nearly $25 million2 over the same period. Other outlets give somewhat different totals. The USA Today Network put Donalds's haul at more than $175 million, the second-biggest for a Florida governor's candidate since Ron DeSantis's 2022 reelection3. The Miami Herald found that Friends of Byron Donalds alone collected more than $155 million4.

According to Florida Politics, the biggest checks came from billionaires Ken Griffin ($15 million) and Jeff Yass ($7.5 million), the private prison operator GEO Group ($4.5 million) and the Seminole Tribe ($6 million), with data center and power companies also heavily involved.2 The Herald noted that the tribe holds a monopoly on online sports betting in Florida under the state gaming compact.4 On the Democratic side, Jolly's biggest donor, hedge fund manager Donald Sussman, gave $1.5 million, and the Democratic Governors Association ultimately gave $1.2 million.2

In the Senate race, Florida Politics reported that by Oct. 14 Nixon had raised $11.3 million and Moody $14.5 million, plus $7.14 million in a victory fund and $3.26 million in a victory committee.5 Most of the outside money came from Stronger Safer Nation, a super PAC that can raise unlimited sums but cannot coordinate with campaigns. It spent $2.95 million supporting Moody and $10.3 million against Nixon.5 Reuters reported that the group was funded by a $2.5 million donation from Citadel founder Ken Griffin and $6.7 million from a Florida state PAC that closed in March, and spent in no other race.6 The Tampa Bay Times reported that it also took $400,000 from RAI Services, part of Reynolds American, and $100,000 from U.S. Sugar.7 On the other side, American Bridge spent $2.5 million in ads for Nixon, and New Deal for All, a Jacksonville-based PAC, spent more than $6.48 million in ads against Moody.5 PolitiFact identified New Deal for All as a PAC launched by Steve Pajcic, a Florida lawyer and former Democratic state legislator.8

What the ads said

The defining ad of the governor's race was Donalds's "Disaster." It claimed Jolly's hurricane insurance proposal would cost Florida families $1,000 a year in what it called a "hurricane tax."9 Jolly's actual plan is a state-backed fund to carry hurricane and wind risk so insurers don't price it in.10 Fox 13 found that the study cited in the ad did not evaluate Jolly's proposal.11 PolitiFact concluded that the plan likely would not save homeowners money but would not create a $1,000 hurricane tax outright.12 Another ad from Friends of Byron Donalds said Jolly wants more taxes on tips, overtime and Social Security.13 Fact-checkers noted that a Florida governor would have no control over those taxes because Florida has no income tax.14

Jolly answered with an ad that rebutted the "hurricane tax" claim and called Donalds a "liar."15 In early October, Jolly said he had enough money to stay on TV through Election Day, but not to separately target Black voters, reach disaffected Republicans or sustain attacks.15 At that point, Jolly and Democrats had bought just $3 million of Florida airtime, according to AdImpact.15

The Senate ads focused on policing and socialism. Stronger Safer Nation's ad said "Radical Angie Nixon wants to defund our police and empty our prisons."16 One of Moody's own spots said "Angie Nixon is actually a socialist" and focused on ideas like abolishing the police, which Nixon said she does not want.17 A New Deal for All ad accused Moody of casting "the deciding vote to block the release of the Epstein files."8 PolitiFact said that ad misses context.

Did it work?

The side that spent more won both races. In the Senate race, Central Florida Public Media's count in late October showed that spending to advantage Moody totaled $27.1 million, compared to about $14.9 million for Nixon.18 The governor's race was more lopsided, roughly six to one by Florida Politics' figures.

Still, both Republicans won by margins 10 to 12 points narrower than Trump's in 2024. The two camps have explained the result differently. "Raising more than $175 million in politics is a powerful tool," Jolly said3 shortly before polls closed. Jolly gave the most credit to Donalds's paid field operation in South Florida, saying "He paid his door knockers $28 an hour."19 Ryan Smith, Donalds's chief strategist, said Jolly got money so late it could only go to TV, and had no turnout operation.19 The USA Today Network reported that national Democrats' attention was diverted to unexpectedly competitive races in South Carolina, Ohio and Georgia.3 Whether that money bought the seats, or only protected them, is for the reader to judge.
