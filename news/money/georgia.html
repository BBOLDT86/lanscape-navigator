The money · race by race · Georgia

Georgia's $200 million self-funder lost; Ossoff's 13-to-1 money edge held

Rick Jackson's own fortune paid for much of Georgia's record ad spending, and he still lost the governor's race by nearly nine points. Jon Ossoff raised almost 13 times as much as Mike Collins and won by 18.

Few states saw more political advertising this year. About $562 million was spent across Georgia, the fifth most of any state, according to political ad tracker AdImpact's Election Day Insights Center data1, as reported by The Atlanta Journal-Constitution. The two biggest spenders had opposite results. Republican Rick Jackson paid for most of his own campaign for governor and lost to Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms by 8.7 points. Democratic Sen. Jon Ossoff outraised Republican Rep. Mike Collins by nearly 13 to 1 and won by 18.1.

Where the money came from

Ossoff's campaign reported $136.6 million in receipts to the Federal Election Commission. Collins's campaign reported $10.7 million. Ossoff's campaign also reported transferring more than $3.6 million to other Democratic candidates, including Bottoms.2

Outside groups reported $30.5 million in independent expenditures in the Senate race. Of that, $19.3 million helped the Democratic side and $11.1 million the Republican side. The biggest single spender was SLF PAC, the Senate Leadership Fund's super PAC, with $9.9 million against Ossoff. In April the group announced an initial $44 million investment in the Georgia U.S. Senate race3. Trackers that also count pending late filings put its total nearer $11.5 million4. The tracker Legis1's tally of the super PAC's filings lists Miriam Adelson at $30 million, Stephen Schwarzman at $17 million, Paul Singer at $14.5 million, and Kenneth Griffin at $10 million5 among its donors. The fund is also closely affiliated with One Nation, a 501(c)(4) nonprofit organization that is not legally required to publicly disclose its donors6. Money from such groups is what the term "dark money" means. Google's ad library credits two One Nation accounts with $9.9 million and $4.6 million in Georgia ads.

On the Democratic side, The Georgia Way spent $6.1 million against Collins. A group of that name was formed by Senate Majority PAC in November 20207 for the runoffs that year. The current committee registered June 12, 20268 as a super PAC and reported $8,500,095.00 in total receipts8 through Oct. 14. The Watch did not find a published list of its 2026 donors. Lower Costs Now spent $1.5 million against Collins and $370,000 for Ossoff. It received more than $50 million as of Oct. 14, with nearly all of its donations coming via transfers from two other political committees — SMP, which contributed $30 million, and FF PAC, which gave $20 million9.

The Republican primary drew money of its own. Hardworking Americans Inc., Gov. Brian Kemp's PAC, spent $4.2 million backing former football coach Derek Dooley. Daniel Dooley, founder of school safety company Centegix and the Senate candidate's brother, donated $100,00010 to the PAC. Democrats asked for an investigation, and Kemp's team pushed back in a statement, calling Democrats' claims "a new low."10 Georgia Values spent $853,000 against Collins, and Fellowship PAC spent $350,000 for him.

The governor's race was paid for mostly by one man. After spending more than $100 million of his own money to win the Republican primary, Jackson plowed another $88 million into his general election campaign11, according to Georgia Public Broadcasting's review of state filings. Late in the race, Bottoms got help: Michael Bloomberg made a $2.5 million donation for get-out-the-vote efforts, and the Democratic Governors Association made a late $5 million contribution, bringing its total to about $16 million.12

What the ads said

The Senate Leadership Fund's most talked-about ad uses AI-generated imagery to depict Democratic Sen.13 Ossoff in a salon. It claimed he voted to raise taxes on Social Security recipients earning under $75,00014. Ossoff responded to the Senate Leadership Fund ad, calling AI video a "slippery slope" for truth, per CNN.15 Collins' campaign responded with another AI-generated video featuring a cartoon depiction of Ossoff reacting angrily to the original ad.16

Collins's own first TV ad of the general election, "Deliver," opens with Collins standing in front of his 18-wheeler17 before attacking Ossoff's record on health care and taxes. Ossoff's "Emergency" ad highlighted a law that capped the price of insulin for seniors on Medicare at $35 a month18. It will run on broadcast television, streaming, and digital platforms as part of a seven-figure buy.18 J Street Action Fund's nearly half-million-dollar campaign focuses on Republican Rep. Mike Collins' extensive ties to white nationalists through his staff and son-in-law.19

Online, Google's report shows Ossoff's campaign spent $22.4 million in Georgia and Jackson's $14.6 million. The prediction market Kalshi's $3.7 million shows up in the same "political" category, though it is not a campaign.

Jackson used his personal fortune to blanket the state with television and digital advertising showing clips of street protests in Atlanta after the 2020 police killings20 of Rayshard Brooks and George Floyd. By AdImpact's count, reported by CNN, pro-Jackson advertising dwarfed pro-Bottoms advertising, $271.5 million to $32.8 million, including nearly $199 million from Jackson's campaign, which relentlessly attacked Bottoms on crime in Atlanta.12

Did it work?

In the Senate race, the side with more money won. Ossoff had more outside money and far more campaign money. By October, Democrats felt safe enough to pull back. Senate Majority PAC said it has made the decision to remove its week 3 ad spending2. Money was only part of the story. Collins's campaign was plagued with troubles from the beginning, from a campaign kickoff ad that misspelled Georgia to lackluster fund-raising and a series of scandalous headlines21, PBS NewsHour reported. In the primary, Kemp's PAC spent $4.2 million on Dooley, and he lost the runoff to Collins.

In the governor's race, the side that spent more lost. Jackson's ads outspent Bottoms's roughly eight to one, and she still won by 8.7 points in a state President Trump carried by 2.2. "Candidate quality, money, incumbency, none of those things seems to matter. Especially money,"22 said Kerwin Swint, a political scientist at Kennesaw State University. One number cuts the other way. Jackson lost by about nine fewer points than Collins did. Whether his spending accounts for that gap, the record cannot say.
