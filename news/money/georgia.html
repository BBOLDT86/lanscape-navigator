Georgia: Rick Jackson Spent Over $200 Million of His Own, and Lost

The healthcare billionaire outspent Keisha Lance Bottoms many times over on TV, mail and turnout. Meanwhile Jon Ossoff's small-donor fundraising dwarfed Mike Collins and his super PAC allies. Both Democrats won, by 8.7 and 18 points.

Georgia's two top races on Nov. 3 tested two kinds of money. In the governor's race, a Republican billionaire paid for nearly all of his own campaign. In the Senate race, a Democratic incumbent raised more than any other Senate candidate in the country. Both Democrats won. Keisha Lance Bottoms beat Rick Jackson by 8.7 points, 2,271,581 votes to 1,909,566. Sen. Jon Ossoff beat Rep. Mike Collins by 18 points, 2,466,422 to 1,712,444. Both counts are the Associated Press tallies published by The New York Times. Donald Trump carried Georgia by 2.2 points in 2024.

The Watch's Federal Election Commission data for Georgia had not been read at press time. The federal figures below come from filings as the press reported them and from the FEC's own committee pages.

Where the money came from

Jackson is the founder of Jackson Healthcare1. He rose from the foster care system to lead a company with over $3 billion in annual revenue1. He promised $50 million of his own money when he entered the race in February. By late October, The Atlanta Journal-Constitution reported that he had poured more than $200 million of his fortune into Georgia's race for governor, putting him among the biggest self-funding candidates in U.S. history2. According to state filing data compiled by PaidForPolitics, Jackson's campaign reported expenditures of about $213 million through Oct. 20, compared with $11.4 million for Bottoms3.

Bottoms' money came late. Reports filed with the State Ethics Commission showed she spent about $5.2 million4 through July. Then she raised $23.2 million since the beginning of August5, the AJC reported. An AJC review after the election found the biggest checks came from Democratic heavyweights. The Democratic Governors Association pumped more than $5 million into her leadership committee, while billionaire Michael Bloomberg contributed $2.5 million and Illinois Gov. JB Pritzker gave $1 million.6 Smaller donations were part of it too: one of her campaign disclosures listed 98,098 contributions7 too small to itemize.

Ossoff's campaign reported raising $33 million from July through September8. The campaign said nearly 97% of the 760,000 donations received during the third quarter were less than $1008. Collins received just shy of $5 million from Jan. 1, 2025, to June 30, 20269.

Outside groups were meant to close that gap. The Senate Leadership Fund, a super PAC controlled by Senate Majority Leader John Thune, has committed to spending $44 million in Georgia in hopes of defeating Ossoff10. It had raised a total of $253.7 million for the 2026 midterms11 as of June 30. It works closely with One Nation, a 501(c)(4) nonprofit organization that is not legally required to publicly disclose its donors12. That arrangement is what the term "dark money" describes. On the Democratic side, a super PAC called The Georgia Way took in $8,500,095.0013 through Oct. 14, according to the FEC. Independent-expenditure filings compiled by VoteLog show it spent $7,050,87114 against Collins, and VoteLog lists its only donor as Senate Majority PAC.

What the ads said

Jackson spent his money mostly on television. His two campaign accounts have spent nearly $165 million on TV and cable ads, at least $8 million on direct mail2. His general-election push began with an $87 million15 initial buy that reached viewers on television and online. AdImpact found Jackson has spent roughly 2% of his ad budget on radio16.

The ads were mostly about Bottoms' time as Atlanta's mayor. One, over footage of the 2020 unrest, said "When the city needed her, she let Atlanta burn"17. A September spot called "Disaster" ran statewide, and Jackson does not appear in it18. In October, the family of Secoriea Turner, an 8-year-old shot and killed in Atlanta in 2020, asked Jackson to take down an ad that used her mother's 911 call. His spokesman, Garrison Douglas, replied that the campaign would "continue to tell the stories of Keisha Lance Bottoms' failures as mayor."19

Bottoms answered on a much smaller budget. By late September, AdImpact counted about $8 million from her campaign and its allies, and that figure includes $7 million to the Bottoms campaign from the Democratic Governors Association20. In her first on-camera attack ad, which ran in all six of Georgia's television markets21, she said Jackson "made his fortune off a broken healthcare system and is still profiting off your pain"22.

In the Senate race, the Senate Leadership Fund's best-known ads showed AI-generated images of Ossoff in a salon. The attack rested on disclosure reports23 showing his campaign had paid an Atlanta-area makeup artist $2,887. Georgia has no law on the books addressing AI-generated political ads.24 Senate Majority PAC's message, set out with its initial $20 million reservation, was that Georgia Republicans backed Donald Trump's tariffs and supported stripping millions from Medicaid25.

Did it work?

Jackson's money did win him the primary. The AJC found it allowed Jackson to dominate the airwaves and overcome Lt. Burt Jones' support among conservative activists and President Donald Trump's endorsement26. It did not win him the general election. Counting only the more than $200 million he put in himself, Jackson spent over $100 for each of the 1,909,566 votes he received. The AP reported that Bottoms won despite a staggering campaign spending deficit27. She becomes the first Democrat to prevail in a Georgia governor's race since 1998.27 The race had looked close: a late-September YouGov poll had Jackson ahead of Bottoms 45% to 44%28, and analysts then blamed her thin ad budget. Her late fundraising surge came after that poll.

In the Senate race, the side with more money won, but both sides' outside groups had pulled back before Election Day. AdImpact reported that the Senate Leadership Fund had recently canceled $7.6 million in ad reservations in Georgia29. Senate Majority PAC dropped a week of its ads, saying Ossoff continues to have a competitive lead in the polls8. Collins also faced reports about people in his circle expressing racist and extremist views29.

Ossoff ran more than nine points ahead of Bottoms, and both Democrats did far better than their party did in 2024. The money totals alone cannot show how much of that came from the spending and how much from the national political climate.
