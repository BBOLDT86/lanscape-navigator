The money · race by race · Virginia

Warner won Virginia by 22 points with almost no outside money

Sen. Mark Warner outraised his Republican challenger about 25 to 1 and faced no outside attack. Virginia's real spending came in an $83 million redistricting vote a court voided, and in Hampton Roads House races where heavier spenders didn't always win.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 8, 2026. 21 sources. All the states › · How Virginia voted ›

On Nov. 3, Sen. Mark Warner, the Democrat seeking a fourth term that he says will be his last, beat Republican Bert Mizusawa, a retired Army major general, 2,188,657 votes to 1,378,325. That is a 22.7-point margin, almost 17 points wider than the Democrats' 5.8-point win in Virginia in the 2024 presidential race. The Watch had not yet read the Federal Election Commission data for Virginia when this was written. The federal dollar figures below come from the nonpartisan Virginia Public Access Project (VPAP) and from local reporting. There was no governor's race this year, because Virginia elected Governor Abigail Spanberger1 in 2025.

Where the money came from

Nearly all of the Senate money belonged to the candidates. Warner finished the race with a fundraising total of $27,493,348, according to the Virginia Public Access Project, while Mizusawa raised $1,097,119.2 A large share of Warner's donors lived outside the state. The Virginia Center for Investigative Journalism at WHRO looked at his itemized donations and found $2.53 million from Virginia and $3.77 million from identifiable locations elsewhere, putting his outside-Virginia share at about 60%3. New York and California3 together supplied almost half of that out-of-state money.

Super PACs largely skipped the race. VoteLog, a tracker that compiles FEC filings, counted $31,518 of independent expenditures4 after the August primaries, all of it supporting Warner. Stephen Farnsworth, a political scientist quoted by WTOP, said "The Republicans are spending their money outside of Virginia, for the most part"5.

The year's biggest money in Virginia came in the spring. Democrats put a constitutional amendment on the April 21 ballot that would have let the legislature redraw the state's congressional districts in the middle of the decade. That vote became the most expensive referendum in Virginia's history, garnering roughly $83.2 million between the two sides, according to the Virginia Public Access Project.6 Most of the "yes" money came from House Majority Forward, a House Democratic-aligned nonprofit.7 Ballotpedia's tally of the state filings also lists The Fairness Project8 at about $10 million and George Soros's Fund for Policy Reform at $5 million.

Both sides relied on "dark money," the legal term for political spending by nonprofits that do not have to name their donors. According to VPAP, as reported by Cardinal News, the "yes" side got more than three times as much as the "no" side in untraceable dollars from 501(c)(4) groups6. On the "no" side, VPAP's records show the Virginians for Fair Maps committee took in $24,456,5859, and $23,541,000 of that came from a 501(c)(4) nonprofit with the same name. Brendan Glavin of OpenSecrets told Cardinal News the two are likely two separate legal entities with the same name, with one passing money to the other10.

The ads

Warner's ads were positive and statewide. WAVY-TV reported that he had been on TV since Sept. 1 talking about what he would do with a fourth term11 and that he doesn't even mention he has an opponent.11 The spots asked voters to "rehire" him, as he says he seeks to lower costs for everyday Americans and address issues related to artificial intelligence.5 The WHRO center's review of FCC filings found that Warner has spent over $7 million in ads12. On Google, Rep. Eugene Vindman of the 7th District spent the most of any candidate, more than $700,00013, and Warner followed at $522,50013. As of early October, Mizusawa has not put his own ads on TV.11 His campaign said Warner "does little more than videos from his car."11

The referendum ads were national in tone. In one ad for Virginians for Fair Elections, former President Barack Obama said "Republicans want to steal enough seats in Congress to rig the next election and wield unchecked power for two more years,"14 By April 17, AdImpact had counted $48.2 million15 in ad spending by that group and $14.1 million by Virginians for Fair Maps.

In the fall, the heavy ad spending was in Hampton Roads. Portsmouth-based WAVY-TV led the outlet rankings with $14.35 million in ad orders.16 In the 2nd District, VPAP figures reported by 13News Now show groups supporting Luria and opposing Kiggans spent more than $9 million on television advertising. Groups supporting Kiggans and opposing Luria spent more than $10.2 million.17 In the campaign's final week, 13News Now counted 557 individual ads for Luria and Kiggans17 on three local stations. Totals vary with what is counted: VPAP tallied more than $77 million17 in political advertising for the midterms, while the WHRO center's FCC review found more than $82 million.

Did it buy anything?

In the Senate race, the candidate who spent far more won by a wide margin. That is a hard result to credit to money, though. The nonpartisan Cook Political Report rates the Senate seat as solidly Democratic.18, and Democrats gained across the state.

The House races show the money didn't decide things on its own. In the 2nd District, outside TV spending was close to even, with Kiggans's side slightly ahead. Luria's campaign has spent $7.7 million, topping Kiggan's $5.2 million, according to the most recent data collected by VPAP.19 Luria led 53.06%20 to 44.56% in unofficial returns. In the 1st District, Wittman has spent $6.7 million, while his challenger, Democrat Shannon Taylor, has spent $1.4 million, according to VPAP ad spending data.13 Taylor won anyway: unofficial Virginia Department of Elections results reported Wednesday showed Taylor with 207,877 votes, or 51%, to Wittman's 198,417, or 48.7%.21 The results have not been certified.21

The referendum's $83 million won at the ballot box but bought no new map. The "yes" side outspent the opposition about three to one, and the measure passed by 2.7 percentage points6. Then the Virginia Supreme Court threw out the result in a 4-3 decision1. It ruled that the congressional district maps issued by this Court in 20211 would govern the 2026 elections. Democrats won the 1st and 2nd districts on those old lines.
