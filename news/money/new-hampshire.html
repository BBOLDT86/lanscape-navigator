The money · race by race · New Hampshire

In New Hampshire, Ayotte's money edge held; Sununu's outside help didn't

Gov. Kelly Ayotte raised about five times as much as Cinde Warmington and won by four points while Democrats won down the ballot. In the Senate race, Republican groups outspent Democratic ones, and Chris Pappas won by nearly 14.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 13, 2026. 17 sources. All the states › · How New Hampshire voted ›

New Hampshire voters split their tickets on Nov. 3. Democrat Chris Pappas won the open U.S. Senate seat over Republican John E. Sununu by 13.7 points, 355,720 votes to 268,340, according to AP results published by The New York Times. On the same ballot, Republican Gov. Kelly Ayotte beat Democrat Cinde Warmington by 4.3 points, 324,410 to 297,339. In the governor's race, the side with more money won. In the Senate race, the side with more outside money lost.

The Watch's Federal Election Commission data for New Hampshire had not been processed when this was published. The Senate figures below come from news reports and are credited to them.

Where the money came from

The governor's race is a state race, so its money is reported to the New Hampshire secretary of state, not the FEC. According to The New Hampshire Union Leader's review, Ayotte raised $9 million.1 The Live Free or Die PAC controlled by the Republican Governors Association gave Ayotte $2 million, more than the $1.8 million in total cash Warmington had collected for her entire campaign.1 Help from the Democratic side looked different from 2024. That year, the Democratic Governors Association gave Craig nearly $10 million2, referring to Joyce Craig, the party's nominee. This year, according to the Union Leader, with the DGA having to juggle State House corner office races in 36 states, the cupboard was bare for Warmington.2 Her campaign said in June that almost all of Warmington's financial support is coming from inside New Hampshire, and that most of her campaign donors are giving less than $100.3 On Oct. 21, Warmington loaned the campaign $117,800 so she could buy more TV ads costing that same amount.1

The Senate money ran the other way. The Concord Monitor reported that Pappas raised $23.7 million compared to Sununu's $5.68 million4. It also reported that PACs and super PACs have put a combined $57 million into this Senate race4. The Union Leader put the Republican side's edge in outside money at 60-40 and wrote that the margin was a lopsided 3-1 Sununu until Senate Democrats moved lately to even the score.5 On the Republican side, the conservative Senate Leadership Fund announced $17 million in spending for Sununu, the Trump-backed No Going Back PAC is ready with $10 million in ads, and the Koch-minded Americans for Prosperity Action is already on the record with more than $2 million.6 No Going Back PAC is a super PAC registered with the FEC on Sept. 17. It shares a treasurer, address and phone number with MAGA Inc., Trump's main super PAC.7 Elon Musk's America PAC spent $755,000 on digital media, printing, phone calls and texts opposing Democratic Rep. Chris Pappas and $265,000 supporting Republican John E. Sununu4. The largest group on the Democratic side was WinSenate, which has spent around $16.7 million to help Pappas get elected.8

What the ads said

The governor's race was fought over one issue. Ayotte's first ad, in June, used video from former Manchester Mayor Joyce Craig's campaign for governor in 2024 that tried to brand Democratic contender Cinde Warmington as an unelectable former opioid lobbyist.9 It was set to run on WMUR-TV and on digital platforms for a week9, and the TV buy of $10,000 is relatively small9. The New Hampshire Republican Party, a party committee, ran ads about Warmington's testimony on behalf of Purdue Pharma, where she described OxyContin as a "miracle drug" with "very few side effects"10. PolitiFact found that Ayotte overstated the length of Warmington's involvement with Purdue Pharma.11 Warmington's first TV ad came in October. It expresses regret over her Purdue Pharma work while shifting focus to Gov.12 Ayotte, on costs. In the closing days, Warmington did one thing Ayotte never did in this campaign, buying ads on the very expensive Boston TV market1.

Most of the Senate ads were attacks on Pappas. NHPR reported that these included allegations that he voted in ways that benefitted companies tied to his husband, who has worked as a lobbyist for Amazon and is now employed by Uber.13 No Going Back PAC also sent mailers, NPR reported, claiming Pappas sold out to Big Insurance because he backed extending premium subsidies for Obamacare.14 Americans for Prosperity Action unveiled big digital and streaming ad buys with twin spots15. WinSenate's ad said Sununu took a job at Akin Gump, one of the largest lobbying firms in the world16, after he lost his Senate seat in 2008.

Did the money work?

In the governor's race, the better-funded candidate won. Warmington said she was heartened that this was such a close race despite that we were outspent by more than 6 to 12. The New Hampshire Bulletin wrote that her message on costs failed to overcome a simple, memorable and devastating label levied by Ayotte and other New Hampshire Republicans: "Cinde Warmington: Opioid Lobbyist."17 Ayotte still ran well behind her party's usual strength in a bad Republican year. She won while Democrats swept New Hampshire's Legislature and Executive Council1. And she finished 18 points of margin behind Pappas's showing at the top of the ballot.

In the Senate race, the side with more outside money lost, and by a lot. Pappas's 13.7-point margin was 10.9 points better than the Democrats' 2024 presidential margin in the state. But the Republicans' outside money went to a candidate whose own campaign raised about a quarter of what his opponent's did. Counting campaigns and outside groups together, the two sides were closer to even than the outside-spending totals alone suggest. Final post-election FEC reports will settle the full totals.
