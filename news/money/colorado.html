The money · race by race · Colorado

In Colorado, the $11 million fight came in June, not November

A super PAC funded mostly by Michael Bloomberg spent about $11 million trying to stop Phil Weiser in the Democratic primary, and lost. By fall, his Republican opponent's campaign never bought TV time, and Weiser won by nearly 28 points.

The most expensive fight over who would govern Colorado ended on June 30, four months before the general election. On Nov. 3, Attorney General Phil Weiser, a Democrat, beat Republican Victor Marx 1,610,582 votes to 879,473. That margin of 27.7 points ran 16.7 points ahead of the Democratic presidential ticket's 11-point win in Colorado in 2024. The Associated Press declared Weiser the winner just 1 minute after polls closed at 7pm.1 On the same ballot, U.S. Sen. John Hickenlooper, a Democrat, beat state Sen. Mark Baisley, a Republican, by 25.3 points.

The Federal Election Commission data The Watch reviewed showed no outside spending in any Colorado federal race. So this report follows the race for governor, a state contest, using Colorado secretary of state filings as reported by the local press.

Where the money came from

Most of the outside money came in the primary, and most of it went against Weiser. Rocky Mountain Way, a super PAC backing U.S. Sen. Michael Bennet, raised and spent about $11 million, including more than $5.1 million from former New York Mayor Michael Bloomberg.2 (Colorado law calls super PACs independent expenditure committees.) Its next-largest donor, according to The Colorado Sun, was Brighter Future for Colorado, a 501(c)(4)3, which gave $1.25 million. That is a nonprofit that does not have to disclose its own donors, which is what the term "dark money" means. The super PAC also received $825,000 from the Colorado Trial Lawyers Association, $50,000 from Uber, $50,000 from Denver-based dialysis company DaVita, $25,000 from health insurer Cigna4.

Weiser raised a record $6.7 million en route to his primary win over U.S. Sen. Michael Bennet in June, overcoming a major deficit as he faced over $17 million in combined spending5 by Bennet's campaign and the super PAC, Colorado Newsline reported. Fighting for Colorado, the super PAC backing Weiser, raised and spent about $1.4 million.6 Its top donors were Jay Monroe, Jason Mendelson and Jim Kelley, all venture capitalists who have contributed a total of $100,000 each.7

On the Republican side, the Freedom Independent Expenditure Committee backed Marx. One of Freedom IEC's largest donors is Sema Construction, Inc., a construction firm based in Centennial, which has donated $435,000.4 After the primary, that money mostly stopped. In the period ending July 27, Fighting for Colorado took in $258,790, while the Freedom Independent Expenditure Committee, which has spent on Marx's behalf, collected just $31,600.5 In an Axios analysis, Republicans running for Colorado's four statewide offices have raised just $5 million, compared with $12.8 million for Democrats8.

The ads

Weiser's campaign went on the air first. On April 15 it launched "Fearless," a 30-second spot about his winning record standing up to the lawless, bullying Trump administration and big corporations that broke the law.9 The campaign said the ad would run on cable, broadcast, digital and streaming. Even so, Bennet's campaign and allied outside groups have outspent the pro-Weiser camp on ads by almost 2-to-1, according to the ad-tracking firm AdImpact.10

The super PAC's money paid for attacks on Weiser in TV and digital ads, as well as in mailers.2 One spot, "Colorado's Watchdog," began airing June 13, according to AdImpact. It said that after oil and gas companies helped elect Weiser, he rolled over11 and failed to join lawsuits against the Trump administration. Other ads from Bennet and the super PAC accused Weiser of a "pay-to-play" scheme tied to donors to an association of attorneys general he once chaired. Colorado Newsline reported that those ads resurfaced four-year-old accusations that originated with the Public Trust Institute, a "dark money" nonprofit with ties to a conservative advocacy network12.

The general election was much quieter. In September, Weiser's campaign released "Plan," which promised homes that first-time buyers could afford. It ran statewide on streaming platforms and on cable and broadcast TV in Grand Junction and Colorado Springs13.

Marx did not answer with TV ads. A CPR News investigation found that more than 36% of Marx's campaign spending — about $1.4 million — is classified as fundraising expenses.14 By comparison, among Colorado campaigns that spent at least $200,000, the median share of spending on advertising is about 32%.14 In the last days of the race, Marx sent fundraising emails asking for $100,000 for his first TV ads, The Colorado Sun reported. But Federal Communications Commission records show Marx's campaign never bought any TV ad time.15 His campaign was also fined $57,647.16 for 210 violations — is believed to be the largest in Colorado history.16 The fine was for accepting donations over the legal limit, and the campaign said it would appeal.

Did the money buy the seat?

In the primary, it did not. The side that spent more lost: with more than 80% of the expected vote counted, Weiser led Bennet by 10 percentage points.10

In November, the side with more money won by a wide margin. Weiser collected $9.3 million through Monday1, according to Axios, while Marx raised $4.4 million but never ran a single campaign television ad.1 It is hard to separate the money from everything else in the race, though. The state has not elected a Republican governor since 200217. Several high-profile Republicans refused to endorse Marx, including both of his primary opponents.18 Former state party chair Ken Buck endorsed Weiser. After the election, CPR News reported that Marx carried significant baggage with him into the campaign, which many party insiders believed hurt the whole ticket.19

The Senate race was hardly contested on the airwaves. KUNC reported that Baisley received effectively no help from national Republicans.20 At the same time, more competitive Senate races this year, like the ones in Maine and Ohio, drew hundreds of millions of dollars in spending.20
