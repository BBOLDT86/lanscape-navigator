In Michigan, the bigger spender won one race and lost the other

Jocelyn Benson's allies far outspent John James in the governor's race. Republicans outspent Abdul El-Sayed in the record Senate race. Democrats won both, by margins far wider than Michigan's 2024 presidential vote.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026; FEC data read Nov. 12, 2026. 20 sources. All the states › · How Michigan voted ›

Michigan voters got two different money stories on the same Nov. 3 ballot. In the race for governor, Democrat Jocelyn Benson and her allies far outspent Republican John James. In the open U.S. Senate race, it went the other way: Republican Mike Rogers and the groups backing him outspent Democrat Abdul El-Sayed. Democrats won both. Benson won by 10.8 points, 2,613,747 votes to 2,094,689. El-Sayed won by 7.1 points, 2,520,388 to 2,180,541. Those are AP results. Donald Trump carried Michigan by 1.4 points in 2024.

A note on sources: The Watch's own read of Federal Election Commission data for Michigan wasn't finished when this story was written. The Senate figures below come from news outlets' counts of FEC filings and from the ad tracker AdImpact. The governor's race is a state race, so its figures come from state filings as Michigan reporters described them.

Where the money came from

By late October, Bridge Michigan had counted about $38.8 million in disclosed spending in Benson's favor, either by her campaign or outside groups supporting her, while there has been about $26.7 million in reported spending for James.1 The biggest group on Benson's side was Making Michigan's Future, a tax-exempt political organization organized under Section 527 of IRS code1. It reported more than $18.3 million in ad spending. The majority of the funding, nearly $7.8 million, came from the Democratic Governors Association1, and billionaire former New York Mayor Michael Bloomberg gave $1 million1. After the election, the Democratic Governors Association put the group's total at $24 million2.

On James's side, one family dominated. Five members of the DeVos family have contributed more than $12 million to three different political action committees supporting James, including $2 million to the new Michigan Victory 2026 committee that is funding James ads through the state Republican Party.1 Elon Musk's America PAC, a super PAC, has reported spending more than $1.2 million supporting James since September and is funded exclusively by Musk.1 James's family company, the James Group International, donated $625,0003 to Mission Michigan, the super PAC backing him.

The national Republican money pulled back. The Republican Governors Association's Michigan Victory Fund was averaging $1.58 million a week in paid media4 from Aug. 18 to Sept. 29. By the week of Oct. 6 it was spending $187,000, leaving Democrat Jocelyn Benson and her allies with $3.79 million on the air for the week, essentially uncontested4, according to the Lansing newsletter MIRS. Late in the race, a $1.8 million ad buy from the Michigan Republican Party5 helped James. Democrats filed a complaint, arguing the buy exceeds state contribution limits in what appears to be a test of Michigan law5 after a U.S. Supreme Court ruling on party spending.

The Senate race was bigger and tilted the other way. The Detroit News, tallying FEC filings, found that since the Aug. 4 primary outside independent expenditure groups have spent more than $170 million on pro-Rogers and anti-Abdul ads.6 Groups aligned with El-Sayed have spent almost $95 million during that time.6 The Senate Leadership Fund, the super PAC aligned with Senate Republican Leader John Thune, said Oct. 5 that it would add $2 million to its planned spend to boost Rogers in Michigan, bringing the total to $53 million.7 El-Sayed's own campaign raised more than Rogers's: $53.8 million as of Oct. 15, which beats Rogers by $30.9 million.6

What the ads said

Making Michigan's Future ran its first TV ad on Sept. 9. It said that in Congress, "James made 145 stock trades and even broke the law by failing to disclose his trades."8 Later online ads claims James voted for $70 billion in tax breaks for data centers9, and a second ad ran on Facebook and Instagram that highlights political contributions from data center developers to James.9 The group also spent about $2.2 million on ads running on Google-owned platforms, nearly all of which are video ads aired on YouTube.1

The RGA's "More Failure" ad said Michigan Democrats have controlled every statewide office10 for eight years. MIRS reported it ran on broadcast TV in the Grand Rapids-Kalamazoo-Battle Creek DMA and Detroit DMA11 and on cable in Lansing and Flint. Mission Michigan's ads went after Benson's husband, Ryan Friedrichs, and his work for the developer of a data center in Saline Township. Bridge Michigan's fact check found no evidence Benson's husband could make millions of dollars on the Saline Township data center, as the attack ad claims.12 It also found that Benson's denial left out his $370,000 salary in 202513.

In the Senate race, PolitiFact reported that one recent Senate Leadership Fund ad said El-Sayed wants to introduce shariah — or Islamic law — to the government, a claim we've rated Pants on Fire!14 El-Sayed's ads blame Rogers for high pharmaceutical costs and say he made millions after leaving Congress by advising large corporations.14 Musk's PAC put about $3.9 million to back Rogers, mostly through online ads and political mail.15

Did it work?

In the governor's race, the side that spent more won. James said in October that Michigan voters were tuning out negative ads16. The RGA pulled out as four different polling-based models show Benson leading by 7 to 8 percentage points.17 Pollster Richard Czuba called the withdrawal "a signal of exactly where this race is for governor"17.

In the Senate race, the side that spent more lost. The Detroit News reported that Rogers and outside groups backing him outspent El-Sayed and his allies by roughly 3:2.18 El-Sayed told supporters that "in a battle between the money and the many that the many can win."19 Bridge Michigan pointed to a bigger force, writing that he was buoyed by favorable national tailwinds20.

Both Democrats did much better than their party did in 2024. Benson moved the state 12.2 points from Trump's margin, and El-Sayed moved it 8.5. The bigger swing came in the race where Democrats outspent Republicans. The smaller one came where they were outspent. Readers can decide how much of that the money explains.
