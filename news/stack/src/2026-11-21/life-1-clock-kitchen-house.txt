=== life | The body clock: light, timing and jet lag | Sleep
> A clock in your brain sets when you feel sleepy and alert, and light is what sets the clock. Knowing when to seek light and when to avoid it fixes more than willpower does.
# A clock in the brain
Nearly every process in your body follows a daily cycle: sleepiness, body temperature, hormone levels, digestion, even reaction time. The cycles are coordinated by a small cluster of nerve cells in the hypothalamus, deep in the brain, sitting just above the point where the nerves from the two eyes cross. This is the master clock. It keeps time by itself. People kept in constant dim conditions with no clues to the hour still sleep and wake on a regular cycle, which is why the rhythm is called circadian, meaning about a day.
# It runs slightly slow
Left to itself, the human clock does not run at exactly 24 hours. Careful laboratory measurements put the average a little longer, at about 24 hours and 10 minutes, with variation from person to person. Without correction you would drift later every day. So the clock has to be reset daily, and the signal that resets it is light. Special cells in the retina, separate from the ones used for seeing shapes, report the overall brightness to the clock. They respond most strongly to blue light, which daylight is rich in.
# Timing is everything
The same light does opposite things depending on when it arrives. Light in the hours after your usual waking time pulls the clock earlier, so that you feel sleepy sooner that night. Light in the evening and the first half of the night pushes the clock later. This is why bright screens and bright rooms late at night make it harder to fall asleep, and why a lie-in with the curtains shut lets the clock slide. Morning light is the most useful single tool for keeping the clock steady.
# The evening signal
As your usual bedtime approaches, a gland in the brain releases melatonin, a hormone that tells the body night has begun. The rise starts roughly two hours before you normally fall asleep, provided the light is dim. Bright light suppresses it. Body temperature follows its own curve, falling through the night to a low point a couple of hours before your usual waking time. That low point is when alertness is at its worst, which is why the small hours of a night shift feel so hard.
# Larks and owls
People differ in when their clocks are set, partly by inheritance. Some are naturally early and some late, and most fall in between. Age shifts it. In the teenage years the clock moves markedly later, which is biology and not laziness, and it drifts earlier again through adult life. Living against your own timing, for instance a late type who must rise at five for work and sleeps in at weekends, produces a mild and permanent jet lag. Keeping the same waking time all week reduces it.
# Jet lag
After a long flight, your clock is still on home time, and it shifts by only about an hour a day. Travelling east is usually harder than travelling west. Going west asks you to stay up later, which the slightly slow clock is inclined to do anyway. Going east asks you to fall asleep earlier, against its drift. The remedy is timed light. After flying east, get outdoor light in the local morning and keep the evening dim. After flying west, get light in the local late afternoon and evening. Across eight or more time zones the right timing is less obvious, and a jet lag planner can work it out for your flights.
# Daily practice
Four habits keep the clock in order. Get outside within an hour or so of waking, even on a grey day, since outdoor light is many times brighter than indoor light. Keep your waking time steady, weekends included. Dim the lights and screens in the last hour or two of the evening. And keep the bedroom dark. If you sleep badly for weeks despite this, feel sleepy in the daytime, or work shifts and are struggling, talk to a clinician, since sleep problems have many causes worth checking.

=== life | Salt: when to add it and why | Food and cooking technique
> Salt does more than make food salty. Added at the right moment, it changes how food holds water, how it browns and how every other flavour comes across.
# What salt does to taste
Salt is one of the five basic tastes, and it also changes how you perceive the others. It suppresses bitterness, which is why a pinch improves coffee that has stewed, grapefruit and dark greens. With bitterness turned down, sweetness and aroma come forward. Food cooked without enough salt does not taste merely unsalted. It tastes flat, as though something were missing that you cannot name. The aim of seasoning is the point at which food tastes fully of itself, just short of tasting salty.
# Salting meat ahead
Sprinkle salt on a piece of meat and watch. Within a few minutes, beads of moisture appear, drawn out by the salt. If you cook it now, that wet surface steams and browns poorly. Wait longer and something else happens. The salt dissolves into the moisture, and over the next half hour or more that brine is drawn back in, carrying salt below the surface. So there are two good moments to salt a steak or a chop. Either immediately before it goes in the pan, or at least 45 minutes ahead. A whole chicken benefits from a day, uncovered in the fridge.
# Water for pasta and vegetables
Pasta and potatoes absorb water as they cook, and it is the only chance to season them all the way through. Salt added afterwards sits on the surface. A workable amount for pasta is about 10 grams of salt per litre of water, roughly two level teaspoons of fine salt. The water should taste pleasantly seasoned, like a mild soup. For green vegetables, well salted boiling water seasons them and helps them cook quickly enough to stay bright. Most of that salt goes down the drain with the water.
# Drawing water out on purpose
The same pull that wets the surface of a steak is useful elsewhere. Toss sliced cucumber or shredded cabbage with salt and leave it for 20 minutes, then squeeze, and you get a salad or a slaw that stays crisp and does not flood the dressing. Salted aubergine slices give up water and absorb less oil when fried. Salting chopped tomatoes concentrates their flavour and gives you a seasoned juice to use in the dressing. In each case, rinse or reduce the salt elsewhere so the dish is not overdone.
# A teaspoon is not a teaspoon
Salt varies greatly in how tightly it packs. Fine table salt is dense. Flaky kosher salt and sea salt flakes are light and full of air. A level teaspoon of table salt can contain nearly twice as much salt by weight as a teaspoon of some flaky salts. This is the commonest reason a recipe turns out too salty or too bland. Know which salt a recipe means, stick to one kind in your kitchen so your hand learns it, and for baking, brining and curing, weigh it.
# Season in stages
Adding all the salt at the end gives a salty surface over a bland interior. Adding a little at each stage builds flavour through the dish. Salt the onions as they soften, which also draws out moisture and helps them cook evenly. Salt the meat before browning. Season the liquid when it goes in. Then taste and adjust at the end. Remember that liquids reduce. A stock that is correctly seasoned at the start will be too salty when boiled down by half, so season reductions late.
# Fixing and judging
If a dish tastes dull, try a pinch of salt in a spoonful before adding it to the pot. If it tastes right but heavy, it may need acid, a squeeze of lemon or a splash of vinegar, and not more salt. If you have oversalted, dilute with more unsalted ingredients, since a potato dropped into the pot does little. Taste as you go, every time. People who have been advised by a clinician to limit salt should follow that advice, and acid, herbs and spices carry more of the load.

=== life | Eggs: what heat does to them | Kitchen science
> An egg goes from liquid to solid over a span of about twenty degrees. Knowing which part sets at which temperature explains every way of cooking one.
# Proteins unfolding
An egg white is about nine tenths water, with proteins folded into tight bundles floating in it. Heat shakes those bundles until they unfold, and the loose strands then link to one another, forming a mesh that traps the water. That is the change from clear liquid to white solid. Keep heating and the mesh tightens and squeezes water out, which is why an overcooked egg is rubbery and a pan of overcooked scrambled eggs weeps. Cooking eggs well means stopping at the tender stage of that process.
# Two parts, two temperatures
The white and the yolk contain different proteins that set at different temperatures. The white begins to thicken at about 62 degrees Celsius and becomes firm by about 80. The yolk begins to thicken at about 65 degrees and sets to a solid at about 70. Those few degrees are the whole game. An egg held at 63 degrees for a long time has a barely set white and a thick, liquid yolk. One taken to 75 has a firm yolk and a tender white.
# Boiling is a race
In a pan of boiling water, heat travels from the outside in. The white, on the outside, reaches its setting temperature first, and the yolk at the centre lags behind. A soft-boiled egg is the moment when the white has set and the yolk has not caught up. For a large egg taken from the fridge and lowered into boiling water, that is about six minutes for a runny yolk, eight for a soft, jammy one and eleven or so for fully firm. Times vary with size and starting temperature, so test with one egg first.
# The green ring
A grey-green layer around the yolk of a hard-boiled egg is a sign of overcooking. Prolonged heat releases sulphur from the white, and it reacts with iron in the yolk where the two meet. The compound is harmless. It comes with a dry, crumbly yolk and a sulphurous smell. To avoid it, cook the eggs no longer than needed and cool them at once in cold water, which stops the heat still travelling inwards and also makes them easier to handle.
# Peeling
Very fresh eggs are hard to peel cleanly, because the white clings to the membrane inside the shell. As an egg ages over a week or two, it loses carbon dioxide through the shell and becomes less acidic, and the membrane lets go more readily. So eggs that have been in the fridge for a while are the better choice for boiling. Starting them in water that is already boiling, or steaming them, also tends to help. Crack the shell all over and peel under running water, starting from the wide end where the air pocket sits.
# Scrambles and custards
Stirring eggs while they heat breaks the mesh into small curds as it forms. Low heat and patient stirring give small, creamy curds. High heat gives large, firm ones in seconds, and a moment too long gives dry ones. Take the pan off while the eggs still look slightly wet, since they carry on cooking. Adding milk, cream or sugar spreads the proteins further apart and raises the temperature at which they set. That is why a custard thickens at around 80 degrees and curdles if it boils.
# Safety
Raw and lightly cooked eggs can carry salmonella, a cause of food poisoning. The risk from any one egg is small, and it is not zero. Mainstream guidance is that young children, pregnant women, older people and anyone with a weakened immune system should eat eggs cooked until both white and yolk are firm, or use pasteurised eggs for dishes that are not fully cooked. Keep eggs in the fridge where local guidance says to, and do not leave egg dishes sitting at room temperature for hours.

=== life | The water heater: the tank in the cupboard | House systems
> A storage water heater is a simple machine with two safety parts that matter a great deal. Ten minutes of attention a year extends its life.
# How the tank works
A storage water heater is an insulated steel tank, typically holding 40 to 50 American gallons in a family house. Cold water enters through a tube that runs down inside to the bottom. Hot water, which rises, is drawn off from the top. A thermostat watches the temperature. When it drops, a gas burner under the tank or electric elements inside it switch on. Because the tank stays hot all day whether or not anyone uses it, some energy is always leaking away through the walls. That standing loss is the price of having hot water ready.
# The relief valve
On the top or side of the tank is a brass valve with a small lever and a pipe running down towards the floor. This is the temperature and pressure relief valve, and it is the most important safety part in the house. Water heated in a sealed tank expands. If a thermostat failed and the burner stayed on, pressure would build until the tank burst. The valve opens before that can happen. Its discharge pipe must never be capped, plugged or blocked. If it drips or releases water, have a plumber find out why.
# The anode rod
Steel and hot water make rust. The tank is lined with glass, but the lining always has small flaws. To protect the steel, a rod of magnesium or aluminium hangs inside the tank. That metal corrodes more readily than steel, so the corrosion attacks the rod and spares the tank. It is called a sacrificial anode, and it is used up over a period of years. Once it is gone, the tank itself starts to rust. Checking the rod every few years and replacing it when it is mostly eaten away can add years to a heater.
# Sediment
Minerals dissolved in the water settle out when it is heated and collect at the bottom of the tank. In a gas heater the layer sits between the burner and the water, so the heater works harder and may rumble or pop as steam bubbles force their way through. In an electric heater it can bury and burn out the lower element. Draining a few gallons from the valve at the bottom once a year flushes much of it out. Hard water areas build sediment faster.
# The temperature setting
The United States Department of Energy suggests a setting of 120 degrees Fahrenheit, about 49 Celsius, for most households. At that temperature a scald takes minutes of contact. At 140 Fahrenheit it takes seconds, which matters most for small children and older people. A lower setting also wastes less heat. There is a trade-off, since bacteria survive more easily in cooler stored water, and some households are advised to store hotter and mix cold in at the outlet. If someone in the home has a weakened immune system, ask a professional what is right.
# Signs of trouble
Tank heaters commonly last somewhere around 8 to 12 years, less with hard water and no maintenance. Watch for rusty hot water, which suggests the tank is corroding. Watch for moisture or a rust trail at the base, and for hot water that runs out sooner than it used to. A tank that has begun to leak from its body cannot be repaired. Find the date of manufacture on the label, and if the heater is past ten years, plan for replacement before it fails on a cold morning.
# What to do and what to leave
A homeowner can check the setting, look at the relief valve's pipe, drain sediment following the maker's instructions, and learn where the cold water shut-off and the gas or power shut-off are. Leave the rest to a licensed plumber: anything on a gas line or flue, replacing the relief valve, electrical work on the elements, and installing a new heater. If you ever smell gas, do not operate switches. Leave the building and call the gas company from outside.

=== life | Fixing a running toilet | Repairs a homeowner can do
> A toilet that hisses or refills by itself is wasting water around the clock. The cause is nearly always one of two cheap parts in the tank.
# What is in the tank
Lift the lid of the tank and set it somewhere safe, since it is heavy and brittle. The water in the tank is clean. You will see four things. A rubber flap at the bottom covers the outlet to the bowl. This is the flapper. A chain links it to the flush handle. A tall valve at one side refills the tank after a flush, controlled by a float that rises with the water. And an open vertical pipe, the overflow tube, stands in the middle to carry away water if the tank overfills.
# How a flush works
Pressing the handle lifts the flapper by its chain. The tank's water rushes down into the bowl and pushes the contents over the bend in the drain. As the tank empties, the flapper drops back onto its seat and seals. The float has fallen with the water, which opens the fill valve, and the tank refills until the float rises far enough to shut the valve. A toilet runs on when one of those two seals fails. Either the flapper is letting water out, or the fill valve is not shutting off.
# Finding which one
Look at the water level first. If water is spilling into the top of the overflow tube, the fill valve is the problem, since it is filling too high or not closing. If the level sits below the top of the tube, test the flapper. Put a few drops of food colouring in the tank and do not flush for fifteen minutes. If colour shows up in the bowl, water is leaking past the flapper. A toilet that refills briefly every so often with nobody near it is doing this.
# Replacing the flapper
Turn off the water at the small valve on the wall or floor behind the toilet, then flush to empty the tank. Unhook the chain from the handle arm and unclip the flapper from the pegs on the overflow tube. Take it with you to the hardware shop, because flappers come in two common sizes and several fittings. Before fitting the new one, wipe the rim it sits on to remove any slime or mineral crust. Clip it on, hook the chain, turn the water on and test.
# Getting the chain right
The chain needs a little slack, about a centimetre. Too tight and it holds the flapper slightly open, so the toilet runs. Too loose and the handle will not lift the flapper far enough for a full flush, or the spare chain can fall under the flapper and stop it sealing. Trim off any long tail of chain. Flush several times and watch the flapper drop and seat each time. This small adjustment cures many toilets on its own.
# The fill valve
If the water was running into the overflow tube, lower the water level. Most modern fill valves have a screw or a sliding clip on the float for this. Set the level about 2 or 3 centimetres below the top of the tube. If the valve will not shut off at any setting, or hisses constantly, replace it. With the water off and the tank empty, undo the supply connection and the large nut under the tank, lift the old valve out and fit the new one following its instructions. Tighten plastic nuts by hand.
# When to call a plumber
These repairs need no special tools and the parts are inexpensive. Some problems are not for a first attempt. Water appearing around the base of the toilet points to the seal between the toilet and the drain. A crack in the tank or bowl means replacement. And if the shut-off valve on the wall will not turn, or drips once you have turned it, leave it and have it replaced, since forcing an old valve can start a leak you cannot stop without closing the main.

=== life | Changing a flat tyre safely | Car care
> The job takes fifteen minutes with the tools already in the car. Most of the danger comes from where you stop and from the jack, so those come first.
# Get somewhere safe
A flat tyre does not have to be changed where it happened. Slow down, put your hazard lights on, and drive gently to a place that is flat, firm and well away from moving traffic, such as a car park or a quiet side road. Driving a short distance on a flat may ruin the tyre, and that is cheaper than being struck by a passing vehicle. On the shoulder of a fast road, the safer choice is often to get everyone out and behind the barrier, and call for roadside help.
# Set up before you lift
Apply the parking brake firmly. Put an automatic in park and a manual in first gear or reverse. If you have a wedge, a brick or a large stone, place it against the wheel diagonally opposite the flat. Get passengers out of the car and off the road. Then find the spare wheel, the jack and the wheel wrench, which are usually under the boot floor. If the car has locking wheel nuts, you will need the special key, often kept with the jack or in the glovebox.
# Loosen first, then lift
Loosen the wheel nuts before raising the car, while the tyre's grip on the ground stops the wheel from turning. Give each nut about half a turn anticlockwise. They can be very tight, and using your foot on the wrench is acceptable. Then find the jacking point nearest the flat. The handbook shows it, and there is often a notch or mark on the seam under the sill. A jack placed anywhere else can bend the floor or slip. Raise the car until the flat tyre is just clear of the ground.
# Never get under it
The jack supplied with a car is designed only to hold it up long enough to change a wheel. It can tip or sink into soft ground. Do not put any part of your body under the car while it is on the jack. A simple precaution is to slide the spare wheel under the sill beside the jack before you take the flat off, and then slide the flat there in its place. If the jack fails, the car lands on the wheel and not on the brake disc or on you.
# Swap the wheel
Remove the loosened nuts and keep them together where they cannot roll away. Pull the wheel straight towards you and off. Lift the spare onto the hub, lining up the holes, and screw the nuts on by hand until they are snug. Tighten them in a criss-cross order, moving to the nut opposite each time and not around the circle. This draws the wheel on evenly. Do not apply full force yet, since pushing hard on a raised car can rock it off the jack.
# Lower and tighten
Lower the car until the tyre is carrying weight, then tighten the nuts firmly with the wrench, again in the criss-cross order. Go around twice. Stow the flat, the jack and the tools so they cannot fly about. The handbook gives a tightening figure for the nuts, and a garage can check it with a torque wrench. It is widely advised to recheck the nuts after the first 50 miles or so, because they can settle.
# After the change
Many spares are narrow temporary wheels. They carry a speed limit, commonly 50 miles an hour, printed on the wheel, and they are meant to get you to a tyre shop and no further. Check the spare's pressure a few times a year, since a spare is often found flat on the day it is needed. Some cars carry no spare at all, only a bottle of sealant and a compressor. That kit can seal a small hole in the tread and will not help with a damaged sidewall, so know which your car has before you need it.
