=== money | APR and APY: two ways to quote one rate | Personal finance mechanics
> Lenders quote the rate that looks smaller and savings accounts quote the one that looks bigger. Both are honest. Here is how to convert between them.
# Two labels
In the United States, loans and credit cards are advertised with an annual percentage rate, or APR. Savings accounts and certificates of deposit are advertised with an annual percentage yield, or APY. The difference is compounding. APR is the yearly rate before interest is charged on interest. APY is what you actually pay or earn over a year once compounding within the year is counted. For the same account, APY is always the larger number, unless interest is added only once a year, in which case the two are equal.
# How the gap arises
Take a credit card with an APR of 18 percent, compounding monthly. The card does not charge 18 percent at the end of the year. It charges one twelfth of that, 1.5 percent, each month, on whatever is owed, including last month's interest. Carry 1,000 dollars for a year with no payments. After one month you owe 1,015. After two, 1,030 dollars and 23 cents, not 1,030. After twelve, about 1,195 dollars and 62 cents. The effective rate was 19.56 percent, not 18. That higher figure is the APY.
# The conversion
To go from APR to APY, divide the APR by the number of compounding periods in a year, add 1, raise the result to the power of the number of periods, and subtract 1. For 18 percent monthly, that is 1.015 multiplied by itself 12 times, which gives 1.1956. For 24 percent monthly it gives 26.8 percent. The gap widens as rates rise. At 4 percent monthly the APY is 4.07 percent, a difference too small to notice. At credit card rates it is a point or two of extra cost.
# Daily, monthly, yearly
Compounding more often raises the yield, but with sharply diminishing effect. At an APR of 18 percent, compounding once a year yields 18 percent. Monthly yields 19.56 percent. Daily yields 19.72 percent. Going from yearly to monthly added a point and a half. Going from monthly to daily added less than a fifth of a point. When a bank advertises daily compounding on savings as a selling point, it is true and nearly irrelevant. The rate itself matters far more than how often it is applied.
# Why each side picks its label
A lender wants the cost to look low, so the headline is the APR. A bank taking deposits wants the reward to look high, so the headline is the APY. Neither is hiding anything, and American rules require these particular figures in these particular advertisements. The trap is comparing one against the other. A loan at 7 percent APR and a savings account at 7 percent APY are not paying and charging the same rate. Convert both to the same basis before you compare.
# What APR leaves in and out
On mortgages and some other loans, the APR includes certain fees spread across the life of the loan, which is why a mortgage with a quoted interest rate of 6.5 percent might show an APR of 6.7 percent. That makes APR useful for comparing two loans with different fee structures. It has a weakness. The calculation assumes you keep the loan for its full term. If you sell or refinance after five years, upfront fees cost you more per year than the APR suggested.
# Using it
Three habits follow. When borrowing, ask for the APR and the compounding period, and work out the yearly cost in dollars on the balance you expect to carry. When saving, compare APY with APY and ignore how often interest compounds. And when you carry a card balance, remember that the true yearly rate is above the number printed on the statement, which makes paying that balance down one of the most reliable uses of spare money. This describes how the figures work, and is not advice about any particular product.

=== money | Fees: the one percent that takes a quarter | Investing ideas
> An annual fee of one percent sounds like almost nothing. Over thirty years it takes roughly a quarter of what you would otherwise have had.
# A small number on a large base
Investment funds charge an annual fee called the expense ratio, quoted as a percentage of everything you have invested. A fund charging 1 percent takes 1 dollar a year for every 100 you hold. It is taken quietly from the fund's assets, so you never see a bill. The reason it matters so much is that it is charged on the whole balance, every year, not on the year's gain. If a fund earns 7 percent before costs and charges 1 percent, a seventh of the return has gone.
# The thirty-year sum
Put 10,000 dollars into an investment that grows 7 percent a year and leave it for 30 years. With no costs it becomes about 76,100 dollars. Now take 1 percent a year off the return, so it grows at 6 percent. After 30 years it is about 57,400 dollars. The difference is about 18,700 dollars, which is 24.5 percent of the larger figure. Nearly a quarter of the final sum went to fees, although no single year's fee looked like more than a rounding error.
# Why it compounds against you
Each dollar paid in fees is also a dollar that stops growing. A fee paid in year one costs you that dollar plus thirty years of what it would have earned. That lost growth is the larger part of the damage. It is compound interest working in reverse, and it follows the same rule as the growth it removes. The longer the period, the worse the effect. Over 10 years, the 1 percent fee takes about 9 percent of the final sum. Over 30, a quarter. Over 40, close to a third.
# The low-cost comparison
Run the same 10,000 dollars for 30 years at 7 percent, less a fee of five hundredths of a percent, which is the kind of charge some broad index funds make. It ends at about 75,100 dollars. The fee cost about 1,000 dollars across three decades, against 18,700 for the 1 percent fund. To justify the higher charge, a fund has to beat the cheaper one by nearly a full percentage point every year for 30 years, after its own costs. Some funds manage it for a while. Few have managed it for decades.
# Fees you do not see in the expense ratio
The expense ratio is the main cost and not the only one. An adviser may charge a separate percentage of your assets each year, on top of the funds' own fees. Some funds carry a sales charge when you buy or sell, called a load. Funds that trade heavily incur dealing costs that reduce returns without appearing in the headline figure. In a taxable account, frequent trading can also create tax bills. Add the layers together. A 1 percent adviser fee on top of funds charging 0.75 percent is a 1.75 percent headwind.
# What a fee should buy
Paying for something is not foolish. Good advice on how much to save, which accounts to use and how not to panic in a falling market can be worth a great deal, and some people would not invest at all without it. The useful question is what you receive for the money, and whether it could be bought another way, such as a flat fee for a plan. What rarely earns its keep is a high fee paid only for the selection of investments, since the fee is certain and the outperformance is not.
# Finding your own number
Look up the expense ratio of each fund you hold. It is in the fund's fact sheet and on most account websites. Multiply each by the amount you hold and add them up to get your yearly cost in dollars. Add any advisory fee. Then ask what that sum buys. You cannot control what markets return. You can control what you pay, and the cost is one of the few figures about an investment that is known in advance. This is a way of thinking about costs, not a recommendation of any fund.

=== money | The cash flow statement: where the money went | Reading financial statements
> The income statement says whether a company made a profit. The cash flow statement says whether it got any money, and what it did with it.
# The third statement
Companies publish three main statements. The income statement shows sales, costs and profit over a period. The balance sheet shows what the company owns and owes on one day. The cash flow statement explains how the cash balance moved between two balance sheets. It is the hardest of the three to dress up, because cash is either in the bank or it is not. It is divided into three sections, and reading them in order tells you most of what you need to know about how a business is really doing.
# Operating: cash from the business itself
The first section starts with net profit and adjusts it back to cash. Costs that did not involve paying anyone this year, chiefly depreciation, are added back. Then come changes in working capital. If customers owe the company more than they did last year, that profit has not arrived as cash, so it is subtracted. If the company built up stock, the cash spent on it is subtracted too. If it is taking longer to pay suppliers, cash goes up. The total is cash from operations, the money the day-to-day business produced.
# Investing: what was spent on the future
The second section records money spent on long-lived things and money received from selling them. The largest line is usually capital expenditure: buildings, machines, vehicles, software. Buying another company appears here as well. This section is normally negative, and that is healthy. A growing business has to invest. What matters is the relationship with the first section. A company that funds its investment out of operating cash is standing on its own feet. One that cannot is depending on outsiders.
# Financing: dealings with owners and lenders
The third section shows money moving between the company and those who fund it. Borrowing brings cash in. Repaying debt sends it out. Issuing new shares brings cash in. Paying dividends and buying back shares send it out. A mature, profitable company usually shows a negative figure here, because it is returning money. A young company usually shows a positive one, because it is raising money. Neither is good or bad in isolation. It depends on whether the first section justifies it.
# A worked example
A company reports a net profit of 10 million dollars. Depreciation was 4 million, so add it back. Customers owe 6 million more than a year ago, so subtract that. Stock rose by 3 million, so subtract that too. Cash from operations is 5 million, half the reported profit. It spent 7 million on equipment. It borrowed 4 million and paid 1 million in dividends. Add up 5, minus 7, plus 3, and cash rose by 1 million. This company is profitable on paper and is borrowing to pay for its growth and its dividend.
# Free cash flow
One figure that does not appear on the statement is worth calculating. Take cash from operations and subtract capital expenditure. The result is free cash flow, the money left after the business has paid for itself and for the upkeep and expansion of its assets. It is what could be used to pay down debt, pay dividends or build up savings. In the example it is 5 million minus 7 million, or minus 2 million. A negative figure for a year or two during expansion is normal. Year after year, it has to be explained.
# What to look for
Compare cash from operations with net profit over several years. They should move roughly together. Profit that consistently runs well ahead of cash is a warning that sales are being booked that customers have not paid for, or that stock is piling up. Check whether investment is paid for from operations or from borrowing. And look at where dividends come from. A dividend funded by free cash flow is sustainable. A dividend funded by new debt is a decision that will have to be revisited.

=== money | How an insurer makes money: underwriting and float | How businesses make money
> An insurance company can pay out almost everything it collects and still do well. It earns from two sources, and the second is holding other people's money.
# The product is a promise
An insurer sells a promise to pay if something bad happens. The customer pays first, in premiums, and the insurer pays later, in claims, sometimes years later and for most customers never. That order of events is unusual. Most businesses pay their costs first and collect from customers afterwards. An insurer collects first and learns what its product cost only when the claims come in. Its two sources of profit both follow from this: the margin between premiums and claims, and the use of the money in between.
# Underwriting
Underwriting is the business of deciding whom to insure and what to charge. The insurer pools many customers whose individual losses cannot be predicted and whose combined losses can, within limits. Out of 10,000 similar houses, nobody knows which will burn, but the number that will is fairly steady from year to year. The premium has to cover the expected claims, the costs of running the company, and a margin. If the premiums collected exceed claims plus expenses, the insurer has made an underwriting profit.
# The combined ratio
The industry measures underwriting with one figure. Add the claims paid and reserved to the expenses of running the business, and divide by the premiums earned. That is the combined ratio. Suppose an insurer earns 100 million dollars in premiums, incurs 70 million in claims and spends 28 million on staff, offices and commissions. The combined ratio is 98 percent, and the underwriting profit is 2 million. A ratio above 100 percent means the insurer paid out more than it took in on its policies.
# Float
Between collecting a premium and paying a claim, the insurer holds the money. Added up across all policies, this pool is known as float. It does not belong to the insurer, since it is owed to future claimants, but the insurer invests it and keeps the income. An insurer with 100 million in annual premiums might hold 150 million in float. Invested mainly in bonds and earning 4 percent, that brings in 6 million a year, three times the underwriting profit in the example.
# Why a small loss can be acceptable
Put the two sources together. An insurer with a combined ratio of 102 percent loses 2 million on underwriting, and with 6 million of investment income still earns 4 million overall. In effect it has borrowed 150 million at a cost of 2 million a year, cheaper than a loan. That is why lines of insurance where claims take years to settle, such as liability cover, attract so much competition. It is also the temptation of the business. Chasing float by cutting premiums too far turns cheap money into very expensive money.
# What goes wrong
Three risks are built in. The first is mispricing, since premiums are set before costs are known, and an error is discovered only years later. The second is correlation. Houses in one town do not burn independently in a wildfire or flood in a hurricane, and a pool of risks that move together is not a pool. Insurers buy their own insurance, called reinsurance, to cap this. The third is investment loss on the float at the moment claims come due, which is why insurers are required to hold capital and invest mostly in safer assets.
# Reading an insurer
To judge an insurance company, look at the combined ratio over ten years, not one, since a single year may be flattered by good weather. Look at whether reserves set aside for old claims have later needed topping up, which suggests past profits were overstated. And look at how the float has grown and what it earns. As a customer, the same picture explains your premium. You are paying for expected claims, the insurer's costs and its margin, less a little that its investment income lets it give back.

=== money | Concessions: how to give ground without losing it | Negotiation
> Every negotiation involves moving from where you started. How you move, and what you ask for each time you do, shapes the deal as much as where you began.
# Movement sends a message
The other side cannot see your limit. They infer it from how you behave, and above all from how you give ground. A large, quick concession tells them your opening was padded and there is more to come. A small, slow one tells them you are near the end. This means the pattern of your concessions is information you are handing over whether you intend it or not. Planning that pattern before you sit down is as much a part of preparing as knowing your walk-away point.
# Trade, do not give
The first rule is that nothing moves for free. When you give something, ask for something in the same sentence. If they want delivery two weeks sooner, the reply is that you can do that if they confirm the order by Friday. A concession with a condition attached is a trade. A concession with nothing attached is a gift, and gifts in a negotiation are rarely answered in kind. They are more often taken as a sign that pressing harder will produce another one.
# Make each step smaller
Suppose you are selling and have 1,000 dollars of room. Giving it in steps of 500, then 300, then 150, then 50 signals that you are running out and the end is near. Giving it as four steps of 250 signals nothing, and the buyer will simply ask for a fifth. Giving 50, then 150, then 300, then 500 teaches the buyer that waiting pays. The total given is the same in all three. Only the shrinking pattern leads the other side to conclude, without being told, that they have reached the floor.
# Give what is cheap to you
The best concessions cost you little and are worth a lot to the other party. A supplier with spare capacity can offer fast delivery at almost no cost. A buyer with cash can offer quick payment. An employer who cannot raise a salary can sometimes offer a day at home or an earlier review. Before negotiating, list everything that could be varied besides the price, and mark what each item costs you. Then ask questions to learn what the other side values. Trades across different items are where both sides gain.
# Name what you are giving up
A concession the other side does not notice earns nothing. Say what it is and what it costs you. This is not a matter of theatre. People value what they are told has value, and a move made silently is often treated as the correction of an inflated opening. Compare two replies. One is fine, 9,500. The other is that 9,500 is below what you had planned for, and you can do it if the contract runs for two years. The second reply marks the movement and asks for a return.
# Do not bid against yourself
If you have made an offer and the other side has only said it is too high, do not improve it. Ask for their figure. Making two moves in a row without a move from them means you are negotiating against yourself, and they have learned that saying no is enough to produce a better offer. The same applies to silence. After you state your position, stop talking. The urge to fill a pause with a sweetener is strong, and the pause is frequently just the other person thinking.
# Before the next one
Write down four things in advance. Your opening position and your walk-away point. The list of items you can vary, with their cost to you. What you will ask for in exchange for each. And the size of your steps, largest first. Then keep a simple record during the talks of what each side has given. It keeps you from conceding twice on the same point, and it lets you say with accuracy that you have moved three times and they have moved once.

=== money | Comparative advantage: why trade pays even for the best at everything | Economics in plain words
> A country, a company or a person can be better at every task and still gain by handing some of them over. The reason is opportunity cost, and one example shows it.
# The puzzle
It seems obvious that if you are better than someone else at two jobs, you should do both yourself. David Ricardo showed in 1817 that this is wrong, using an example of England and Portugal trading cloth and wine. What matters is not who is better in absolute terms. It is what each party gives up to produce a thing. Time spent on one task is time not spent on another, and the party who gives up least to make something has what economists call a comparative advantage in it.
# Two producers
Take two countries with 100 days of labour each. In the first, a day makes 10 rolls of cloth or 5 barrels of wine. In the second, a day makes 2 rolls of cloth or 4 barrels of wine. The first is better at both. Now look at the cost of wine in each. In the first country, a barrel of wine means giving up 2 rolls of cloth. In the second, a barrel means giving up only half a roll. Wine is cheaper to make in the second country, measured in the cloth forgone.
# Without trade
Suppose each country splits its time evenly. The first makes 500 rolls of cloth and 250 barrels of wine. The second makes 100 rolls and 200 barrels. Between them, the world has 600 rolls of cloth and 450 barrels of wine. Each country consumes exactly what it makes. Nothing is wrong with this arrangement, except that the same people working the same days could have more of both goods.
# With specialisation
Let the second country make only wine, 400 barrels. Let the first spend 70 days on cloth and 30 on wine, making 700 rolls and 150 barrels. The world now has 700 rolls of cloth, up from 600, and 550 barrels of wine, up from 450. Nobody worked harder and nobody learned a new skill. The extra output came entirely from each country shifting towards the thing it gives up least to make.
# Both sides gain
Now they trade 150 rolls of cloth for 150 barrels of wine. The first country ends up with 550 rolls and 300 barrels, against 500 and 250 before. The second ends up with 150 rolls and 250 barrels, against 100 and 200 before. Each has more of both. The price, one roll per barrel, works because it lies between the two countries' own costs. The first country would have to give up 2 rolls at home to get a barrel. The second gets a roll for 1 barrel that would cost it 2 at home.
# The same thing at a desk
The principle applies to people. A lawyer who types faster than her assistant still gains by handing over the typing, because an hour of her typing costs an hour of legal work, and that is worth much more. A founder who can do the bookkeeping better than anyone should usually still not do it. When deciding what to delegate, do not ask whether you could do it better. Ask what else your time would produce, and hand over the tasks where the gap is smallest.
# What it does not say
The result is about total gains, and it leaves real questions open. It does not say that everyone within a country gains. Cloth workers in the second country lose their work when it specialises in wine, and the gain to the whole is no comfort unless some of it reaches them. It assumes workers can move between industries, which takes time and sometimes never happens. And it describes a moment. Countries can and do change what they are good at through investment and learning.

=== money | Roth or traditional: when to pay the tax | Taxes and retirement accounts
> American retirement accounts come in two tax flavours. The choice between them rests on one comparison, your tax rate now against your tax rate later.
# Two orders of events
This piece describes United States accounts. A traditional account, such as a traditional IRA or 401(k), generally lets you put money in before income tax, grows untaxed, and is taxed as income when you withdraw. A Roth account takes money on which you have already paid tax, grows untaxed, and is generally not taxed on withdrawal in retirement if the conditions are met. One taxes the seed and the other taxes the harvest. Contribution limits, income limits and conditions change, so check the current rules or ask a tax professional before acting.
# The sum that surprises people
Suppose you have 5,000 dollars of salary to set aside, pay tax at 22 percent, and the investment quadruples by retirement. In a traditional account, all 5,000 goes in and becomes 20,000. Withdraw it at 22 percent tax and you keep 15,600. In a Roth, you pay the tax first, so 3,900 goes in. It quadruples to 15,600, with no tax due. The results are identical. Paying a given rate at the start or at the end makes no difference, because multiplication gives the same answer in either order.
# What actually decides it
The results differ only when the two rates differ. If your rate in retirement will be lower than today's, the traditional account wins, because you avoid tax at the high rate and pay at the low one. In the example, withdrawing at 12 percent leaves 17,600. If your rate will be higher later, the Roth wins. So the choice is a forecast about your own future income and about future tax law, and nobody knows either for certain. That uncertainty is a reason many people hold some of each.
# Who tends to choose which
Some rough patterns follow. People early in a career, in a low bracket and expecting to earn more, often lean towards Roth, since they are paying tax at what may be their lowest rate. People in their peak earning years, in a high bracket, often lean towards traditional, expecting a lower income in retirement. Retirement income is often lower than working income simply because the saving stops and some costs fall away. These are tendencies, and your circumstances may not fit them.
# The hidden size difference
Contribution limits are stated in dollars, and the same dollar figure applies to both types. But a dollar in a Roth is worth more than a dollar in a traditional account, because the tax on it has already been paid. Someone who puts the maximum into a Roth has sheltered more real wealth than someone who puts the same figure into a traditional account, part of which belongs to the tax authority. For savers who reach the limits, this tilts the comparison towards Roth.
# Other differences
Three further points are worth knowing. If an employer matches your contributions, take the match first whichever type you choose, since it is an immediate return on your money. Rules on when the government forces withdrawals to begin have differed between the two types, which affects people who do not need the money and want to leave it growing. And a mix of both gives you a choice in retirement about which account to draw from in a given year, which can help manage your tax bill.
# A way to decide
Write down your current marginal tax rate, the rate on your last dollar of income. Then make an honest estimate of your income in retirement and the rate it would attract under today's brackets. If the retirement rate is clearly lower, favour traditional. If clearly higher, favour Roth. If you cannot tell, split your contributions, which guarantees you are not entirely wrong. Review it when your income changes sharply. This is a description of how the trade-off works, not tax advice for your situation.
