=== electronics | The RC low-pass filter: one resistor, one capacitor | Analog building blocks
> Two cheap parts can strip the fast wiggles off a signal and keep the slow part. One formula tells you where the line between fast and slow falls.
# What it does
A low-pass filter lets slow changes through and holds fast ones back. The simplest version uses two parts. The signal goes through a resistor, and a capacitor connects the far end of that resistor to ground. You take the output across the capacitor. A steady voltage passes untouched, because once the capacitor has charged, no current flows and nothing is lost in the resistor. A rapid ripple is flattened, because the capacitor cannot charge and discharge fast enough through the resistor to follow it. The same two parts sit on sensor inputs, audio tone controls and power rails everywhere.
# Why it works
Think of it as a voltage divider in which the bottom leg changes with frequency. A capacitor resists slow signals strongly and fast signals weakly. Its opposition, called reactance, halves every time the frequency doubles. At low frequency the capacitor looks like a huge resistance, so nearly all of the input appears across it. At high frequency it looks like a small resistance, so most of the input is dropped across the resistor instead and little is left at the output. Nothing switches on or off. The filter simply slides from passing nearly everything to passing nearly nothing as the frequency climbs.
# The cutoff frequency
The point where the slide begins in earnest is the cutoff frequency. It equals 1 divided by the product of 2, pi, the resistance in ohms and the capacitance in farads. Take 10 kilohms and 100 nanofarads. Multiply 10,000 by 0.0000001 and you get 0.001. Multiply by 6.28 and divide into 1, and the answer is about 159 hertz. At exactly that frequency the output is about 71 percent of the input, which engineers call the minus 3 decibel point. Half of the signal's power gets through. Well below 159 hertz, almost everything passes.
# How steep it is
Above the cutoff, the output falls in a simple pattern. Each time the frequency goes up tenfold, the output drops to a tenth. With the 159 hertz filter, a tone at about 1,600 hertz comes out at roughly one tenth of its size, and one at 16,000 hertz at roughly one hundredth. That slope is described as 20 decibels per decade. It is gentle. A single resistor and capacitor will not separate two signals that are close together in frequency. It works well when the thing you want and the thing you do not want are far apart.
# The same filter seen in time
Multiply the resistance by the capacitance and you get the time constant, in seconds. For 10 kilohms and 100 nanofarads it is 1 millisecond. If the input jumps suddenly from 0 to 5 volts, the output does not jump. It rises along a curve, reaching about 63 percent of the way, or 3.2 volts, after one time constant, and about 99 percent after five. This is the same behaviour as the frequency picture, described differently. A filter that blocks fast signals must also respond slowly to sudden steps. You always trade smoothness against speed.
# The loading trap
The formula assumes that whatever feeds the filter has a low resistance and whatever follows it draws almost no current. Break that and the numbers move. Hang a 10 kilohm load on the output of the 10 kilohm filter and two things go wrong at once. The steady output falls to half, because the resistor and the load form a divider. The cutoff also doubles to about 318 hertz. A workable rule is to keep the load at least ten times larger than the filter resistor. When that is not possible, follow the filter with an op-amp wired as a buffer.
# Putting it to work
Three jobs come up constantly. To turn a pulse-width signal from a microcontroller into a steady voltage, pick a cutoff far below the pulse rate. With pulses at 1,000 hertz, 10 kilohms and 1 microfarad give a cutoff near 16 hertz, which leaves only a small ripple. To quieten a noisy sensor before an analog input, set the cutoff a little above the fastest change you care about. And if you swap the two parts, with the capacitor in series and the resistor to ground, you get a high-pass filter with the same cutoff formula, which removes a steady offset and keeps the changes.

=== electronics | The comparator: deciding between two voltages | Analog building blocks
> A comparator answers one question: which of two voltages is higher. Add one resistor for hysteresis and it stops dithering when the answer is close.
# One question, two answers
A comparator has two inputs, marked plus and minus, and one output. When the plus input is at a higher voltage than the minus input, the output goes high. When it is lower, the output goes low. There is nothing in between. That turns a smoothly varying voltage, such as the reading from a light or temperature sensor, into a yes or a no. An op-amp with no feedback will behave this way, but a dedicated part such as the LM393 does it better. It is built to slam from one extreme to the other quickly and to recover at once.
# The output needs a pull-up
The LM393 and many of its relatives have what is called an open-collector output. The chip can pull its output down to ground, but it cannot push it up. For the high state you add a resistor, commonly 10 kilohms, from the output to a positive supply. This looks like a nuisance and is in fact a feature. The pull-up resistor can go to a different supply from the one that powers the comparator. A comparator running on 12 volts can therefore drive a 3.3 volt microcontroller pin directly, simply by pulling up to 3.3 volts.
# The chatter problem
Real signals carry noise, and slow signals spend a long time near the threshold. Put those together and a plain comparator misbehaves. Picture a light sensor switching on a lamp at dusk. As the light fades, the sensor voltage drifts slowly down through the threshold, and the noise riding on it crosses back and forth dozens of times. The output flips each time. A relay driven from it will buzz, and a microcontroller counting edges will count nonsense. The comparator is doing exactly what it was asked. The trouble is having a single threshold for both directions.
# Hysteresis: two thresholds
The cure is to give the comparator two thresholds, one for a rising signal and a lower one for a falling signal. Once the output has switched, the signal must travel back across the gap before it switches again, so small noise cannot undo the decision. This is hysteresis. A room thermostat works the same way. It turns the heat on at 19 degrees and off at 21, not on and off at 20. You get hysteresis by feeding a little of the output back to the plus input through a resistor, so that the output state nudges its own threshold.
# A worked example
Run the comparator from 5 volts. Two 10 kilohm resistors from 5 volts to ground set the plus input at 2.5 volts. Feed the signal into the minus input. Now add a 100 kilohm resistor from the output back to the plus input. When the output is high, that resistor pulls the plus input up a little, to about 2.6 volts. When the output is low, it pulls it down to about 2.4 volts. The signal must rise above 2.6 volts to switch the output low and fall below 2.4 volts to switch it back. The gap is about 0.2 volts.
# Choosing the gap
Make the gap wider than the noise and no wider than the job can tolerate. Look at the signal on an oscilloscope, or log it, and see how much it jitters. If the noise is 50 millivolts from peak to peak, a gap of 100 to 200 millivolts gives a comfortable margin. Lowering the feedback resistor widens the gap. Raising it narrows the gap. Too wide and the circuit becomes sluggish, like a thermostat that lets the room swing by five degrees before it reacts. A filter capacitor on the signal helps as well, and the two cures work together.
# Where you meet it
Comparators with hysteresis are everywhere once you look. A low-battery cut-off uses one so that the load does not flicker on and off as the battery recovers. Logic chips such as the 74HC14 build the idea into every input and call it a Schmitt trigger, which is why they clean up slow or noisy edges. Two practical cautions for the LM393. Its inputs work down to ground but not all the way up to the positive supply, so keep them about 1.5 volts below it. And tie the inputs of any unused half of the chip to fixed voltages so it does not chatter by itself.

=== electronics | The LM317: one rule, any voltage | Famous chips
> The LM317 adjustable regulator has been on benches since the 1970s. It does one thing, holding 1.25 volts between two of its pins, and everything else follows from that.
# Three pins
The LM317 is a linear voltage regulator with three pins: input, output and adjust. National Semiconductor introduced it in the 1970s and several companies still make it. Fixed regulators give you one voltage per part number. This one gives any output from 1.25 volts to about 37 volts, set by two resistors, at up to 1.5 amps in the common package. It protects itself against overheating and short circuits, which is part of why it became the default choice for bench supplies and one-off projects. If you understand a single fact about how it behaves, you can design with it from memory.
# The single rule
The chip adjusts its output until the output pin sits exactly 1.25 volts above the adjust pin. That is all. It does not know or care what the output voltage is relative to ground. Connect the adjust pin to ground and the output is 1.25 volts. Lift the adjust pin to 3.75 volts by any means and the output becomes 5 volts. The standard circuit lifts the adjust pin with two resistors, and it does so in a way that stays accurate as the load changes.
# Setting the output
Put a resistor of 240 ohms between the output and adjust pins. Because the chip holds 1.25 volts across it, a fixed current of about 5 milliamps flows through it. That current has nowhere to go but down through a second resistor from the adjust pin to ground, and the voltage it develops there lifts the output. The output equals 1.25 volts times 1 plus the second resistor divided by the first. For 5 volts, the second resistor is 720 ohms. With 1.5 kilohms you get about 9 volts. A potentiometer in that position makes a variable supply.
# Headroom
A linear regulator can only reduce voltage, and it needs some to work with. For the LM317, plan on the input being at least 3 volts above the output. A 5 volt output wants 8 volts or more coming in. If the input sags below that margin, the output follows it down and stops being regulated. This matters with batteries. A 9 volt battery feeding a 5 volt LM317 circuit starts with margin to spare and loses regulation well before the battery is actually empty. Regulators described as low dropout exist for exactly that situation.
# The heat sum
Everything the regulator removes becomes heat. Multiply the voltage it drops by the current through it. With 12 volts in, 5 volts out and half an amp, it drops 7 volts and burns 3.5 watts. In the common tab package with no heatsink, each watt raises the chip's temperature by roughly 50 degrees Celsius, so 3.5 watts is far too much and the chip will shut itself down. A bare package is good for about 1 watt. Beyond that, bolt on a heatsink, and remember that the metal tab is connected to the output, not to ground.
# A constant current source
The same rule makes a current regulator from one resistor. Connect the resistor between the output and adjust pins and take the load from the adjust end. The chip keeps 1.25 volts across the resistor, so the current is 1.25 divided by the resistance, whatever the load does. A 62 ohm resistor gives about 20 milliamps, enough to run a string of LEDs at steady brightness from a supply that wanders. For 1 amp you need 1.25 ohms, and that resistor dissipates 1.25 watts, so choose its power rating accordingly.
# Building it properly
Three details separate a circuit that works from one that mostly works. Fit a capacitor of about 100 nanofarads close to the input pin if the supply is more than a few centimetres away, and about 1 microfarad on the output to steady it when the load changes suddenly. Keep the 240 ohm resistor close to the chip, since the regulator needs a small minimum load to stay in control and that resistor provides it. And pick a switching regulator when the voltage drop is large or the power comes from a battery, because a linear part wastes the difference as heat.

=== electronics | The thermistor: temperature from a divider | Sensors
> A thermistor is a resistor that changes with temperature. One fixed resistor and an analog input turn it into a thermometer good to a fraction of a degree.
# A resistor that feels heat
A thermistor is a small bead of ceramic whose resistance depends strongly on temperature. The common kind is called NTC, for negative temperature coefficient, which means the resistance falls as the part warms. They cost very little, respond in a second or two, and sit inside thermostats, battery packs, 3-D printers and car engines. The usual hobby part is rated 10 kilohms at 25 degrees Celsius. Two numbers on the datasheet describe it: that resistance at 25 degrees, and a figure called beta, often around 3,950, which says how steeply the resistance changes.
# The curve is not a straight line
With a beta of 3,950, a 10 kilohm thermistor measures about 33.6 kilohms at freezing, 10 kilohms at 25 degrees and about 3.6 kilohms at 50 degrees. Near room temperature the resistance changes by roughly 4.5 percent for each degree. That is a very large signal. A platinum sensor changes by less than half a percent per degree. The price of that sensitivity is a curve. Equal steps in temperature do not give equal steps in resistance, so you cannot convert with a simple multiplication. The conversion takes a logarithm, which any microcontroller can do.
# Wiring it
Make a voltage divider. Connect a fixed 10 kilohm resistor from the 3.3 volt supply to an analog input pin, and the thermistor from that pin to ground. At 25 degrees the two are equal and the pin sits at half the supply, 1.65 volts. At 50 degrees the thermistor has fallen to 3.6 kilohms and the pin reads about 0.87 volts. At freezing it reads about 2.54 volts. Choose the fixed resistor to equal the thermistor's resistance at the middle of the range you care about. That puts the steepest, most sensitive part of the response where you need it.
# From a reading to a resistance
A 10-bit converter reports a number from 0 to 1,023, in proportion to the pin voltage. You do not need to turn that into volts. The thermistor's resistance equals the fixed resistor times the reading, divided by 1,023 minus the reading. Suppose the reading is 270. Then 270 divided by 753 is about 0.359, and the thermistor is 3.59 kilohms. The supply voltage cancelled out of the sum, as long as the converter uses that same supply as its reference. A sagging supply therefore does not disturb the temperature, which is a real advantage of the divider.
# From a resistance to a temperature
The beta equation does the last step. Divide the measured resistance by the resistance at 25 degrees and take the natural logarithm. Divide that by beta. Add 1 divided by 298.15, which is 25 degrees expressed in kelvin. The result is 1 divided by the temperature in kelvin. For 3.59 kilohms the logarithm is about minus 1.026, and working it through gives 323.2 kelvin. Subtract 273.15 and you have 50 degrees Celsius. Over a span of a few tens of degrees this is good to a fraction of a degree. For wider ranges, datasheets give a three-term formula called Steinhart-Hart.
# Where the errors come from
Three things limit accuracy. Tolerance comes first. A thermistor specified to 1 percent is good to about a quarter of a degree at 25 degrees, and the fixed resistor adds its own error, so use a 1 percent part there too. Self-heating is second. The measuring current warms the bead slightly. With 10 kilohms the power is a fraction of a milliwatt and the effect is small, but a low-value divider can add a visible offset. Resolution is third. Near 25 degrees a 10-bit converter resolves about a tenth of a degree. At the hot and cold extremes each count is worth much more.
# When to choose something else
A thermistor suits the range from roughly minus 40 to 125 degrees Celsius, where it is cheap, fast and sensitive. For readings that need no arithmetic, a digital sensor such as the DS18B20 reports degrees directly over one wire, with a typical accuracy of half a degree. For ovens, kilns and flames, use a thermocouple, which survives many hundreds of degrees. For laboratory precision, use a platinum resistance sensor. Whichever you pick, check it against melting ice in water, which sits very close to 0 degrees, before trusting it.

=== electronics | Driving a DC motor: from one transistor to the H-bridge | Motors and how to drive them
> A motor needs far more current than a logic pin can give, and it fights back when switched off. Here is the safe way to run one forwards, backwards and at any speed.
# A motor is not an LED
A microcontroller pin can supply perhaps 20 milliamps. A small hobby motor draws a few hundred milliamps while spinning freely and several times that at the moment it starts or when it is held still, which is called the stall current. Connecting a motor directly to a pin will not turn it and may damage the pin. The pin's job is to control a switch, and the switch carries the motor current from a separate supply. A motor is also a coil of wire, and coils store energy in their magnetic field. That stored energy is the second problem to solve.
# One direction with one transistor
For a motor that only runs one way, use an N-channel MOSFET of the kind described as logic level, which turns fully on with 3.3 or 5 volts at its gate. Connect the motor between the positive supply and the transistor's drain, and the source to ground. Drive the gate from the microcontroller through a resistor of about 100 ohms. Add a 10 kilohm resistor from gate to ground, so the transistor stays off while the microcontroller is starting up and its pins are floating. The grounds of the motor supply and the microcontroller must be joined.
# The diode you must not leave out
Current in a coil cannot stop instantly. When the transistor switches off, the motor's inductance keeps pushing current and the voltage at the drain leaps upward until something gives way, often the transistor. The fix is one diode connected across the motor, pointing against the normal flow, with its striped end to the positive supply. In normal running it does nothing. At switch-off it gives the coil current a loop to circulate in and die away harmlessly. A fast Schottky diode such as the 1N5819 suits small motors. This part is called a flyback diode.
# Speed by switching fast
Speed is controlled by switching the transistor on and off rapidly and varying the fraction of time it is on. At half the time on, a 12 volt motor behaves roughly as if it were fed 6 volts. The motor's own inertia and inductance smooth out the pulses. Switching at a few thousand times a second works but can produce an audible whine from the windings. Switching above about 20,000 times a second moves the noise out of hearing. Very low duty will not start a motor from rest, since it needs a certain push to overcome friction.
# Reversing: the H-bridge
To reverse a DC motor you must swap the polarity across it. An H-bridge does that with four switches arranged like the letter H, with the motor as the crossbar. Close the top left and bottom right switches and current flows through the motor one way. Close the top right and bottom left and it flows the other way. Close both bottom switches and the motor's terminals are shorted together, which brakes it firmly, because a spinning motor is a generator. Open all four and it coasts to a stop.
# The forbidden state
There is one combination that must never happen. If the top and bottom switches on the same side are both closed, they form a short circuit straight across the power supply. This is called shoot-through, and it destroys transistors in an instant. It can occur for a few millionths of a second during every reversal if the timing is careless. Driver chips handle this internally, which is the main reason to buy one. The old L298 works but can lose around 2 volts or more in its transistors. Modern MOSFET drivers such as the TB6612FNG and DRV8833 lose far less.
# Sizing and wiring
Choose the driver for the stall current, not the running current. You can estimate stall current by measuring the motor's winding resistance with a multimeter. A 12 volt motor that reads 6 ohms will draw about 2 amps at stall. Give the motors their own supply where you can, and put a capacitor of a few hundred microfarads near the driver, because the surge at start-up can dip the supply enough to reset a microcontroller. A 100 nanofarad ceramic capacitor soldered across the motor terminals cuts the electrical noise from the brushes.

=== electronics | How a touchscreen finds your finger | How it works
> A phone screen does not feel pressure. It measures hundreds of tiny capacitors many times a second and notices which ones your finger has disturbed.
# No pressure needed
The glass on a phone has no moving parts and does not respond to force. It senses the electrical effect of a finger. Older touch panels, the resistive kind still found on some industrial equipment, worked by pressure. Two flexible conductive layers were pushed together and the contact point was measured. They needed a firm press and could track only one point reliably. The capacitive screens that became standard on phones in the late 2000s respond to the lightest contact, track many fingers at once and sit behind hard glass that does not wear out.
# A grid of capacitors
Behind the cover glass are two layers of electrodes, one running in rows and one in columns, separated by a thin insulator. They are made of indium tin oxide, a material that conducts electricity and is nearly transparent in a thin film. Wherever a row crosses a column, the two conductors and the insulator between them form a very small capacitor. A phone screen might have 15 columns and 30 rows, which makes 450 crossings. The electrodes are often shaped as chains of diamonds so that the electric field spreads up through the glass.
# What a finger changes
Your body conducts electricity and is very large compared with the electrodes. When a fingertip comes close to a crossing, some of the electric field that linked the row to the column ends on the finger instead. Less charge passes between that row and column, so the capacitance at the crossing falls slightly. The change is tiny, a fraction of an already small value, but it is repeatable. Note that the finger does not need to touch a conductor. The effect passes through the glass, which is why a screen works through a thin protector.
# Scanning the grid
A controller chip measures every crossing in turn. It sends a signal down one row and measures how much of it arrives on each column. Then it moves to the next row. One complete pass over the grid produces a table of numbers, one per crossing, and the controller repeats the pass many times every second, commonly 60 times or more. It subtracts a stored baseline taken when nothing was touching. What remains is a picture of the screen in which each fingertip shows up as a small bump covering several neighbouring crossings.
# Finer than the grid
The electrodes are spaced about 4 or 5 millimetres apart, yet a screen places your finger to a fraction of a millimetre. It does that by interpolation. A fingertip disturbs several crossings at once, and the controller calculates the centre of weight of the bump. Suppose three neighbouring columns show changes of 20, 60 and 40. The middle one is strongest, but the right one beats the left, so the centre lies a little to the right of the middle column, by about a sixth of the spacing. On a 5 millimetre grid that is under 1 millimetre.
# Many fingers, gloves and water
Because each crossing is measured separately, two fingers make two separate bumps, and pinching and rotating gestures follow directly. Ordinary gloves fail because they hold the finger too far from the glass and do not conduct. Gloves made for screens have conductive thread in the fingertips. Water is the awkward case. A droplet conducts, links neighbouring electrodes and can look like a touch, which is why a wet screen behaves erratically. Controllers use extra measurements to tell a grounded finger from a floating droplet, with partial success.
# Build a touch button
The same idea works on a bench. A touch button is a copper pad on a circuit board, covered by plastic or glass, wired to a microcontroller pin. The microcontroller measures how long the pad takes to charge. A nearby finger adds a few picofarads and the time grows. Some microcontrollers, the ESP32 among them, have pins with this measurement built in. The code that makes it reliable keeps a slowly moving baseline, so drift from temperature and humidity is ignored, and reports a touch only when the reading departs from that baseline by a set margin.

=== electronics | From gates to an adder | Computer architecture from gates up
> Every calculation a computer makes comes down to adding binary digits with a handful of logic gates. You can follow the whole circuit in five minutes.
# Addition is a short list of rules
Adding in binary uses the method you learned for decimal, with fewer cases. In any column, 0 plus 0 is 0. 0 plus 1 is 1. And 1 plus 1 is 2, which binary writes as 1 0, so you put down 0 and carry 1 to the next column. There are no other cases for two digits. Because the rules are so few, they can be built from logic gates, which are small circuits that take ones and zeros in and give a one or a zero out according to a fixed rule.
# Two gates add one column
Look at the sum digit alone. It is 1 when exactly one of the two inputs is 1, and 0 when both are the same. That is the rule of the gate called exclusive OR, written XOR. Now look at the carry. It is 1 only when both inputs are 1, which is the rule of an AND gate. Feed the same two input bits to an XOR gate and an AND gate and you have a circuit that adds two bits. It is called a half adder, because it handles only half the job.
# Handling the carry coming in
Every column except the first may also receive a carry from the column to its right, so a real adder stage needs three inputs. A full adder is built from two half adders and an OR gate. The first half adder adds the two input bits. The second adds the incoming carry to that result. If either half adder produced a carry, the OR gate passes it on. Test it with all three inputs at 1. The answer should be 3, which is 1 1 in binary. The circuit gives a sum of 1 and a carry of 1.
# Chaining the stages
To add numbers of four bits, line up four full adders and connect each carry output to the carry input of the next. Add 6 and 7, which are 0 1 1 0 and 0 1 1 1. Start at the right. The first column is 0 plus 1, giving 1 and no carry. The second is 1 plus 1, giving 0 and a carry. The third is 1 plus 1 plus the carry, giving 1 and a carry. The fourth is 0 plus 0 plus the carry, giving 1. The result is 1 1 0 1, which is 13.
# Why the simple adder is slow
In that chain, the last stage cannot settle until the carry has worked its way through every stage before it. The design is called a ripple-carry adder for that reason. Each stage adds a small delay, and across 64 bits those delays add up to a limit on how fast the processor can run. Faster designs, called carry-lookahead adders, use extra gates to work out in advance whether each group of columns will produce or pass on a carry. They spend more transistors to save time, which is the most common trade in chip design.
# Everything from one kind of gate
A NAND gate gives 0 only when both of its inputs are 1. Any other gate can be made from NAND gates alone, which is why it is called universal. An XOR takes four of them. A complete full adder can be made from nine. In the CMOS technology used in almost all chips, a two-input NAND gate is four transistors. So one stage of this adder is 36 transistors, and a 64-bit ripple adder a little over 2,000. A modern processor has billions of transistors, most of them doing work as plain as this.
# Subtraction and a bench version
A computer subtracts with the same adder. To take one number from another, it flips every bit of the second number and adds 1, a form called two's complement, and then adds as normal. Flipping is done by a row of XOR gates, and the extra 1 goes into the spare carry input of the first stage. To see all this working, the 74HC283 chip contains a complete four-bit adder. Wire eight switches to its inputs and five LEDs to its outputs on a breadboard, and you can check the sum of 6 and 7 for yourself.
