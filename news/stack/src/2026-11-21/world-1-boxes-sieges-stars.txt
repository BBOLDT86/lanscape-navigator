=== world | The shipping container: the box that shrank the world | Inventions that changed things
> A steel box with standard corners cut the cost of loading a ship so far that distance almost stopped mattering to trade.
# Before the box
Until the 1950s, general cargo crossed the sea as separate items: sacks, crates, barrels, bales, drums. Gangs of dockworkers carried each piece from a truck or railcar into a shed, then onto the quay, then into the hold, where it was fitted in by hand. At the other end the process ran in reverse. A ship could spend as long in port as at sea. Goods were damaged and stolen along the way. For many products, the cost of getting through the two ports was a larger obstacle to trade than the ocean in between.
# One man's impatience
Malcom McLean ran a trucking company in North Carolina. He had watched his trucks wait at the docks while cargo was handled piece by piece, and he asked why the whole trailer body could not simply be lifted onto the ship. He sold his trucking business, bought a shipping line, and had a wartime tanker fitted with a deck to carry boxes. On April 26, 1956, the Ideal X sailed from Newark, New Jersey, for Houston with 58 containers aboard. A crane had loaded them in hours.
# The numbers
The saving was dramatic. By figures widely cited from McLean's operation, loading loose cargo onto a ship at the time cost 5 dollars and 86 cents a ton. Loading the Ideal X cost under 16 cents a ton. The box was sealed at the factory and not opened until it reached the customer, so theft and breakage fell as well. A ship that could be emptied and refilled in a day earned money at sea instead of sitting at a quay, and that changed the economics of the vessel itself.
# The standard
A box is only useful if every crane, ship, truck and railcar can handle it. Early containers came in many sizes, and each line's equipment fitted only its own. During the 1960s, international standards settled on lengths of 20 and 40 feet and on a common corner fitting, a design by the engineer Keith Tantlinger that lets a crane grab a box and lets boxes lock to each other in a stack. Capacity has been counted ever since in twenty-foot equivalent units. From then on, a container packed in one country could travel on any carrier.
# Ports moved
Containers need deep water, vast paved yards and road and rail links, not rows of narrow piers and warehouses in a city centre. Old ports that could not adapt declined quickly. The piers of Manhattan and the docks of east London emptied, while new terminals grew at places such as Port Elizabeth in New Jersey and Felixstowe in England. Dock work changed from mass manual labour to a small number of skilled crane and vehicle operators. The disputes over that change were long and bitter.
# What it did to trade
Once the cost of moving goods fell far enough, it became practical to make a product in several countries. Parts could be made where each was cheapest, shipped to another country for assembly and shipped again for sale. The supply chains behind electronics, clothing and cars depend on this. Ships grew to match. The Ideal X carried 58 boxes. The largest container ships built since the late 2010s carry more than 20,000 twenty-foot units, and a single vessel can hold the contents of thousands of trucks.
# Why it is worth remembering
The container contained no new science. Steel boxes, cranes and ships all existed. The invention was a system: a standard unit and everything else redesigned around it. That is a useful pattern to recognise. Some of the largest gains come from agreeing on an interface, so that many separate parties can work together without negotiating each time. The cost was real too. It fell on dock communities, who lost a way of life within a generation.
+ Further reading: Marc Levinson, The Box (Princeton University Press, 2006).

=== world | 1453: the fall of Constantinople | Turning points in history
> For a thousand years a triple wall kept every army out of Constantinople. In the spring of 1453 a young sultan broke it with the largest guns yet built.
# The last Roman city
Constantinople was founded in the year 330 as the new capital of the Roman Empire. When the western half of the empire collapsed in the fifth century, the eastern half carried on, Greek-speaking and Christian, for another thousand years. Historians call it the Byzantine Empire. Its people called themselves Romans. By 1453 it had shrunk to little more than the city itself and a few scattered territories, surrounded by the growing Ottoman state. The city was still a great prize. It sat on the strait between Europe and Asia and controlled the passage to the Black Sea.
# The walls
What had kept the city safe was its land wall, built in the fifth century under the emperor Theodosius the Second. An attacker met a wide moat, then a low wall, then an outer wall with towers, then a massive inner wall with taller towers. It ran for several miles from sea to sea across the neck of the peninsula. Sea walls guarded the other sides, and a great chain could be drawn across the mouth of the harbour, the Golden Horn. Armies had tried the walls for centuries and failed.
# The two sides
The sultan, Mehmed the Second, was 21 years old. He brought an army usually estimated at 80,000 or more, and a fleet. Inside the walls, the emperor Constantine the Eleventh had about 7,000 fighting men to defend miles of fortification. Among them were several hundred soldiers from Genoa under Giovanni Giustiniani, an expert in siege defence. Appeals to the Christian powers of the west brought little. The siege began on April 6, 1453.
# The guns
Gunpowder artillery was not new, but Mehmed used it on a new scale. A Hungarian gun founder named Orban, who had first offered his services to the emperor and been turned away for lack of money, cast enormous bronze cannon for the sultan. The largest fired stone balls weighing hundreds of pounds and could be fired only a few times a day. For weeks the guns battered the land walls. Each night the defenders filled the breaches with earth, timber and barrels. The old masonry had been built to stop ladders and siege towers.
# Ships over the hill
The chain kept the Ottoman fleet out of the Golden Horn, so the sea wall along the harbour needed few guards. Mehmed removed that advantage with a feat of engineering. In late April his men built a track of greased timbers over the hill behind the suburb of Galata and hauled dozens of ships overland on rollers, launching them into the harbour behind the chain. The defenders, already too few, now had to spread along another stretch of wall.
# The last assault
Before dawn on May 29, the Ottomans attacked along the whole land wall in waves. For hours the line held. Then Giustiniani was badly wounded and carried from the wall, his men wavered, and the sultan's elite troops, the janissaries, forced their way over the stockade. Constantine the Eleventh is said to have thrown off his imperial insignia and died fighting in the breach. His body was never certainly identified. By midday the city had fallen, after 53 days.
# What changed
Mehmed made the city his capital and the great church of Hagia Sophia a mosque. The Ottoman Empire, now firmly astride two continents, would be a major power for more than four centuries. The Roman Empire, by one way of counting, had finally ended. The siege also carried a military message. High stone walls, the basis of defence for thousands of years, could be knocked down by cannon. Across Europe, fortress builders began replacing tall thin walls with low, thick, angled earthworks designed to absorb shot.

=== world | Marie Curie in five minutes | Lives in five minutes
> She coined the word radioactivity, found two elements in a shed, and remains the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.
# Warsaw
Maria Sklodowska was born in Warsaw on November 7, 1867, the youngest of five children of two teachers. Poland was then under Russian rule, and its universities did not admit women. She studied in secret with an informal network of classes that moved from house to house to avoid the authorities. She and her sister Bronislawa made a pact. Maria would work as a governess to pay for her sister's medical studies in Paris, and her sister would then help her in turn. She waited years for her chance.
# Paris
In 1891, aged 24, she enrolled at the Sorbonne. She lived in an unheated attic, and by her own account sometimes had too little to eat. She took a degree in physics in 1893 and another in mathematics the following year. Looking for laboratory space, she was introduced to Pierre Curie, a physicist already known for his work on crystals and magnetism. They married in 1895. Their honeymoon was a cycling tour of the French countryside, on bicycles bought with a wedding gift.
# A new property of matter
In 1896 Henri Becquerel found that uranium gave off invisible rays that fogged photographic plates. Marie Curie chose these rays as the subject of her doctorate. Using a sensitive instrument Pierre had helped invent, she measured the faint electrical effect the rays produced in air. She found that the strength of the rays depended only on the amount of uranium present, not on its chemical form. She concluded that the rays came from within the atom itself, and she named the phenomenon radioactivity.
# Two elements
She then noticed that pitchblende, a uranium ore, was more radioactive than its uranium content could explain. Something else had to be in it. Pierre put aside his own research to join her. In 1898 they announced two new elements: polonium, named for her homeland, and radium. To prove radium existed, they had to isolate it. Working in a draughty shed, she processed tonnes of ore by hand over nearly four years, stirring boiling vats with an iron rod, to obtain about a tenth of a gram of a radium salt.
# Prizes and loss
In 1903 the Curies shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Becquerel. She was the first woman to win one. In 1906 Pierre was killed in a Paris street, struck by a horse-drawn wagon. She was left with two young daughters. The Sorbonne gave her his teaching post, making her its first woman professor. In 1911 she won a second Nobel Prize, this time in chemistry, for the discovery of the two elements. No one else has won in two different sciences.
# The war
When war came in 1914, she saw that X-rays could locate bullets and fractures before surgery, and that the army had almost no equipment near the front. She raised money, fitted cars with X-ray machines and generators, learned to drive, and took them to field hospitals herself. Her daughter Irene, still in her teens, worked beside her. She also trained women as X-ray operators. Well over a million wounded soldiers are estimated to have been examined with the units she organised.
# The cost and the legacy
Nobody then understood the danger of radiation. She carried test tubes of radium in her pockets and kept them in a desk drawer, admiring their glow. She died on July 4, 1934, of aplastic anaemia, a failure of the bone marrow that was very probably caused by her years of exposure. Her notebooks are still radioactive and are kept in lead-lined boxes. A year after her death, her daughter Irene and son-in-law Frederic Joliot won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In 1995 her remains were moved to the Pantheon in Paris.

=== world | Why the Moon always shows the same face | Space
> The Moon spins exactly once for each trip around the Earth. That is no coincidence. Gravity slowed it until the two motions matched.
# One face
Every human who has ever looked at the Moon has seen the same side of it. The familiar pattern of dark patches never rotates out of view. The far side stayed completely unknown until 1959, when the Soviet probe Luna 3 flew behind the Moon and sent back the first photographs. It is not a dark side. It receives as much sunlight as the near side does. When the Moon is new for us, the far side is in full daylight.
# It does rotate
It is tempting to say the Moon does not spin. It does, once every 27.3 days, which is exactly the time it takes to circle the Earth. Try it with two objects on a table. Move a cup around a bottle while keeping the cup's handle pointed at the bottle. By the time the cup has gone all the way around, its handle has pointed in every direction of the room. It has turned once on its own axis. The Moon is doing the same thing.
# How gravity did it
The match is the result of tides. The Earth pulls harder on the near side of the Moon than on the far side, which stretches the Moon very slightly into a rugby-ball shape with a bulge pointing towards us. Long ago the Moon spun faster. Its rotation kept carrying the bulge off the line between the two bodies, and Earth's gravity kept tugging the bulge back. That tug worked as a brake. Over millions of years it slowed the spin until the bulge stayed put, and the same face has pointed at Earth ever since.
# A little more than half
Careful watchers can see slightly more than one hemisphere. The Moon's orbit is an ellipse, so it travels faster when near the Earth and slower when far, while its spin stays steady. The two get slightly out of step during each orbit, and the Moon appears to rock from side to side. Its axis is also tilted a little, which lets us peek over the top and bottom. These wobbles are called librations. Over time, about 59 percent of the surface can be seen from Earth.
# The Earth is being braked too
The process runs both ways. The Moon raises tides in Earth's oceans, and the friction of that water against the sea floor slows our planet's rotation. The effect is tiny. The day lengthens by roughly 2 thousandths of a second each century. But it adds up. Growth lines in fossil corals suggest that about 400 million years ago a year held roughly 400 days, each a couple of hours shorter than now.
# The Moon is leaving
The energy Earth loses goes partly into pushing the Moon outward. Apollo astronauts and Soviet rovers left reflectors on the surface, and observatories bounce laser pulses off them to time the round trip. The measurements show the Moon receding at about 3.8 centimetres a year, roughly the rate at which fingernails grow. In principle the process ends when Earth's day matches the Moon's orbit and each world shows the other one face. That would take far longer than the Sun will last.
# A common arrangement
Tidal locking is the usual fate of a moon close to its planet. The large moons of Jupiter and Saturn all keep one face to their planets. Pluto and its moon Charon have gone the whole way and are locked to each other, so that from one side of Pluto, Charon hangs motionless in the sky and from the other side it is never seen. Many planets orbiting close to small, dim stars are expected to be locked as well, with one side in permanent day.

=== world | The birthday problem: why coincidences are common | Mathematics ideas
> In a group of only 23 people, two of them more likely than not share a birthday. The reason is that you are counting pairs, not people.
# The surprise
Ask how many people must be in a room before there is an even chance that two of them share a birthday, and most guesses land well above 100. With 365 days to choose from, it feels as though you would need about half that many people. The answer is 23. With 23 people the chance of at least one shared birthday is 50.7 percent. With 30 it is about 71 percent. With 50 it is 97 percent, and with 70 it is 99.9 percent.
# Pairs, not people
Intuition goes wrong because it imagines one person comparing their own birthday with everyone else's. That gives only 22 comparisons in a group of 23. But the question is whether any two people match. The first person can pair with 22 others, the second with 21 more, and so on. That makes 253 different pairs. Each pair has a 1 in 365 chance of matching. With 253 chances at a rare event, a hit somewhere becomes likely. The number of pairs grows roughly with the square of the group size.
# The calculation
The clean way to work it out is to find the chance that nobody matches and subtract from 1. The second person avoids the first person's birthday with probability 364 over 365. The third avoids both with probability 363 over 365. Keep multiplying. By the twenty-third person the fraction is 343 over 365, and the product of all 22 fractions is about 0.493. So the chance that all 23 birthdays differ is 49.3 percent, and the chance that at least two coincide is 50.7 percent.
# A different question
Do not confuse this with the chance that someone shares your birthday. That is a much rarer event, because only the pairs that include you count. In a room of 23 it is about 6 percent. To reach an even chance that someone matches your own birthday, you need 253 other people. The gap between those two questions, any match versus a particular match, explains a great deal about why coincidences feel more miraculous than they are.
# Coincidences in general
You notice the coincidence that happened. You do not count the thousands of possible coincidences that did not. Meeting a neighbour in a foreign airport seems astonishing. But you would have been equally astonished to meet any one of hundreds of acquaintances, in any of dozens of places, in any year. Add up all the pairs and the event stops being unlikely. With enough people and enough chances, it would be strange if nothing strange occurred. A lottery drawing the same numbers twice is this effect at work.
# Why programmers care
The same arithmetic matters in computing. Programs often give each file or password a short fingerprint called a hash. Two different inputs with the same hash are a collision, and it is tempting to think collisions are negligible if there are billions of possible values. The birthday sum says otherwise. An even chance of a collision arrives after about 1.2 times the square root of the number of possible values. For 365 days that gives about 23. For a hash with about 4 billion possible values, it gives only about 77,000 items.
# The lesson to keep
When you estimate the chance of a coincidence, count the opportunities for it honestly. Ask how many pairs, how many days, how many people could have produced something you would have found equally striking. A one in a million event happens many times a day in a large country. The birthday problem is a small, checkable example of a large point. Your sense of what is unlikely is tuned to yourself, and the world runs on all the pairs at once.

=== world | How we know the age of the Earth | How we know
> The figure of 4.54 billion years comes from measuring atoms in meteorites. Getting there took a century of argument and one of the first ultra-clean laboratories.
# Early estimates
In 1650 the scholar James Ussher added up the generations in scripture and dated creation to 4004 before Christ. By the nineteenth century, geologists looking at the thickness of rock layers and the slowness of erosion were sure the Earth was vastly older, though they could not say by how much. The physicist Lord Kelvin tried a calculation. He assumed the planet began molten and worked out how long it would take to cool to its present state. His answers ranged from 20 million to a few hundred million years, and he came to favour the low end.
# What Kelvin could not know
Kelvin's physics was sound and his assumptions were not. He did not know that the Earth has an internal source of heat. Radioactivity, discovered in 1896, keeps the interior warm, and slow churning in the mantle carries heat up from the depths. Either would upset his sum. Geologists and biologists had needed far more time than he allowed, and the discovery of radioactivity showed where it had been hiding. It also provided the tool that would settle the matter.
# Atoms as clocks
A radioactive element decays into another at a fixed pace, set by its half-life, the time for half of any sample to change. Uranium 238 decays through a chain of steps into lead 206 with a half-life of about 4.5 billion years. Uranium 235 becomes lead 207 with a half-life of about 700 million years. Nothing known in nature speeds or slows these rates. So if a mineral formed containing uranium and no lead, the ratio of lead to uranium in it today tells you how long ago it formed.
# The first dates
In the first decade of the twentieth century, Ernest Rutherford and the American chemist Bertram Boltwood applied the idea. Boltwood's uranium minerals gave ages from about 400 million to more than 2 billion years, far beyond Kelvin's range. The English geologist Arthur Holmes spent decades refining the method. A difficulty remained. The Earth's surface is constantly recycled by erosion and the movement of plates, so no rock survives from the very beginning. The oldest known mineral grains, zircon crystals from the Jack Hills of Western Australia, are about 4.4 billion years old.
# The meteorite
The answer came from space. Meteorites are leftovers from the formation of the solar system, made at the same time as the Earth and unaltered since. In the early 1950s the American geochemist Clair Patterson set out to measure the lead in fragments of the iron meteorite that made Meteor Crater in Arizona. That meteorite contains almost no uranium, so its lead shows the mix the solar system started with. Comparing it with lead from Earth and from other meteorites gave the time elapsed. In 1956 he published the result: 4.55 billion years, give or take 70 million.
# The clean room
Patterson's first measurements made no sense. His samples kept showing far too much lead. He eventually realised the lead was coming from the laboratory itself: from dust, from tools, from his own skin and hair. He spent years building one of the first ultra-clean laboratories to keep it out. The experience showed him how much lead human activity had spread through the environment, mainly from leaded petrol, and he spent much of his later career campaigning to have it removed.
# Why the figure is trusted
The modern value is 4.54 billion years, with an uncertainty of about 1 percent, and it has barely moved since 1956. Its strength is agreement between independent lines. Different decay systems with different half-lives give matching ages. Many meteorites of different types cluster at the same date. The oldest rocks brought back from the Moon are a little younger, as they should be. When separate methods with separate weaknesses keep arriving at one number, the reasonable conclusion is that the number is right.
