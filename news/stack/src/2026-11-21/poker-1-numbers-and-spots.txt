=== poker | Flopping a set: the odds behind set mining | Probability for card players
> A small pocket pair is mostly a ticket to hit three of a kind. Here is how often that happens and how deep the stacks must be for the ticket to pay.
# What a set is
When you hold a pocket pair and one of the two remaining cards of that rank comes on the board, you have a set. It is three of a kind in its most disguised form, because two of the three cards are hidden in your hand. A player holding an ace and a king on a board of king, seven, deuce has no way to see that an opponent with pocket sevens is far ahead. Calling a raise with a small pair mainly in the hope of making this hand is known as set mining.
# The number to remember
You will flop a set or better 11.8 percent of the time, which is about one flop in eight and a half, or odds of 7.5 to 1 against. The sum is short. Fifty unseen cards remain and two of them help you. The chance that all three flop cards miss is 48 over 50, times 47 over 49, times 46 over 48, which is 88.2 percent. If you could always see all five board cards the figure would rise to 19.2 percent, but you will rarely get to the river cheaply with an underpair.
# Why 7.5 to 1 is not the price
It is tempting to conclude that you need 7.5 times the call in winnings to break even. In practice you need much more. Sometimes you flop the set and everyone folds to your first bet. Sometimes the opponent has missed and puts in nothing more. And now and then your set loses. A common working rule is that the player you are up against should have at least 15 times the size of the call left in their stack, and 20 times is more comfortable. The money you win must come from behind, not from the pot already there.
# A worked example
The blinds are 2 and 5 dollars. A solid player raises to 15 and you hold a pair of fives on the button. The call is 15, so the rule asks for 225 to 300 dollars in the raiser's stack and in yours. If the raiser has 500, call. If the raiser has only 120 left, fold, since even winning the whole stack every time you hit would barely cover the seven or eight times you missed. The same reasoning says to fold small pairs to a large reraise. The price has tripled and the stacks have not.
# How strong the set is
On a flop of king, seven, deuce in three different suits, a set of sevens has 91 percent equity against a pair of aces and 98 percent against an ace and a king that has paired the king. Draws change the picture. On king of spades, seven of spades, deuce of diamonds, an opponent with the ace and queen of spades still wins about 26 percent of the time. That is why a set should usually be bet at once on a board with two cards of a suit. Slow play gives the draw a free card.
# When it goes wrong
Two fears come with sets, and one is mostly imaginary. When two players hold different pocket pairs, both flop a set only about 1 percent of the time. Losing set under set is painful and rare, and it is not a reason to fold a set of deuces. The realistic danger is the board itself: three or four cards to a flush or a straight by the river. Against that, your set has help. A flopped set improves to a full house or four of a kind by the river about one time in three.
# Using it
Treat small pairs before the flop as a question about stacks, not cards. With deep stacks and a raiser who cannot fold an overpair, call and hope. With short stacks, or against a player who gives up easily after the flop, the same pair has little value as a call. When you miss, which is seven times in eight, let the hand go without a struggle unless you have a clear reason to continue. When you hit, build the pot from the first street. The profit from all those cheap folds arrives in a few large pots.

=== poker | What the flop gives you: the odds of hitting | Probability for card players
> Most hands miss most flops. Knowing the real frequencies of pairs, draws and overcards stops you from expecting help that is not coming.
# Start from the miss
Three cards arrive on the flop and you hold two. With two unpaired hole cards, you will pair at least one of them 32.4 percent of the time. Turn that around and you get the fact that shapes the game after the flop. An unpaired hand misses about two flops in three. That is true of an ace and a king just as it is of a seven and a deuce. The same applies to your opponent, which is why the player who bets first so often takes the pot.
# Where 32.4 percent comes from
After your two cards, 50 remain. Six of them pair one of your cards and 44 do not. The number of possible flops is 19,600. The number made up entirely of cards that miss you is 13,244. Divide and you get 67.6 percent for a complete miss, so 32.4 percent for at least a pair. That figure includes the rare better results. Two pair using both your cards arrives about 2 percent of the time and three of a kind using one of your cards a little over 1 percent.
# Suited cards
Two cards of the same suit flop a made flush 0.84 percent of the time, about once in 119 tries. They flop a flush draw, with two more of the suit, 10.9 percent of the time. From there the draw completes by the river about 35 percent of the time. Multiply through and a suited hand ends up making a flush by this route only a few times in a hundred. Being suited is worth a few percentage points of equity. It improves a hand that was already playable. It does not make a weak hand a strong one.
# Connected cards
Take a jack and a ten. They flop a straight 1.3 percent of the time. They flop a draw with eight cards that complete it 9.7 percent of the time, and a gutshot with four such cards 16.8 percent of the time. So a strong straight draw is about as common as a flush draw, roughly one flop in ten. Cards with a gap between them make fewer straights, and cards near the ends of the deck, such as an ace and a king or a three and a deuce, make fewer still.
# Overcards to your pair
Big pocket pairs fear one thing on the flop, a higher card. With two kings, an ace appears on the flop 22.6 percent of the time. With two queens, at least one ace or king appears 41.4 percent of the time. With two jacks, an overcard appears 57 percent of the time, more often than not. This is why jacks are awkward to play. They are a strong hand before the flop that will be looking at a higher card on most boards, and you should decide in advance how you will respond.
# What the numbers say to do
Since everyone misses most flops, a bet represents a hand more often than it has one, and folding every time you miss is a costly habit against frequent bettors. Equally, a strong draw on the flop is uncommon enough that you should not pay over the odds before the flop hoping to find one. Choose starting hands because they make good pairs or dominate the hands that will call you. Treat flushes and straights as a bonus on top.
# Carry four of them
Four numbers are worth keeping. You pair up on about one flop in three. You flop a flush draw with suited cards about one time in nine. You flop a set with a pocket pair about one time in eight and a half. And an overcard to two jacks shows up on more than half of flops. With those in your head, you can judge quickly whether an opponent's story is likely, and whether your own plan depends on an event that happens too rarely to pay for it.

=== poker | Stealing the blinds: the arithmetic of the button raise | Preflop by position
> When everyone folds to you in late position, the blinds are money lying on the table. A short sum shows how often a raise has to work, and what changes the answer.
# The situation
The action folds around to you on the button. Only the small blind and the big blind remain, each holding two random cards, and between them they have put 1.5 big blinds into the pot. A raise here is called a steal, though it is a perfectly normal play. You have the best position for the rest of the hand, only two players can stop you, and both of them will be out of position if they call. The question is how wide to raise, and arithmetic gives a good first answer.
# The break-even point
Suppose you raise to 2.5 big blinds. You are risking 2.5 to win the 1.5 already there. If your cards had no value at all when called, the raise would break even when both blinds fold 2.5 times in every 4, which is 62.5 percent. Now make the raise 2 big blinds. You risk 2 to win 1.5, and the break-even point falls to 57 percent. A smaller raise needs to succeed less often. That is the main reason opening raises have shrunk over the years.
# Two players have to fold
Both blinds must fold for the steal to win at once, so their individual folding frequencies multiply. If each folds 80 percent of the time, both fold 64 percent of the time, and the steal profits even with worthless cards. If each folds 70 percent, both fold only 49 percent, and worthless cards lose money. Watch the two players to your left for an orbit or two before you widen your range. One stubborn defender in the big blind changes the sum more than any chart does.
# Your cards are not worthless
The break-even figure assumes you never win after being called, which is far too gloomy. When a blind calls, you still hold real cards, you act last on every street, and you will often win with a single bet on the flop. This is why the range of hands worth raising on the button is so wide. Hands like king and nine of different suits, or any two suited cards with some connection, fail as calls and succeed as steals, because part of their profit comes from the folds.
# Antes change everything
In tournaments an ante is often added to the pot, commonly paid as one extra big blind. The pot before you act is then 2.5 big blinds, not 1.5. A raise to 2 big blinds now risks 2 to win 2.5 and needs to work only 44 percent of the time. That large shift is why late-stage tournament play looks so aggressive. The players raising with weak hands are not being reckless. The pot they are raising into has nearly doubled relative to the price of trying.
# When to tighten up
The steal depends on folds, so its enemy is the reraise. If the players in the blinds reraise often, your weakest steals lose their raise without seeing a flop. Against them, drop the bottom of your range and keep hands that can call a reraise or raise again. Stack sizes matter too. When a blind has a short stack of 10 to 15 big blinds, expect an all-in over your raise, and only open hands you are content to call with, or hands so weak that folding to the shove is easy.
# The view from the blinds
The same sum tells the blinds what to do. If a button raiser is opening very wide, folding the big blind 80 percent of the time hands over a steady profit. The big blind already has one big blind in the pot and is getting a good price to call, so it should defend with a wide range of suited and connected hands, and reraise some of the time with both strong hands and a few bluffs. The small blind has a worse price and worse position, and should mostly reraise or fold.

=== poker | Paired boards: when the flop shows a pair | Postflop by board type
> A flop such as king, seven, seven is hard for anyone to hit. That single fact decides who should bet, how much, and when to believe a raise.
# How often and why it matters
About 17 percent of flops, one in six, contain a pair. They play differently from other boards for one reason. With two cards of one rank already on the table, only two remain in the deck, so very few hands contain one. On a flop of king, seven, seven, most of the hands that either player can hold are no better than they were before the flop. Pots on paired boards are therefore often won by whoever shows interest first, and big hands are rarer than they feel.
# Count the combinations
Put a raiser's likely hands on king, seven, seven and count. An ace with a king can be made 12 ways, since three kings and four aces remain. An ace with a seven of the same suit can be made only 2 ways. Pocket sevens, for four of a kind, 1 way. Even a player who calls with many suited hands containing a seven holds one a small fraction of the time. Hands with a king outnumber hands with a seven several times over in most ranges. Top pair is a strong hand here.
# Who the board favours
The answer depends on the rank of the pair. On a high paired board such as king, king, four, the player who raised before the flop holds more kings, because raising ranges are full of high cards. On a low paired board such as five, five, deuce, the caller, particularly from the big blind, holds more fives, because cheap calls are made with small suited cards. When the pair is low and you were the raiser, expect more resistance. When the pair is high, expect to be given credit.
# Betting as the raiser
Because the board is so hard to hit, a small bet does the work of a large one. A bet of a quarter to a third of the pot, made with most of your range, wins the pot often enough against hands that missed. It also keeps your losses small when you run into the rare three of a kind. There is little to protect against, since few draws exist on a flop like king, seven, seven in three suits, so large bets mostly fold out the hands you beat.
# When you are raised
A raise on a paired board claims a narrow set of hands, mainly three of a kind. Since there are so few of those, a raise here is also a favourite bluff of aggressive players, who know you will rarely be strong either. Do not fold top pair automatically. Against a habitual bluffer, call and see what the turn brings. Against a quiet, careful player at low stakes, a raise followed by a second large bet on the turn is three of a kind far more often than not, and top pair becomes a fold.
# Holding the three of a kind
When you do hold a seven on king, seven, seven, two things need watching. The first is your kicker. If an opponent also holds a seven, the side card decides the pot, and a seven with a small card is in poor shape against a seven with an ace. The second is the turn and river. Any king gives a full house to a player holding a king, which beats your three sevens unless you fill up too. Play the hand for value, and take pause if a passive opponent wakes up after the board changes.
# A simple plan
As the raiser on a high paired board, bet small and often. As the raiser on a low paired board against a big blind caller, check more of your weak hands and be ready to be bet at. As the caller, pick a few hands with overcards or a possible draw and raise the small bet now and then, since the raiser is so often weak. And throughout, weigh a show of strength by the number of hands that could actually have it.

=== poker | ICM by hand: working one example | Tournament play
> The independent chip model turns tournament stacks into money. Doing the sum once, with three players, makes clear why a coin flip for your stack is a bad bet near the prizes.
# The setup
Three players are left in a small tournament. The prize pool is 1,000 dollars, paid 500 for first, 300 for second and 200 for third. The stacks are 5,000 chips for player A, 3,000 for player B and 2,000 for player C. Everyone is already sure of 200. The question the independent chip model answers is how much each stack is worth right now, on average, if the players are of equal skill. The model makes one assumption. Your chance of finishing first equals your share of the chips.
# First place
Start with first place, which is the easy part. Player A has half the chips and so wins half the time. Player B wins 30 percent of the time and player C 20 percent. If the tournament paid everything to the winner, the calculation would stop here and each chip would be worth the same amount of money. It does not stop here, because second and third place also pay, and a player with few chips collects those prizes more often than chip counts alone suggest.
# Second place for the leader
To find how often A finishes second, imagine each rival winning in turn. If B wins, which happens 30 percent of the time, remove B's chips. A then has 5,000 of the remaining 7,000, so A takes second about 71 percent of those times. If C wins, 20 percent of the time, A has 5,000 of the remaining 8,000, and takes second 62.5 percent of those times. Added together, A finishes second about 34 percent of the time. First is 50 percent, so third must be the remaining 16 percent.
# The money
Now multiply. For player A, 50 percent of 500 is 250. About 34 percent of 300 is 102. About 16 percent of 200 is 32. Player A's stack is worth about 384 dollars. The same working gives about 328 dollars for B and about 289 for C, and the three figures add up to the 1,000 in the pool. Look at what happened. A holds half the chips but only 38 percent of the money. C holds a fifth of the chips and 29 percent of the money.
# Why chips lose value as you gain them
Suppose C doubles up through B, so the stacks become 5,000 for A, 1,000 for B and 4,000 for C. Run the sum again and C's stack is worth about 364 dollars. C doubled in chips, from 2,000 to 4,000, but rose in money only from 289 to 364, a gain of about 76 dollars. Had C lost the same pot, C would be out in third with 200, a loss of about 89 dollars. The same number of chips is worth more when lost than when won.
# What that does to a call
Say B moves all in and C must decide whether to call for the whole stack. Ignore the blinds to keep it simple. In chips, C risks 2,000 to win 2,000 and needs to win half the time. In money, C risks 89 dollars to gain 76. Divide 89 by the sum of the two, 165, and C needs to win about 54 percent of the time. Those extra four points are sometimes called a risk premium. With more players left and bigger jumps between prizes, the premium can be several times larger.
# Using it at the table
Nobody does this arithmetic during a hand, and software does it instantly for study afterwards. What you carry to the table is the shape of the result. Calling an all-in for your tournament life requires a better hand than the pot odds alone suggest. Moving all in yourself is more attractive than calling, because your opponents face the same penalty for calling you. Big stacks can lean on middle stacks, who have the most to lose. And far from the money, the effect is small enough to ignore.

=== poker | Minimum defence frequency: how often you must not fold | Game theory in plain words
> If you fold too often to a bet, any two cards become a profitable bluff against you. One fraction tells you where that line sits for every bet size.
# The problem it solves
Imagine an opponent who knows you fold whenever you lack a strong hand. That opponent can bet every time with nothing and show a profit, because the pot they pick up when you fold outweighs what they lose when you happen to call. To stop this, you have to continue with enough hands that a bet with nothing merely breaks even. The share of your hands you must continue with is called the minimum defence frequency. It depends on one thing, the size of the bet compared with the pot.
# The fraction
Divide the pot before the bet by the pot plus the bet. That is the share of your range that should call or raise. Facing a bet the size of the pot, the answer is one half. Facing half the pot, it is two thirds. Facing a third of the pot, it is three quarters. Facing twice the pot, it is one third. The pattern is easy to hold in mind. Small bets must be defended widely, and large bets allow you to fold most of what you hold.
# Why it works
Look at it from the bluffer's side. Someone who bets 50 into a pot of 100 risks 50 to win 100. The bluff shows a profit if it works more than one time in three. So you must not fold more than one time in three, which means defending two thirds. For a pot-sized bet of 100, the bluffer risks 100 to win 100 and needs folds more than half the time, so you defend half. The defender's fraction and the bluffer's break-even point are the same sum seen from opposite seats.
# It applies to ranges, not hands
The fraction says nothing about what to do with the two cards in front of you until you place them among all the hands you could hold in that spot. Suppose you reach the river and face a bet of half the pot. List the hands you could plausibly have, strongest to weakest. The top two thirds of that list continue. If your actual hand sits in the top two thirds, call. If it sits in the bottom third, fold without regret, even if it is a hand that looks pretty.
# A worked example
You defended your big blind, called a bet on a flop of queen, eight, three, and both of you checked the turn. The river is a deuce and your opponent bets 60 into 80. The fraction is 80 divided by 140, or 57 percent. Your range here contains queens with various kickers, eights, pocket pairs between the eight and the queen, and a few missed hands. Queens and the better eights make up more than 57 percent of it. So a pair of eights with a good kicker calls, and a pair of fours folds.
# Where it stops being right
The fraction is a defence against an opponent who bluffs as often as the theory allows. Many players do not. At low stakes, a large river bet from a cautious player is a real hand most of the time. Against that player, defending half your range to a pot-sized bet pays off strong hands again and again. Fold more. Against a player who fires at every checked pot, defend more than the fraction. It is a baseline that protects you against unknown opponents, and you should move away from it as you learn who you are playing.
# What to take from it
Use the fraction in three ways. First, as a check on yourself. If you find you fold to nearly every bet on the turn and river, you are being robbed, and the sums say so. Second, as a guide when bluffing. A half-pot bluff needs to work a third of the time, so aim it at players who fold more than that. Third, as a reason to size your own bets with thought, since a large bet forces fewer calls from a correct defender, and a small one invites many.

=== poker | Roulette: one number that never changes | Other games' maths
> Every spin of a roulette wheel carries the same built-in loss, whatever you bet on and however you stagger your stakes. The sum takes one line.
# The wheel
An American roulette wheel has 38 pockets: the numbers 1 to 36, half red and half black, plus a green zero and a green double zero. A European wheel has 37, with a single zero. You can bet on one number, on small groups of numbers, or on large groups such as red, odd, or the first dozen. The payouts are set as if the green pockets did not exist. That gap between the true odds and the paid odds is the whole business model of the game, and it can be worked out exactly.
# One number
A bet on a single number pays 35 to 1. On an American wheel there are 38 pockets, so the true odds against you are 37 to 1. Picture betting 1 dollar on the same number for 38 spins, with every pocket coming up once. You win once and collect 35, and lose the other 37 times. You are down 2 dollars on 38 dollars bet. That is a loss of 5.26 percent, or about 5 cents on each dollar wagered. It is called the house edge.
# Red or black
Try the bet that feels safest. Red pays even money and covers 18 pockets. Over the same 38 spins you win 18 times and lose 20, because the two green pockets count against you. Down 2 dollars on 38 again, the same 5.26 percent. Work through dozens, columns, splits and corners and each comes to exactly the same figure. On the American wheel there is one exception, a bet on the five numbers zero, double zero, 1, 2 and 3, which pays 6 to 1 and loses 7.89 percent. Avoid that one.
# One zero or two
On a European wheel the single-number bet still pays 35 to 1, but there are only 37 pockets. Over 37 spins you win 35 once and lose 36 times, down 1 dollar on 37, which is 2.70 percent. A single zero halves the cost of playing. Some tables also use a French rule on even-money bets, under which you lose only half your stake when zero comes up, bringing the edge on those bets to 1.35 percent. If you are going to play, the choice of wheel matters more than anything you do afterwards.
# Why doubling up fails
The oldest system is to bet on red, double the stake after every loss, and return to the starting stake after a win, so each win recovers all losses plus one unit. Start at 10 dollars. After seven losses in a row you have lost 1,270 dollars and the next bet is 1,280, all to end up 10 ahead. Seven straight losses happen about 1.1 percent of the time on an American wheel, roughly once in every 89 sequences. Table limits and finite wallets guarantee that the long losing run arrives before the system can outlast it.
# The wheel has no memory
On August 18, 1913, at the casino in Monte Carlo, black came up 26 times in a row. Players lost heavily betting on red during the run, convinced it was due. It was not. Each spin is independent of the last, and after 10 blacks the chance of red is what it always was. The belief that a streak must correct itself is known as the gambler's fallacy. No arrangement of stakes changes the edge either. Every system is a different way of dividing up the same expected loss.
# What it costs
Treat the edge as a price. Multiply your average bet by the number of spins and by the edge. At 10 dollars a spin and 40 spins an hour on an American wheel, you wager 400 dollars an hour, and 5.26 percent of that is about 21 dollars. That is the average cost of an hour of entertainment, with wide swings around it on any single night. Seen that way, the sensible decisions are plain. Pick the single-zero wheel, set the amount you are willing to pay, and stop when it is spent.
