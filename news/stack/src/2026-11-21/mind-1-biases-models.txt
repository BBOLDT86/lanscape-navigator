=== mind | The default effect: the option nobody chose | Biases one at a time
> Whatever happens when you do nothing is the choice most people end up with. Whoever sets the default has quietly made the decision for the majority.
# Doing nothing is a choice
Almost every decision comes with a default, the outcome that applies if you take no action. The plan your employer enrols you in, the settings a new phone ships with, the box already ticked on a form. In principle a default is only a starting point, and anyone can change it. In practice, large majorities never do. This is known as the default effect, and it is one of the most dependable findings in the study of decisions. It works on important matters as strongly as on trivial ones.
# The organ donor figures
The best-known evidence comes from a 2003 paper by Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein, who compared European countries. In countries where people had to opt in to be organ donors, consent was low, about 12 percent in Germany. In countries where people were presumed to consent unless they opted out, it was over 99 percent, as in Austria next door. Two similar societies differed by a factor of eight on a serious question, and the difference tracked the form. Note that these are consent figures. Actual transplant rates depend on much else and differ far less.
# Why defaults stick
Several forces push the same way. Changing a default takes effort, even if it is only finding the form, and effort postponed is often effort never made. A default also looks like a recommendation, as if someone who knows more has chosen it for people like you. And switching feels like taking responsibility. If you opt out and things go badly, the loss is your doing. If you stay and things go badly, it merely happened. People will accept a worse outcome to avoid that feeling.
# Saving for retirement
Pension enrolment shows the effect on money. When American employers began enrolling new staff in retirement plans automatically, with the freedom to leave, participation among new employees rose sharply. An influential study by Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea, published in 2001, found it went from roughly a third to well over four fifths at one company. The same study showed the cost. Most people also stayed at the default contribution rate and the default fund, even when those were set low and cautious. The default helped them start and then held them back.
# Defaults set against you
A default is chosen by someone, and that someone has interests. Free trials renew into paid subscriptions unless you cancel. Add-on insurance is ticked in advance at the checkout. Software arrives with data sharing switched on. Each relies on the fact that most people will not act. A useful habit is to treat any option that was selected for you as a proposal from the party who selected it, and to ask who benefits if you leave it alone.
# Setting your own
The effect can be turned to your advantage, because you are allowed to set defaults for your future self. An automatic transfer to savings on payday makes saving the thing that happens when you do nothing. A standing weekly appointment for exercise does the same. So does keeping fruit on the counter and biscuits on a high shelf. In each case you make the decision once, when you are thinking clearly, and after that the path of least effort leads where you wanted to go.
# An audit worth an hour
Once a year, list the defaults you are living with and never chose. Your pension contribution rate and fund. Your bank account and the interest it pays. Your insurance renewals, phone plan and subscriptions. The privacy settings on your accounts. For each, ask whether you would pick it today if you were starting fresh. Most will be fine. One or two will be costing real money, and changing them takes minutes. The effort that kept you from doing so was small. It was only larger than nothing.

=== mind | Survivorship bias: the evidence that did not come back | Biases one at a time
> We study the winners because they are the ones still around to study. The failures that would complete the picture have dropped out of sight.
# The missing half
Survivorship bias is the mistake of drawing conclusions from the cases that passed through some filter while forgetting those that did not. The survivors are visible and the failures are not, so the survivors look like the whole story. The classic case is the wartime study of bombers returning with bullet holes, in which the statistician Abraham Wald saw that the armour belonged where the returning planes were unmarked, since planes hit there had not come back. The same error appears in business, investing, health and everyday advice.
# Lessons from the successful
Read a few profiles of company founders and patterns appear. They took big risks, ignored doubters, left university early, worked extreme hours. The trouble is that the same was true of a great many founders whose companies failed, and nobody wrote their profiles. A trait shared by all the winners tells you nothing unless you know how common it was among the losers. If 100 people take a wild risk and 3 succeed spectacularly, the 3 will credit the risk, and the 97 will not be asked.
# Funds that vanish
Investing gives a measurable example. A fund company may launch many funds. Those that perform badly are closed or merged into others, and their records disappear from the tables. Look at the funds available today and their average past return is flattered, because the worst have been removed from the count. Any comparison of the funds that exist now against the market is biased upward for that reason. Careful studies add the dead funds back in, and the average result gets worse when they do.
# They do not make them like they used to
Old buildings, old furniture and old tools often seem better made than modern ones. Some were. But what you are looking at is the fraction that lasted a century. The badly built houses of 1900 fell down or were demolished long ago, and the flimsy chairs were burned. The same filter applies to music and books. The songs still played from fifty years ago are the best few of that era, and they are being compared against everything released this year, good and bad.
# When the filter is the result
The most dangerous version is when the selection process creates the very pattern you think you have discovered. Suppose a hospital studies its patients and finds that those who arrive by helicopter fare worse than those who arrive by car. The helicopter is not the cause. It is sent to the worst cases. Or suppose a coach studies athletes who reached the top and finds they all trained through injuries. Those who trained through injuries and were finished by them are no longer athletes and are not in the sample.
# The question to ask
One question exposes the bias in almost every case. What would I be seeing if this did not work? If the failures would be invisible, then the successes you can see prove little. Ask where the sample came from and what had to happen for a case to be in it. Ask how many started. A testimonial page shows the customers who were happy. A list of long-lived people who smoked shows the ones who lived. In each case the denominator is missing.
# Putting it to use
When you take advice from a success, look for people who did the same things and failed, and see what actually differed. When you judge a strategy, find the base rate: of all who tried it, how many got the result. When you review your own decisions, keep records of the ones that went badly as well as the ones that went well, since memory runs its own survivorship filter and keeps the wins. The aim is modest. Before you learn a lesson from what is in front of you, check what has been removed.

=== mind | The memory palace: remembering with places | Learning and memory
> Your memory for places is far stronger than your memory for lists. A technique more than two thousand years old lets you store one in the other.
# An old method
The method of loci, also called the memory palace, was taught to orators in ancient Greece and Rome, who had to deliver long speeches without notes. Cicero tells the story of its origin. The poet Simonides stepped outside a banquet hall shortly before the roof fell in, and afterwards he could name the dead by recalling where each guest had been sitting. The lesson drawn from it was that order and location hold memories in place. Competitors in modern memory contests still use the same method.
# Why it works
Human memory was shaped by finding the way home and remembering where things are, not by storing lists of words. You can walk through your childhood home in your head and recall rooms you have not seen in decades. You probably cannot recall a shopping list from last week. The technique borrows the strong system to prop up the weak one. Each item you want to remember is turned into an image and placed at a spot along a familiar route. To recall, you walk the route in your mind and look.
# Build the route
Choose a place you know thoroughly, such as your home. Fix a path through it and pick distinct stops in a set order: the front door, the coat hook, the hall table, the sofa, the television, the kitchen sink, the fridge. The stops must always come in the same sequence, because the sequence is what preserves the order of the list. Ten stops is plenty to begin with. Walk the path in your head twice, until you can name each stop without hesitation.
# Place the images
Suppose the list is eggs, batteries, stamps, olive oil, a birthday card, onions and string. At the front door, picture a huge egg wedged in the frame, cracking as you push past. On the coat hook, a coat with batteries pouring from its pockets. The hall table is papered with stamps. The sofa is soaked in olive oil and you slide off it. The television is showing a giant birthday card singing. The sink is full of onions and your eyes sting. The fridge is tied shut with string.
# Make them strange
Ordinary images fade and odd ones stay. The pictures that work are exaggerated, moving, and involve the senses: something enormous, something broken, a smell, a noise, something happening to you. Each image should interact with its location, not merely sit near it. An egg beside the door is forgettable. An egg jammed in the door that you have to break to get in is not. This takes a few seconds per item at first and becomes quick with practice.
# What it is good for
The method suits anything with a fixed order or a set number of points: the headings of a talk, the steps of a procedure, a list of names, the main arguments of a chapter. For a speech, place one image for each section along the route and walk it as you speak. It is less suited to understanding. It holds the pegs and not the meaning, so use it once you already understand the material and need to keep it available. It combines well with spaced review.
# Start tonight
Try it on tomorrow's to-do list. Use seven stops in your home and seven tasks. Spend ten seconds on each image, then walk the route once before bed and once in the morning. Most people recall all seven on the first attempt, and are surprised. Keep separate routes for separate purposes, such as your home for errands and your walk to work for a presentation, so that old images do not get in the way of new ones. Images you stop revisiting fade within days, and the route is free again.

=== mind | Regression to the mean: why streaks fade | Decisions under uncertainty
> An extreme result is usually followed by a more ordinary one, with no cause at all. Missing this leads people to credit cures, punishments and jinxes that did nothing.
# The pattern
Any result that depends partly on skill and partly on luck will show the same behaviour. When the result is extreme, the next one tends to be closer to average. A golfer who shoots an outstanding first round usually scores worse in the second. A student with a terrible test usually does better next time. Nothing has to change for this to happen. The extreme result needed good or bad luck on top of ability, and luck does not repeat on demand. Francis Galton described the effect in the 1880s, while studying the heights of parents and children.
# Why it must happen
Think of a score as ability plus luck. The people with the very best scores on a given day are, as a group, both able and lucky. Tomorrow their ability is unchanged, but their luck will be average on the whole, some up and some down. So the group's scores fall back part of the way towards the middle. The reverse holds for the worst scores. The more luck is involved in a result, the stronger the pull. Where there is no luck at all, there is no regression.
# The instructor's mistake
Daniel Kahneman told of flight instructors who were certain that praise harmed performance and criticism improved it. They had seen it repeatedly. A cadet praised for an unusually good landing did worse the next time. A cadet shouted at for an unusually bad one did better. Both changes would have happened if the instructor had said nothing, because unusually good and unusually bad landings are followed by more typical ones. The instructors had drawn a firm lesson about teaching from a statistical effect.
# Cures that do nothing
The same trap waits in health. People seek treatment when their symptoms are at their worst. Back pain, colds and low moods fluctuate, and the worst days are naturally followed by better ones. Whatever was tried at the low point, whether a remedy, a supplement or a change of pillow, gets the credit for an improvement that was coming anyway. This is the main reason medical research uses control groups. Comparing treated patients with similar untreated ones is the only way to separate the treatment from the drift back to normal.
# Jinxes and slumps
Sport is full of supposed curses that are this effect in costume. An athlete appears on a magazine cover after the best run of their career, and the following months are less brilliant. A manager is sacked after a dreadful stretch, and results improve under the replacement. A rookie of the year has a weaker second season. In each case the moment of attention was chosen because of an extreme, and extremes are followed by something more ordinary whoever is on the cover or in the manager's chair.
# In business and at home
A company's best-performing branch this quarter is unlikely to be the best next quarter. The sales representative who topped the table will probably do well, but less well. A speed camera placed at the site of last year's cluster of accidents will be followed by fewer accidents there, partly because a cluster is an extreme. Before you conclude that an intervention worked, or that a star has faded, ask what would have happened with no intervention at all.
# How to allow for it
Three habits help. First, judge by long records, not by the latest result, since an average over many trials contains less luck. Second, when you predict what follows an extreme result, shade your guess towards the average, and shade it further when luck plays a larger part. Third, when testing whether something works, compare against a similar group that was left alone. And be slow to reward or punish on the strength of one outstanding or dreadful result. It told you less than it seemed to.

=== mind | How to apologise so it lands | Communication and persuasion
> A good apology repairs trust and a bad one makes things worse. The difference lies in a few parts that can be learned, and in a few words to leave out.
# What an apology is for
An apology does a specific job. Someone has been wronged, and they need to know three things: that you understand what you did, that you accept it was your doing, and that it is less likely to happen again. Regret alone does not deliver those. Many apologies fail because they are aimed at ending the speaker's discomfort or closing the subject. The test of an apology is whether the other person ends up feeling understood, not whether you feel you have said sorry.
# Name what you did
Begin with the specific act. Say that you are sorry you missed the deadline you had promised and left them to explain it to the client. Do not say that you are sorry about what happened. Vague apologies sound like guesses, and they leave the other person wondering whether you know what the problem was. Being specific costs something, because it means saying the unflattering thing aloud. That cost is part of what makes the apology believable.
# Own it without the escape hatches
Three small phrases ruin apologies. The word if, as in sorry if anyone was offended, questions whether anything happened. The word but, as in sorry, but I was under pressure, cancels what came before it. And the passive voice, as in mistakes were made, removes the person who made them. Say I did this. Explanations are not forbidden. They belong later, and only if asked for or useful, and they must not be offered as a reason the other person should not mind.
# Acknowledge the effect
Say what your action cost them, in their terms. You were embarrassed in front of your team. You had to stay late to fix it. You stopped being able to rely on what I told you. This is the part most often left out, and it is the part the listener is waiting for. If you do not know how it affected them, ask, and then listen without defending yourself. Being told the impact is uncomfortable. Sitting through it is evidence that you take it seriously.
# Repair and commit
Then say what you will do. Where the damage can be mended, offer to mend it: redo the work, replace the thing, correct the record with the people who heard the wrong version. Where it cannot, say what will be different. A specific change is worth more than a promise to do better. From now on I will send you the draft two days ahead is something they can check. Do not promise what you may not deliver, since a broken commitment made during an apology is worse than none.
# Timing and manner
Apologise soon, though not so fast that you have not understood what you are apologising for. For anything serious, do it in person or by voice, where your tone can be heard. Keep it short. A long apology starts to be about you and your feelings. Do not ask for forgiveness on the spot or expect the mood to lift at once. The other person is entitled to stay annoyed for a while. Having apologised, do not bring it up again to check whether you are forgiven.
# Receiving one
Two things help when you are on the other side. If the apology is real, say so plainly, with thanks. You need not say it is fine when it is not. Thank you, I appreciate you saying that is enough. If the apology is hollow, you can accept the gesture and still name what is missing, for instance that you would like to know it will not happen again. And judge the apology finally by what follows. The words open the door. The changed behaviour is the apology being kept.

=== mind | Occam's razor: the simplest explanation that fits | Mental models
> When several explanations fit the facts, start with the one that assumes the least. It is a rule for where to look first, not a guarantee of where the truth lies.
# The rule
William of Ockham was an English friar and philosopher of the fourteenth century. The principle that carries his name, in a common wording, says that entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity. In plain terms, do not assume more than you need to. If two explanations account for the same facts equally well, prefer the one with fewer moving parts. The razor is the image of shaving off assumptions that do no work. Ockham did not invent the idea, and versions appear as far back as Aristotle, but he used it so often that it took his name.
# Why simpler is likelier
There is arithmetic behind the preference. Every extra assumption is another thing that has to be true. Suppose an explanation needs four independent assumptions and each has an 80 percent chance of being right. The chance that all four hold is about 41 percent. A rival explanation needing one such assumption stands at 80 percent. Elaborate stories feel more convincing because they are detailed, but each detail is another place to be wrong. A chain of plausible steps can be an unlikely whole.
# An everyday case
Your car will not start. One explanation is a flat battery. Another is that a fault in the engine computer has coincided with a failed fuel pump. Both fit the silence when you turn the key. The first needs one common failure. The second needs two rarer ones at once. The razor says to check the battery first. Mechanics, doctors and engineers work this way by training. A saying taught to medical students makes the same point: when you hear hoofbeats, think of horses before zebras.
# A famous near miss
In 2011, physicists at an experiment called OPERA reported particles called neutrinos apparently arriving faster than light, which would have overturned a century of physics. The two candidate explanations were a revolution in the laws of nature or an undetected error in a very complicated measurement. Most physicists, including the team itself, bet on the error and asked others to help find it. The following year it was traced to faulty equipment, including a loose fibre-optic cable. The simpler explanation was duller and correct.
# Simple does not mean easy
The razor is often misused to favour whichever explanation is easiest to state or most comfortable to believe. That is not what it says. The count that matters is of assumptions, not of words. It was magic is short and assumes a great deal. The razor also applies only among explanations that fit all the evidence. If the simple story leaves facts unexplained, it has not earned its place, and you must accept a more complicated one. A wise limit is to make things as simple as possible and no simpler.
# When more than one thing is true
The world is not obliged to be simple. In medicine there is a counter-saying, known as Hickam's dictum, that a patient can have as many diseases as they please. An elderly patient with five symptoms may well have three conditions and not one rare disease that explains them all. Accidents and business failures usually have several causes acting together. So use the razor to decide the order in which to test ideas, and let the evidence decide which idea survives.
# Using it
When you face a puzzle, write down the candidate explanations and under each list what would have to be true for it to hold. Count the items and judge how likely each one is. Test the leanest candidate first, in the cheapest way available. If it fails, move to the next. Be most suspicious of your own explanation when you notice yourself adding a new assumption each time a fact does not fit. An explanation that needs constant patching is telling you something.

=== mind | The golden mean: Aristotle on virtue as a skill | Philosophy for daily life
> Aristotle held that good character lies between two ways of going wrong and is built by practice, like a craft. His account still works as a method.
# Character as a craft
In the Nicomachean Ethics, written in the fourth century before Christ, Aristotle asks what makes a person good at living. His answer is that virtues are settled habits of feeling and acting well, and that they are acquired the way a skill is acquired. Nobody becomes a builder by reading about building. People become builders by building. In the same way, he says, we become just by doing just acts and brave by doing brave ones. Character is the result of what you repeatedly do.
# Between too much and too little
His best-known idea is that each virtue sits between two faults, one of excess and one of deficiency. Courage lies between cowardice, which fears too much, and rashness, which fears too little. Generosity lies between meanness and wastefulness. Proper confidence lies between boasting and false modesty. Good humour lies between being a clown and being a bore. So a virtue is not the opposite of a single vice. There are two ways to miss, and the virtue is the hitting of a target between them.
# The mean is not the middle
This is often mistaken for advice to be moderate in everything, and Aristotle rules that out. The right amount is relative to the person and the situation. His example is food. A quantity that is too little for a champion wrestler in training may be too much for a beginner. Likewise there is no fixed correct amount of anger. Some situations call for a great deal and some for none. The virtue is feeling and acting to the right degree, at the right time, towards the right people, for the right reason.
# Why rules run out
Because the target moves with circumstances, no rule book can replace judgement. Aristotle calls the needed ability practical wisdom. It is the capacity to see what a particular situation requires, and it comes from experience. That is why he thinks the young can be brilliant at mathematics and yet rarely have it. When unsure, he suggests looking to a person of good judgement and asking what they would do. Having a real example in mind, a mentor or a grandparent, is often more useful than a principle.
# Correcting your aim
He adds practical advice for people who know they tend to miss. First, of the two faults, one is usually worse, so steer away from that one. Second, notice which way you personally lean. Someone inclined to avoid conflict should aim at what feels to them like bluntness, and will probably land on honesty. He compares this to straightening a bent piece of wood by bending it the other way. Third, be wary of pleasure when judging your own case, since it bends the verdict.
# A worked example
Take giving feedback to a colleague whose report is weak. The deficiency is saying nothing or offering empty praise, which protects you and fails them. The excess is listing every flaw in front of others. The mean depends on particulars. A new hire needs two main points and encouragement, in private. A senior colleague before a board meeting needs the full list today. The same honest impulse takes different forms. What stays constant is the aim, their improvement, and the question of what this person in this situation needs.
# Practising it
Pick one trait you would like to strengthen. Name its two failure modes, and admit which one you usually fall into. For a month, in the situations where it arises, lean deliberately the other way and take note of what happens. Aristotle's claim is that the act comes first and the feeling follows. Doing the brave thing while afraid is how fear loosens. Over time the right response stops requiring effort and becomes what you are like. He also reminds his readers that a person is made by many days, not one.
+ Further reading: Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book 2.
