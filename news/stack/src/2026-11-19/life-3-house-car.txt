=== life | Your breaker panel: what it does and what it is telling you | House systems
> The grey box on the wall is the safety system for every wire in the house. Knowing how to read it and reset it is basic household literacy.
# What the panel is
Electricity enters a house through one large cable and is divided at the panel into many separate circuits, each serving a few rooms or a single large appliance. Every circuit passes through its own breaker, a switch that turns itself off if too much current flows. At the top is a main breaker that cuts power to the whole house. In North America, typical homes have service of 100 to 200 amps. The panel is the one place where you can shut everything off.
# Why breakers exist
Wire heats up as current passes through it, and a wire carrying more than it is rated for can get hot enough to start a fire inside a wall. Each breaker is matched to the wire it protects. A 15 amp breaker protects the thinner wire used for lighting and ordinary outlets. A 20 amp breaker protects the heavier wire used in kitchens and bathrooms. When the current exceeds the rating, the breaker trips before the wire overheats.
# The arithmetic of an overload
In a 120 volt system, a 15 amp circuit can deliver 1,800 watts, and for loads that run for hours the safe figure is about 1,440. A portable heater draws about 1,500 watts by itself. A hair dryer draws nearly the same. Run two such appliances on one circuit and the breaker will trip, exactly as designed. The usual cause of a tripped breaker is not a fault. It is too many things plugged into the same circuit.
# How to reset one
A tripped breaker sits in a middle position or has moved to off, and some show a coloured flag. Unplug or switch off what was running on that circuit. Push the breaker handle firmly all the way to off, then back to on. It should stay. If it trips again immediately with nothing connected, there is a fault in the wiring or the breaker, and that is a job for an electrician. Do not keep resetting it.
# Ground fault protection
Some outlets and breakers have test and reset buttons. These are ground fault interrupters. They compare the current going out with the current coming back, and if a few thousandths of an amp go missing, perhaps through a person, they cut the power in a fraction of a second. They are required near water: kitchens, bathrooms, garages and outdoors. Press the test button every month or so. The power should cut off, and reset should restore it.
# Arc fault protection
Newer homes also have arc fault breakers, which listen electronically for the signature of sparking in damaged wires or loose connections, a common cause of house fires. They can be tripped by some older appliances and tools. If one trips repeatedly on a particular device, the device may be at fault. If it trips for no evident reason, have the circuit checked. It may be detecting a genuine problem inside a wall.
# Label the circuits
Many panels have a chart inside the door that is blank, wrong or illegible. An afternoon spent correcting it is well used. Turn off one breaker at a time and note what stops working, with a lamp or radio plugged in as an indicator. Write the result clearly. In an emergency, or when you simply want to change a light fitting safely, knowing which breaker controls what saves time and removes guesswork.
# When to call a professional
Some signs should never be ignored: a breaker that feels hot, a burning or fishy smell near the panel or an outlet, buzzing or crackling, scorch marks, or lights that dim sharply when an appliance starts. Never replace a breaker with a larger one to stop it tripping, since that removes the protection for the wire. Resetting breakers and labelling circuits are tasks for a householder. Work inside the panel is for a licensed electrician.

=== life | Where your water shuts off, and why to find it today | House systems
> A burst pipe can release hundreds of litres an hour. The difference between a wet floor and a ruined house is knowing where one valve is.
# The main shutoff
Every house has a valve that stops all water entering it. It is usually found where the supply pipe comes through the foundation or floor, often in a basement, crawl space, utility room or garage, and commonly near the water meter. In warm climates it may be outside on an exterior wall. There is often a second valve at the meter by the street, which belongs to the water company. Find yours now, while nothing is leaking.
# Two kinds of valve
Older houses tend to have gate valves, which have a round handle like a garden tap and take several full turns clockwise to close. Newer ones have ball valves, with a straight lever. A ball valve is open when the lever lies along the pipe and closed when it is turned a quarter turn to sit across it. Gate valves that have not been touched in years can stick or fail to close fully.
# Test it
Once a year, close the main valve and open a tap to confirm that the flow stops. A valve that is stuck, or one that lets water continue to trickle, should be replaced by a plumber before you need it. Do not force a seized gate valve, since the stem can snap. Hang a tag on the valve so that anyone in the household, or a neighbour looking after the house, can identify it quickly.
# Fixture valves
Most sinks and toilets have their own small shutoff valves on the pipes beneath them, and washing machines have a pair behind them. These let you isolate one fixture without cutting water to the whole house. If a toilet is overflowing or a tap connector is spraying, turn the small valve under it clockwise until it stops. These valves also seize with age, and they are worth turning off and on once in a while.
# If a pipe bursts
The order of action matters. Shut off the main valve first. Then open the lowest cold tap in the house to drain what remains in the pipes, and turn off the water heater so that it does not run dry while heating. If water is near electrical outlets or appliances, switch off the affected circuits at the panel before stepping into it. Then move belongings, photograph the damage for insurance and call a plumber.
# Frozen pipes
Water expands when it freezes and can split a pipe. The split often goes unnoticed until the thaw, when the leak begins. Pipes in unheated spaces and outside walls are most at risk. In a severe cold spell, let vulnerable taps drip slightly, open cupboard doors under sinks to let warm air in and keep the heating on if you are away. Garden taps should be shut off from inside and drained before winter.
# The silent leaks
Not all leaks are dramatic. A toilet that runs between flushes can waste a great deal of water in a month. Put a few drops of food colouring in the cistern and wait fifteen minutes without flushing. If colour appears in the bowl, the rubber flapper valve is leaking, and replacing it is a cheap, simple job. To check the whole house, turn off every tap and watch the meter. If it is still moving, water is escaping somewhere.
# Going away
For any absence longer than a few days, consider closing the main valve before you leave. A failed washing machine hose or a leaking connector in an empty house can run for a week. With the water off, the worst case is whatever was already in the pipes. Some homeowners fit leak sensors or automatic shutoff valves that close the supply when water is detected on the floor, which insurers increasingly encourage.

=== life | The furnace filter: the cheapest maintenance in the house | House systems
> A cardboard rectangle protects the most expensive appliance you own. Changing it on time takes two minutes and prevents a surprising number of breakdowns.
# What the filter is for
In a forced-air heating and cooling system, a fan pulls air from the rooms, pushes it across a heating or cooling element and sends it back through the ducts. The filter sits in the path of the returning air. Its original and main purpose is to protect the equipment: to keep dust from coating the fan and the coils. Cleaner air for the occupants is a welcome secondary benefit, depending on the grade of filter fitted.
# What a clogged filter does
As a filter loads with dust, less air can pass through it. The fan works harder and uses more electricity. In cooling season, reduced airflow lets the cooling coil get too cold, and it can ice over and stop working. In heating season, the furnace runs too hot and a safety switch shuts it down. Many service calls for a system that has stopped heating or cooling end with the technician pulling out a filter that is grey and matted.
# The rating scale
Filters are graded on a scale called MERV, which for household products runs from about 1 to 16. Higher numbers capture smaller particles. Cheap fibreglass filters, rated around 1 to 4, stop lint and little else. Pleated filters rated about 8 catch most dust, pollen and mould spores and suit the majority of homes. Filters rated 11 to 13 capture finer particles, including much of the smoke from wildfires, and are favoured by people with allergies.
# Higher is not always better
A denser filter resists airflow more. Some systems, especially older ones with the common one-inch filter slot, were not designed for a high-rated filter and can suffer the same problems as with a clogged one. If you want finer filtration, check the equipment's manual or ask a technician. Systems built with a thicker filter cabinet, taking a filter four or five inches deep, handle high ratings easily, because the larger surface area offsets the resistance.
# How often
For a standard one-inch pleated filter, check monthly and expect to replace it every one to three months. Homes with pets, smokers, construction dust nearby or heavy use need the shorter interval. Thicker filters last six months to a year. The simplest test is to hold the filter up to a light. If you cannot see light through it, replace it. Setting a recurring reminder on your phone works better than relying on memory.
# Changing it
Turn the system off at the thermostat. Find the filter, which sits either behind a return air grille in a wall or ceiling or in a slot beside the furnace or air handler. Slide the old one out and note the size printed on its frame. Slide the new one in with the arrow on its frame pointing in the direction the air travels, toward the fan. Fitting it backwards makes it less effective and can cause it to collapse.
# The savings
The United States Department of Energy estimates that replacing a dirty filter with a clean one can lower an air conditioner's energy use by 5 to 15 percent. The larger saving is in avoided repairs and longer equipment life, since strained fans and overheating furnaces fail early. Buying filters in multi-packs and writing the installation date on the frame with a marker makes the habit easy to keep.
# The rest of the system
While you are there, a few other checks help. Keep supply and return vents clear of furniture and rugs. Keep the outdoor unit of an air conditioner free of leaves and with clear space around it. Have the system inspected by a technician every year or two. Any fuel-burning furnace should be accompanied by working carbon monoxide alarms in the home, tested regularly, since the gas has no smell and can be fatal.

=== life | Car maintenance: the short list that matters | Car care
> Modern cars are far more reliable than their reputation, provided a handful of items are attended to on time. The schedule is in the glovebox.
# The manual is the authority
Every car comes with a maintenance schedule in the owner's manual, written by the engineers who designed it. It lists what should be inspected or replaced and at what mileage or age. It overrides general advice from friends, forums and service counters. Many cars also calculate oil life and display a reminder. If you have lost the manual, the manufacturer's website normally provides it. Reading the schedule once takes ten minutes and tells you what the car actually needs.
# Oil
Oil lubricates and cools the engine and carries away contaminants, and it degrades with use. The old rule of changing it every 3,000 miles dates from an earlier era. Modern engines using synthetic oil commonly go 5,000 to 10,000 miles between changes, as their manuals specify. Check the level with the dipstick once a month, on level ground with the engine off. Running an engine low on oil is one of the few ways to destroy it quickly.
# Other fluids
Coolant keeps the engine from overheating and from freezing, and is replaced at long intervals. Brake fluid absorbs moisture from the air over time, which lowers its boiling point and corrodes parts, so many manufacturers call for replacing it every two or three years regardless of mileage. Transmission fluid has its own schedule. Windscreen washer fluid simply needs topping up. Never open the coolant cap on a hot engine, since the system is under pressure.
# Filters and belts
The engine air filter keeps dust out of the cylinders and is typically replaced every 15,000 to 30,000 miles, sooner in dusty conditions. The cabin air filter cleans what you breathe. Both are usually easy to change yourself. Engines with a rubber timing belt need it replaced at the interval in the manual, commonly somewhere between 60,000 and 100,000 miles. If it breaks, many engines suffer severe internal damage, so this one should not be postponed.
# Brakes
Brake pads wear with every stop. Most have a small metal tab that begins to squeal against the disc when the pad is nearly finished. A grinding noise means the pad material is gone and metal is cutting into the disc, which turns a routine job into an expensive one. A brake pedal that feels soft, sinks or pulses, or a car that pulls to one side under braking, should be inspected promptly.
# The battery
A car battery typically lasts three to five years, less in very hot climates. It gives little warning. The usual signs are slow cranking on a cold morning and dimming lights at idle. Many parts shops test batteries without charge. Replacing a battery at four or five years, before it leaves you stranded, is reasonable. Keep the terminals clean and tight. A white or green crust on them can be scrubbed away with a brush.
# Warning lights
A steady check engine light means the car's computer has recorded a fault, often minor, and it should be investigated soon. A flashing check engine light indicates an engine misfire that can damage the exhaust system, and calls for easing off and getting the car seen quickly. A red oil pressure or temperature warning means stop as soon as it is safe and switch off. An inexpensive reader plugged into the diagnostic port under the dashboard displays the stored fault codes.
# Keep a record
Write down every service with the date and mileage, and keep the receipts. A record tells you what is due, prevents you from paying for the same work twice and adds real value when you sell. It also helps when dealing with a garage. When a service adviser recommends something, compare it with the schedule in the manual. If it is not listed there, ask what specific problem it addresses before agreeing.

=== life | Tyres: pressure, tread and the codes on the sidewall | Car care
> Four patches of rubber, each about the size of a hand, are all that connect a car to the road. Five minutes a month keeps them doing their job.
# The right pressure
The correct pressure for your tyres is printed on a sticker inside the driver's door frame, and in the manual. It is not the number moulded into the tyre's sidewall, which is the maximum the tyre can hold. The door figure was chosen by the car maker to balance grip, comfort, wear and fuel economy for that vehicle. Front and rear pressures sometimes differ, and there may be a higher figure for a full load.
# Check it cold, monthly
Tyres lose a little air all the time, and pressure also changes with temperature, dropping by roughly one pound per square inch for every 10 degrees Fahrenheit the air cools. Check once a month and before long trips, when the tyres are cold, meaning before driving or after only a short distance. A digital gauge costs very little and is more reliable than the ones on forecourt air lines. Include the spare if the car has one.
# What low pressure does
An under-inflated tyre flexes more as it rolls. That builds heat, wears the outer edges of the tread, increases fuel consumption and lengthens stopping distances. In the worst case, heat causes the tyre to fail at speed. The dashboard warning light for tyre pressure generally comes on only when a tyre is about a quarter below the recommended figure. By then it has been low for some time, so the light is a backstop, not a substitute for checking.
# Tread depth
Tread grooves exist to channel water out from under the tyre. As they wear shallow, the tyre begins to ride on top of the water in heavy rain and grip disappears. In most of the United States, the legal minimum is two thirty-seconds of an inch. Tyres have small raised bars in the grooves that become level with the tread at that depth. Wet braking deteriorates well before the legal limit, and many safety groups recommend replacing at four thirty-seconds.
# The coin test
You can check tread without a gauge. Put an American penny into a groove with Lincoln's head pointing down. If you can see the top of his head, the tread is at or below the legal minimum. Do the same with a quarter. If the top of Washington's head is visible, the tread is below four thirty-seconds and wet grip is reduced. Check several places across each tyre, since uneven wear is common.
# What uneven wear means
The pattern of wear is diagnostic. Wear on both outer edges points to under-inflation. Wear down the centre points to over-inflation. Wear on one edge only suggests the wheels are out of alignment. Scalloped or patchy wear can indicate worn suspension parts or an unbalanced wheel. Rotating the tyres between positions at the interval in the manual, typically every 5,000 to 8,000 miles, evens out wear and extends their life.
# Reading the sidewall
A marking such as 225/60R17 describes the size. The first number is the width in millimetres. The second is the height of the sidewall as a percentage of the width. R means radial construction, and the last number is the wheel diameter in inches. Elsewhere, after the letters DOT, a four-digit code gives the week and year of manufacture. A tyre marked 2321 was made in the twenty-third week of 2021.
# Age matters too
Rubber hardens and cracks with age, whatever the tread depth. Many vehicle and tyre manufacturers recommend replacing tyres somewhere between six and ten years after manufacture, even if they look serviceable. This catches people out on cars that are driven little, and on spares and trailers. Look for fine cracks in the sidewalls and bulges, which indicate internal damage. A bulging tyre should be replaced before the car is driven any distance.

=== life | How to jump-start a car safely | Car care
> A flat battery is the most common breakdown there is. The procedure is simple, and the order of the four connections is the part that matters.
# Is it the battery
The classic signs of a flat battery are a rapid clicking when you turn the key, an engine that turns over slowly and gives up, or no response at all with the dashboard lights dim or dead. If the lights are bright and the engine cranks strongly but will not fire, the battery is probably not the problem and a jump will not help. Interior lights left on, short trips in cold weather and an ageing battery are the usual causes.
# Before you connect anything
Check your owner's manual first, since some cars have special jump-start terminals under the bonnet instead of at the battery, and hybrids and electric cars have their own rules. Do not attempt to jump a battery that is cracked, leaking or frozen. Park the working car close without the two vehicles touching. Switch off both engines and all accessories, set the parking brakes and put both cars in park or neutral.
# The four connections
Jump leads have a red pair of clamps and a black pair. Connect in this order. First, a red clamp to the positive terminal of the flat battery, marked with a plus sign. Second, the other red clamp to the positive terminal of the good battery. Third, a black clamp to the negative terminal of the good battery. Fourth, the last black clamp to a bare, unpainted metal part of the engine or frame of the car with the flat battery, away from the battery itself.
# Why the last clamp goes on metal
The final connection completes the circuit and often makes a small spark. A battery being charged can give off hydrogen gas, which is flammable. Making that last connection on a solid metal part some distance from the battery keeps any spark away from the gas. It is a small precaution with a real purpose, and it is the step most often skipped. Keep the clamps from touching each other once any of them is connected.
# Starting
Start the engine of the working car and let it run for a few minutes to put some charge into the flat battery. Then try to start the other car. If it does not catch, wait a few more minutes and try again. Do not crank for more than a few seconds at a time. If it refuses after several attempts, the battery may be beyond recovery, or the fault lies elsewhere, such as the starter motor or a bad connection.
# Disconnecting
Once the car is running, remove the leads in the reverse order: black from the metal ground, black from the good battery, red from the good battery, red from the revived one. Keep the clamps apart as you go. Then drive the revived car for at least twenty to thirty minutes so that the alternator can recharge the battery. Short trips will not do it, and the car may fail to start again next time.
# Afterwards
A battery that went flat because a light was left on will usually recover fully. One that went flat for no clear reason is probably near the end of its life, especially if it is more than four years old. Have it tested. If a fairly new battery keeps going flat, the charging system or a hidden electrical drain may be responsible, and a garage can measure both quickly.
# The portable alternative
Compact lithium jump starters, about the size of a paperback book, have largely removed the need for a second car. You connect the unit's clamps to the battery, wait a moment and start the engine. They hold their charge for months, though they should be topped up a few times a year, and most also charge phones. For anyone who drives alone or in remote places, keeping one in the boot is inexpensive insurance.
