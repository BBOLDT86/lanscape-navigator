=== money | How banks create money | Economics in plain words
> Most money is not printed by governments. It comes into existence when a bank makes a loan, which sounds like a trick and is simply how the system works.
# What a bank does
A bank takes deposits and makes loans. It pays depositors a low rate of interest, charges borrowers a higher one and lives on the difference. The usual picture is that the bank lends out the money savers have brought in. That picture is not quite right, and the correction matters. When a bank lends, it does not hand over someone else's savings. It creates a new deposit in the borrower's account.
# Loans create deposits
Suppose a bank approves a loan of 20,000 dollars for a car. It credits the borrower's account with 20,000. No other customer's balance goes down. The bank has gained an asset, the loan it is owed, and an equal liability, the new deposit. The borrower pays the car dealer, whose own bank account rises. There is now 20,000 dollars more money in the economy than there was. The Bank of England described the process in exactly these terms in a 2014 paper.
# Most money is bank deposits
Notes and coins are a small part of the money supply. In modern economies, the great majority of what people use as money is balances in bank accounts, and those balances were created by lending. When loans are repaid, the money is extinguished. The total quantity of money therefore expands when banks lend freely and shrinks when they pull back. That is why a banking crisis so quickly becomes a crisis for the whole economy.
# What limits the process
Banks cannot create money without end. A bank must find borrowers who are willing to borrow and likely to repay, because bad loans destroy it. Regulators require it to hold a cushion of its own capital against losses, in proportion to its lending. It needs enough ready funds to meet withdrawals and payments to other banks. And the interest rate set by the central bank governs how much borrowers want to borrow at all.
# The central bank
Each country or currency area has a central bank, such as the Federal Reserve in the United States. It is the bank for banks. Commercial banks hold accounts with it and settle payments among themselves across those accounts. Its chief tool is the short-term interest rate, which influences every other rate in the economy. Raising it makes borrowing dearer and slows the creation of money. Lowering it does the reverse. That is how it leans against inflation or recession.
# Why banks are fragile
A bank's deposits can be withdrawn on demand, while its loans are repaid over years. If all depositors asked for their money on the same day, no bank could pay, however sound. Normally they do not. But if depositors fear a bank is in trouble, the sensible move for each one is to get out first, and the rush itself can bring down a healthy bank. This is a bank run, and for centuries runs were a regular feature of economic life.
# The safety net
Two inventions largely ended ordinary runs. Deposit insurance promises that depositors will be repaid up to a limit even if their bank fails, which removes the reason to panic. In the United States, the limit is 250,000 dollars per depositor at each insured bank. The central bank also acts as lender of last resort, supplying emergency funds to solvent banks that are short of cash. In return for this protection, banks accept regulation of how much risk they take.
# Why this matters to you
Understanding the mechanism clears up several puzzles. It explains why interest rate decisions affect house prices and employment. It explains why governments rescue banks that seem to deserve failure, since the alternative is a sudden contraction of money and credit for everyone. And it explains why it is prudent to keep balances within the insured limit at any one institution. Money in a bank is a claim on that bank, made safe by insurance.

=== money | Opportunity cost and sunk cost | Economics in plain words
> Two ideas from economics improve almost any decision. One is about the options you give up. The other is about the money you have already spent.
# The real cost of anything
The cost of a choice is not only the money it takes. It is the best alternative you gave up to make it. Economists call this opportunity cost. An evening at the cinema costs the ticket and whatever else you would have done with those hours. A year at university costs the fees plus the salary you did not earn. Because the alternative never appears on a bill, people habitually leave it out of the reckoning.
# Money has alternatives
Every dollar can be used once. Spending 5,000 dollars on a holiday means not investing it, not paying down a loan and not buying anything else. That does not make the holiday a mistake. It means the proper comparison is between the holiday and the best other use of the money. Businesses apply the same test when they ask whether a project earns more than the funds could earn elsewhere. A profitable project can still be the wrong one.
# Time works the same way
Time is more limited than money, so its opportunity cost is higher. Saying yes to one commitment is saying no to whatever else could have filled those hours. A person who drives across town to save 10 dollars has sold an hour for 10 dollars, whether or not they see it that way. Working out roughly what an hour of your time is worth to you makes many small decisions about queuing, repairing and bargain-hunting much easier.
# Free is not free
Things with no price still have a cost. A free concert costs the evening. A free sample costs your attention and perhaps your email address. Storing an unused item costs the space and the money you could have had by selling it. The useful question about anything is what you are giving up to have it. When the honest answer is nothing you would rather have, the decision is easy.
# Sunk costs
A sunk cost is money or effort already spent that cannot be recovered whatever you do next. Rational decisions look only forward, at the costs and benefits still to come. What has been spent is the same under every option and should not affect the choice. People find this very hard. Having paid for something, they feel obliged to use it, and having started something, they feel obliged to finish, to avoid admitting the earlier outlay was wasted.
# The fallacy in action
You have paid for a ticket to a show, and on the night you feel ill and do not want to go. The money is gone either way. The only live question is whether the evening would be better spent at the show or at home. Yet many people go, to get their money's worth. Companies do the same with failing projects. Britain and France kept funding the Concorde airliner long after it was clear it would never repay its costs.
# How to catch it
A reliable test is to imagine arriving at the situation fresh. If someone handed you this half-finished project today, at no cost, would you choose to put in what is needed to complete it? If you did not already own this share, would you buy it at today's price? If the answer is no, the past investment is the only thing holding you there. Phrases such as we have come too far to stop now are a warning sign.
# Using them together
The two ideas are partners. Sunk cost reasoning tells you to ignore what cannot be changed. Opportunity cost reasoning tells you what to compare instead: the best available use of your remaining money and time from this point on. Together they amount to one instruction. Look only forward, and compare the option in front of you with the best alternative, not with nothing and not with the past.

=== money | Supply, demand and elasticity | Economics in plain words
> Prices are not set by sellers alone or by costs alone. They emerge from two forces meeting, and one extra idea explains why some prices swing wildly.
# Two curves
Demand describes how much of something buyers want at each price. As a rule, the lower the price, the more they want. Supply describes how much sellers are willing to offer at each price. As a rule, the higher the price, the more they offer. There is one price at which the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers offer. Markets tend toward that price, without anyone being in charge of finding it.
# How the price is found
If the price is above that level, sellers offer more than buyers want. Unsold goods pile up, and sellers cut prices to move them. If the price is below it, buyers want more than is available. Shelves empty, queues form and sellers find they can charge more. The price settles where neither pressure remains. No one needs to know the whole picture. Each buyer and seller responds only to the price in front of them.
# Shifts
Prices change when something moves one of the curves. A fashion for a product, rising incomes or a higher price for a substitute increases demand, and the price rises. A good harvest, a new production method or new competitors increase supply, and the price falls. A frost that destroys part of a coffee crop reduces supply and raises the price of coffee everywhere. Most business news about prices is a story about one of these shifts.
# Elasticity
How strongly buyers react to a price change is called the price elasticity of demand. If a 10 percent rise in price causes people to buy 20 percent less, demand is elastic. If it causes them to buy only 2 percent less, demand is inelastic. The difference matters enormously to sellers. With elastic demand, raising the price reduces total revenue. With inelastic demand, raising the price increases it.
# What makes demand inelastic
Three things make buyers insensitive to price. The good is a necessity. There are no close substitutes. And it takes a small share of the buyer's budget. Petrol in the short run fits the first two: people must still get to work, and the car they own runs on nothing else, so a large price rise cuts consumption only slightly. Salt fits all three. A particular brand of breakfast cereal fits none, since a dozen alternatives sit beside it.
# Time changes everything
Demand becomes more elastic as time passes. When fuel prices rise, drivers can do little at first. Over several years they buy more efficient cars, move closer to work or change how they travel, and consumption falls much further. Supply behaves the same way. In the short run, a farmer cannot grow more wheat than was planted. Given a few seasons of high prices, more land is sown. This is why price spikes in commodities tend to be sharp and temporary.
# Volatile markets
When both supply and demand are inelastic, small disturbances produce large price swings. If buyers must have the product and sellers cannot quickly produce more, a modest shortfall requires a big price rise to bring the two back into balance. Oil, electricity, some foods and housing in constrained cities behave this way. Markets where buyers can easily switch and sellers can easily expand, such as most manufactured goods, have far steadier prices.
# What prices do
A price is a signal and an incentive at once. A rising price tells buyers to economize and tells producers to supply more, without either needing to know why the shortage arose. When prices are prevented from moving, the adjustment must happen in another way, usually through queues, shortages or surpluses. Whether to accept those effects in exchange for other goals, such as affordability or fairness, is a matter for politics. The mechanism itself is not seriously disputed.

=== money | Insurance: what is worth insuring and what is not | Your money
> Insurance loses money on average, by design. It is still one of the most sensible purchases you can make, for the right risks only.
# Pooling risk
Insurance works by pooling. A thousand households each face a small chance of a house fire in a given year. For any one of them, the loss would be ruinous. For the group, the number of fires is fairly predictable. Each household pays a modest premium, and the pooled money rebuilds the few houses that burn. An unpredictable catastrophe for the individual is turned into a small, certain cost shared by all.
# You should expect to lose
Premiums must cover the claims paid out plus the insurer's staff, offices, advertising and profit. So customers as a group pay in more than they get back. Measured purely by expected value, buying insurance is a losing bet. That is not a criticism. You are paying a fee to be rid of a risk you could not bear. The purchase makes sense when the possible loss would wreck your finances and little sense when it would not.
# Insure against ruin
The principle that follows is to insure against losses you could not absorb and to carry the rest yourself. A house burning down, a serious illness or injury, being held liable for harming someone else, or the death or disability of a family's earner are all capable of destroying a household's finances. These are worth insuring fully. A cracked phone screen or a failed toaster is an annoyance that savings can cover.
# Small policies are poor value
Extended warranties on appliances and electronics, insurance on individual gadgets and similar small policies are generally expensive relative to the risk they cover, since sales commissions and administration absorb much of the premium. Consumer organizations have long advised against most of them. If you decline all such offers and put the money aside, you will almost certainly come out ahead over the years, and you will have the cash for the occasional repair.
# Deductibles
The deductible, or excess, is the part of any claim you pay yourself. A higher one lowers the premium, often considerably, because it removes the small claims that are costly for an insurer to process. This is the same principle again. Choose the highest deductible you could comfortably pay from savings, and let the policy protect you against the large losses. An emergency fund and a high deductible work well together.
# Problems insurers face
Two difficulties shape every policy. People who know they are at higher risk are keener to buy cover, which pushes up the average cost. Insurers respond with questionnaires, medical checks and exclusions. And people who are insured may take less care, since someone else will pay. Insurers respond with deductibles and conditions. Much of the fine print that irritates customers exists to manage these two problems.
# Reading a policy
Three items deserve attention before you buy. The limit is the most the insurer will pay, and it should match the loss you actually face. The exclusions list what is not covered, and floods, earthquakes and wear are commonly left out of standard home policies. The basis of payment says whether you receive what an item would cost to replace or only its depreciated value. Comparing quotes on price alone, without these, compares different products.
# A sensible approach
List the events that could seriously damage your finances and check that each is covered up to a realistic amount. For most households, that means health, home or contents, motor liability, and life and disability cover if others depend on your income. Raise deductibles to what you can afford. Decline cover for small things. Review the whole arrangement every couple of years or after any major change in your life, and seek qualified advice when the amounts are large.

=== money | Dollar-cost averaging: investing on a schedule | Your money
> Putting in a fixed amount at regular intervals removes the need to choose the right moment. What it achieves, and what it does not, is often misunderstood.
# The method
Dollar-cost averaging means investing the same sum of money at fixed intervals, whatever the price happens to be. Three hundred dollars on the first of each month into the same fund, for example. You make no attempt to judge whether the market is high or low. The decision is made once and then carried out automatically. Anyone who contributes to a retirement plan from each pay cheque is already doing it.
# A built-in advantage
Because the amount is fixed, it buys more units when the price is low and fewer when it is high. Suppose you invest 100 dollars in each of three months at prices of 10, 5 and 10 dollars. You buy 10 units, then 20, then 10. You hold 40 units for 300 dollars, an average cost of 7.50 each. The average price over the period was 8.33. Your cost is lower because your money automatically bought the most when the price was lowest.
# The behavioural benefit
The larger advantage is psychological. Investors are tempted to wait for a better moment, to buy after prices have risen and to sell in a panic after they have fallen. An automatic schedule takes those decisions away. A falling market becomes a chance to buy more units for the same money. People who invest by standing order tend to keep going through downturns that drive others out of the market at the worst time.
# With a lump sum
A different question arises when you already have a large sum, such as an inheritance. Should you invest it all now or feed it in over a year? A much-cited study by the fund company Vanguard, covering several decades of market history in three countries, found that investing at once did better about two thirds of the time. The reason is simple. Markets rise more often than they fall, so money waiting on the sidelines usually misses some growth.
# Why people still phase it in
The remaining third of the time, investing all at once is followed by a fall, and the experience is painful. Spreading a lump sum over several months reduces the chance of that outcome and the regret that comes with it. It costs a little expected return in exchange for a calmer mind. If the alternative to phasing is being too anxious to invest at all, phasing is clearly the better course. Choose a schedule in advance and keep to it.
# What it does not do
Dollar-cost averaging does not protect against loss. If the investment falls and stays down, you lose money, having bought all the way down. It does not improve a poor investment. And it works only if you continue during declines, which is when it feels worst and achieves most. Stopping contributions when prices fall throws away the whole advantage of the method.
# Making it automatic
The practical value lies in removing yourself from the process. Arrange for the transfer to happen on the day you are paid, before the money can be spent elsewhere. Choose a low-cost, broadly diversified fund. Increase the amount when your income rises. Then look at the account rarely. The fewer decisions you have to take, the fewer opportunities there are to take a bad one.
# The bigger picture
Arguments about the best timing involve small differences. How much you save, how long you remain invested and how little you pay in costs matter far more. A person who invests a modest amount every month for thirty years, in good markets and bad, will almost certainly do better than one who spends those years waiting for the ideal moment. A regular habit is unexciting, and that is its strength.

=== money | Bubbles: from tulips to dot-coms | Manias and crashes
> Every few decades, the price of something rises far beyond what it can be worth, and then collapses. The stories differ in detail and are alike in structure.
# What a bubble is
A bubble is a sharp rise in the price of an asset that is driven by the expectation of further rises, not by what the asset earns or is used for. People buy because prices are going up, and their buying pushes prices up further. The process sustains itself until new buyers run out. Then prices fall, usually much faster than they rose. Bubbles are easy to identify afterwards and notoriously hard to call at the time.
# Tulips
The earliest famous example is from the Dutch Republic in the 1630s. Rare tulip bulbs, especially those with striped petals, became luxury goods, and contracts to buy them changed hands at soaring prices. At the peak, a single prized bulb was reported to cost as much as a fine house. In February 1637 buyers failed to appear at an auction, and the market collapsed within days. Modern historians think the wider economic harm was small and that later moralists exaggerated the tale.
# The South Sea
In 1720 the South Sea Company, which held a British trading monopoly of little practical value, offered to take over much of the national debt in exchange for shares. Its share price rose roughly eightfold in a few months, helped by bribes and by lending investors the money to buy. Dozens of imitation companies were floated. By the autumn, the price had collapsed. Among those who lost heavily was Isaac Newton, by then master of the Royal Mint.
# Railways and radio
Bubbles often attach to real technological change. In 1840s Britain, investors poured money into railway companies, many of which planned lines that were never built. Shares crashed, yet the country was left with a rail network. In the late 1920s, shares in radio and car companies soared before the crash of 1929. The technology in each case did transform the world. The investors who paid peak prices mostly lost their money all the same.
# The dot-com boom
In the late 1990s, the arrival of the internet set off a rush into technology shares. Companies with no profits and little revenue were valued in the billions. The Nasdaq index of American technology shares roughly doubled in the year before its peak in March 2000. Over the next two and a half years it fell by almost 80 percent, and it did not return to that peak for fifteen years. The internet proved even more important than its enthusiasts claimed.
# The common pattern
Historians of finance describe a recurring sequence. Something genuinely new appears and creates real profits. Prices rise and attract attention. Credit becomes easy, and people borrow to buy. A story spreads that old rules of value no longer apply. Newcomers with no experience join in, encouraged by neighbours who have made money. Insiders begin to sell. A small shock or simply a pause sets off a rush for the exit.
# Why clever people join in
Bubbles do not require stupidity. A professional manager who stays out while prices double will lose clients long before being proved right. Selling early looks like an error for months or years. Many participants know that prices are too high and hope to sell to someone else before the fall. A saying often attributed to the economist John Maynard Keynes holds that a market can stay irrational for longer than an investor can stay solvent.
# What an ordinary investor can do
Nobody can reliably time the top, so the defences are structural. Do not borrow to invest in something whose price has recently soared. Be wary when the main argument for buying is that the price has gone up. Be suspicious of any claim that this time the old measures do not apply. Hold a diversified portfolio so that no single enthusiasm can sink you. And accept that you will sometimes watch other people get rich quickly, for a while.
