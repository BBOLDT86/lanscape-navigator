=== world | The Black Death: the plague that remade Europe | Turning points
> Between 1347 and 1351 a disease killed something like a third to a half of Europe's people. The survivors inherited a different world.
# Arrival
In October 1347, trading ships from the Black Sea put in at Messina in Sicily with dead and dying sailors aboard. The disease they carried had been moving west across Asia for years along the caravan routes. From Sicily it spread through the ports of the Mediterranean and then inland along roads and rivers. By 1348 it had reached Paris and London. By 1350 it was in Scandinavia. Within about four years it had passed through almost every part of Europe.
# The disease
The cause was a bacterium, Yersinia pestis, carried by fleas that live on rats and other rodents. Bitten people developed fever and painful swellings in the groin, armpit or neck, called buboes, which gave bubonic plague its name. Around half of those infected died, usually within a week. When the infection reached the lungs, it spread directly from person to person through coughing, and that form was almost always fatal. DNA recovered from medieval plague burials has confirmed the identification.
# The scale
Nobody counted the dead precisely, but historians estimate that Europe lost between a third and a half of its population in those few years, tens of millions of people. Some towns and villages were emptied entirely. Florence may have lost more than half its inhabitants. Monasteries, where people lived close together, were devastated. There were too few living to bury the dead properly, and mass graves were dug outside city walls.
# How people understood it
With no knowledge of germs, people looked for other explanations. Physicians blamed corrupted air or an unfavourable alignment of the planets. Many saw divine punishment, and processions of penitents whipped themselves through the streets. Others looked for someone to blame. Jewish communities were accused of poisoning wells and were massacred in many towns across the Rhineland and beyond, despite a papal statement that the accusation was false.
# Quarantine
Some responses did help. Italian ports began to isolate arriving ships and travellers before allowing them ashore. The city of Ragusa, now Dubrovnik, introduced a thirty-day isolation period in 1377, and Venice later extended it to forty days. The Italian for forty days gave us the word quarantine. Officials did not know why isolation worked. They had observed that it did, and public health boards with real powers were among the lasting institutions the plague produced.
# Labour becomes scarce
The economic effects were profound. With so many dead, workers were suddenly in short supply and land was plentiful. Surviving peasants and labourers could demand higher wages and better terms, or leave for a lord who offered more. Authorities tried to stop this. England passed a law in 1351 to hold wages at their earlier level. It was widely evaded, and resentment of such measures fed the great English peasant revolt of 1381.
# Longer consequences
Historians continue to argue over how far the plague altered the course of European history. Many hold that higher wages and scarce labour weakened serfdom in western Europe and encouraged labour-saving methods. The shock to the Church's authority, when prayer and clergy proved powerless, may have prepared ground for later religious upheaval. What is certain is that the population of Europe took roughly a century and a half or more to recover its earlier level.
# It came back
The Black Death was the worst wave of a pandemic that kept returning. Outbreaks struck somewhere in Europe every few years for the next three centuries, including the Great Plague of London in 1665. The disease still exists in wild rodent populations on several continents and causes a small number of human cases each year. Today it is treated with common antibiotics, and when treated promptly it is rarely fatal.

=== world | Why Rome fell | Turning points
> The western Roman Empire did not collapse on a single day. It came apart over about two centuries, and historians have proposed more than two hundred reasons why.
# What actually ended
The traditional date is the year 476, when a general named Odoacer deposed the last western emperor, a boy called Romulus Augustulus, and did not bother to replace him. By then the western empire controlled little beyond Italy. Importantly, only half of the empire ended. The eastern half, governed from Constantinople, continued for nearly a thousand years more, until the city fell to the Ottoman Turks in 1453. Its people called themselves Romans to the end.
# A century of crisis
The trouble began much earlier. In the third century the empire nearly disintegrated. In about fifty years there were more than twenty emperors, most of whom died violently. Armies fought each other to install their own generals. Plagues killed large numbers. The government paid its soldiers by reducing the silver in the coinage until it was nearly worthless, producing severe inflation. The empire survived, but as a more militarized, heavily taxed and rigid state.
# An empire too large for one ruler
At the end of that century, the emperor Diocletian concluded that the empire was too big for one man to defend and divided its administration between east and west. Constantine later founded a new capital in the east, in 330, on the site that became Constantinople. The eastern half was richer, more urban and easier to defend. Over time, the two halves grew apart, and the west, with longer frontiers and less money, became the weaker partner.
# Pressure on the frontiers
In the late fourth century, the Huns moved into eastern Europe from the steppe and pushed other peoples before them. Large groups of Goths sought refuge inside the empire. Mistreated by Roman officials, they rebelled, and in 378 they destroyed a Roman army at Adrianople and killed the eastern emperor. From then on, whole peoples under their own kings lived inside the frontiers, sometimes as allies and sometimes as enemies.
# The army changes
Rome increasingly relied on these groups to do its fighting, settling them on land in return for military service. This was cheaper than raising and training Roman recruits, and it worked for a time. But such forces were loyal to their own leaders first. In 410 a Gothic army under Alaric, which had served Rome and felt cheated of its rewards, sacked the city of Rome itself. The shock was felt across the Mediterranean world.
# Losing the tax base
The decisive losses were financial. As provinces passed under the control of Goths, Franks, Vandals and others, their taxes stopped reaching the imperial government. The gravest blow came in 439, when the Vandals seized North Africa, the richest region of the west and the main source of grain for the city of Rome. With less revenue, the state could pay fewer soldiers, and with fewer soldiers it lost more territory. The spiral did not reverse.
# The argument about causes
In the eighteenth century, the English historian Edward Gibbon wrote the most famous account and gave much of the blame to the loss of civic virtue and the rise of Christianity. Few historians now accept that. A German scholar once catalogued 210 different causes that had been proposed. Current explanations emphasize the combination of civil war, fiscal weakness and external pressure. Some historians stress violent collapse. Others see a gradual transformation into the kingdoms of early medieval Europe.
# What was lost and what survived
In the west, the changes were real. Cities shrank dramatically, and the population of Rome itself fell from perhaps a million to a few tens of thousands. Long-distance trade declined, and literacy became rarer. Yet much endured. Roman law, the Latin language and the Christian Church carried on, and the new kings governed with Roman officials and titles. French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese and Romanian are all Latin, spoken continuously and changed over time.

=== world | The Silk Road: the network that joined Eurasia | Turning points
> It was never one road and few travellers crossed all of it. For over fifteen hundred years it carried goods, religions, technologies and diseases across a continent.
# A name invented later
Nobody who used these routes called them the Silk Road. The term was coined in 1877 by a German geographer, Ferdinand von Richthofen. It describes a web of tracks linking China with Central Asia, India, Persia and the Mediterranean, crossing deserts and some of the highest mountain passes on Earth. From the Chinese capital to the eastern Mediterranean was a journey of more than six thousand kilometres. There were also sea routes through the Indian Ocean that carried even more cargo.
# How it began
Chinese accounts date regular contact to the second century before the common era. The Han emperor sent an envoy named Zhang Qian westward to seek allies against nomadic raiders. He was captured and held for years, but he eventually returned with reports of rich kingdoms and superb horses in Central Asia. China extended its military reach westward to protect the routes, and trade followed. At the other end, the Roman Empire was developing an appetite for eastern luxuries.
# Relay trade
Goods crossed the continent in stages. A bale of silk might change hands a dozen times between China and Syria, each merchant carrying it for one section and selling it on at a profit. Very few individuals travelled the entire distance. Merchants from Sogdiana, the region around Samarkand, dominated the central stretches for centuries. Oasis cities such as Kashgar, Dunhuang and Merv grew rich providing water, lodging, markets and protection to the caravans.
# What moved
Silk travelled west and gave the routes their modern name. For centuries only China knew how to make it, and Romans paid for it in gold, to the alarm of some of their writers. Spices, jade, porcelain, lacquer and tea also went west. Glassware, wool, wine, silver and gold went east, along with horses from Central Asia. The trade was in goods light and valuable enough to justify months on the back of a camel.
# Ideas travelled further than goods
The most lasting cargo was not for sale. Buddhism spread from India into Central Asia and reached China along these routes. Later, Christianity in its eastern forms and then Islam moved the same way. Technologies crossed too. Papermaking, invented in China, reached the Islamic world in the eighth century and Europe several centuries later. Printing, gunpowder and the magnetic compass followed. Crops, musical instruments, artistic styles and mathematical ideas all moved along the network.
# The Mongol century
The routes were at their safest in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, when the Mongol Empire controlled most of the territory between China and eastern Europe. A system of relay stations and guarantees for merchants made long journeys practical. The Venetian Marco Polo travelled to China and back in those years, between 1271 and 1295, and his account astonished European readers. A single political authority across the continent lowered the cost and risk of trade dramatically.
# Disease on the caravans
The same connections carried disease. The plague that reached Europe in 1347 is thought to have travelled west along the trade routes from Central Asia in the preceding years. The network that had enriched cities for a millennium delivered the pandemic that killed a large share of their inhabitants. Dense, long-distance links spread whatever enters them, harmful as well as valuable. That observation applies equally to later networks.
# Decline
The overland routes faded for several reasons. The Mongol Empire broke up and the roads became more dangerous. In 1498 Portuguese ships reached India by sailing around Africa, opening a sea route that could carry far more cargo at lower cost. Ocean shipping gradually took over long-distance trade between Europe and Asia. The oasis cities declined, and some were abandoned to the sand, where archaeologists rediscovered them in the twentieth century.

=== world | The Industrial Revolution: when growth began | Turning points
> For most of history, living standards barely changed from one century to the next. Then, in Britain around 1760, something started that has not stopped.
# The flat line
Before the eighteenth century, the average person almost everywhere lived at a level not much above subsistence. When harvests improved, populations grew until the gain per person was used up. Economic historians estimate that average income per head changed little for thousands of years. Then output per person in Britain began to rise and kept rising, decade after decade. That sustained growth, more than any single invention, is what the term Industrial Revolution describes.
# Cotton
The first transformed industry was textiles. Spinning thread by hand was slow, and a series of machines mechanized it from the 1760s: the spinning jenny, which let one worker spin many threads at once, and the water frame, which used water power to do the same on a larger scale. Weaving was mechanized later. Production moved from cottages to factories built beside rivers. The price of cotton cloth fell sharply, and Britain came to supply much of the world.
# Steam
The second ingredient was a new source of power. A crude steam engine had pumped water from mines since 1712. In the 1760s the Scottish instrument maker James Watt redesigned it with a separate chamber to condense the steam, which cut fuel consumption dramatically. Later versions could turn a wheel. Factories no longer needed to stand beside a river. They could be built anywhere coal could be delivered, and they could run in any season.
# Coal and iron
Steam needed coal, and Britain had plenty close to the surface and near water transport. Coal also changed ironmaking. Smelting iron had depended on charcoal from dwindling forests. From the early eighteenth century, ironmasters learned to use coke made from coal instead, and iron became cheap and abundant. Cheap iron made better machines, engines, bridges and rails. Each advance fed the others, which is why the changes came as a cluster.
# Why Britain
Historians have long debated why it started there. Britain had accessible coal, good waterways and a large trading network. Its wages were high compared with other countries while its energy was cheap, which made machines that saved labour and burned coal unusually profitable. It had secure property rights, an active culture of practical experiment and capital to invest. Most scholars see a combination of these factors, and they disagree about which mattered most.
# The human cost
For the first generations of factory workers, the benefits were hard to see. Wages for ordinary workers rose little for decades while output soared. Days of twelve hours or more were standard. Children as young as five or six worked in mills and mines. Cities such as Manchester grew far faster than housing or sanitation, and life expectancy in the poorest districts was shockingly low. Parliament began to restrict child labour in factories in 1833.
# Railways
The steam engine put on wheels changed everything again. The first public railway to use steam locomotives opened in the north of England in 1825, and the line between Liverpool and Manchester followed in 1830. Within twenty years Britain had thousands of miles of track. Journeys that had taken days took hours. Fresh food reached cities, coal reached factories, and standard time was introduced across the country so that timetables could work.
# The world since
From about the middle of the nineteenth century, wages in Britain began to rise steadily, and the new methods spread to Belgium, Germany, the United States and beyond. The countries that industrialized pulled far ahead of those that did not, creating gaps in wealth that still shape the world. Average incomes in industrial countries are now many times what they were in 1750. The burning of fossil fuels that powered all of it is also the origin of modern climate change.

=== world | The crash of 1929 and the Great Depression | Turning points
> The stock market collapse of October 1929 is remembered as the start of the Depression. The crash was dramatic, but it was not, by itself, what broke the economy.
# The boom
The 1920s were prosperous in the United States. Electricity, cars and radios spread into ordinary homes. Share prices rose year after year, and by the late 1920s a growing number of people were buying stocks with borrowed money. An investor could put down as little as a tenth of the price and borrow the rest from a broker, using the shares as security. That worked splendidly while prices climbed. The Dow Jones index reached its peak in early September 1929.
# The crash
Prices slid through September and October. On Thursday, October 24, panic selling began. Bankers pooled money to steady the market, and it held for a few days. Then on Monday, October 28, the index fell about 13 percent, and on Tuesday, October 29, about 12 percent more. Investors who had borrowed were forced to sell to repay their loans, which pushed prices down further and forced more selling. Fortunes disappeared in a week.
# The long slide
The October crash was only the beginning. After a partial recovery in early 1930, the market fell for more than two years. By its low point in the summer of 1932, the Dow had lost close to 90 percent of its value from the 1929 peak. It did not return to that peak until 1954, a quarter of a century later. Someone who bought at the top and held on waited most of a working lifetime to break even.
# From crash to depression
Relatively few Americans owned shares in 1929, so the crash itself touched a minority. The broader damage came through other channels. Confidence collapsed and spending fell. Businesses cut production and laid off workers, who then spent less. The crucial blow was to the banks. Thousands failed between 1930 and 1933. There was no deposit insurance, so when a bank closed, its customers' savings were gone, and fear of that set off runs on healthy banks too.
# How bad it got
By 1933, about a quarter of the American workforce was unemployed. Economic output had fallen by more than a quarter. Prices fell steeply, which made debts harder to repay and bankrupted farmers and firms. Breadlines and shanty towns appeared in major cities. The slump spread across the world through trade and finance, and its political consequences in Europe, above all in Germany, were catastrophic.
# What went wrong in policy
Economists now largely agree that policy mistakes turned a severe recession into a catastrophe. The Federal Reserve allowed the supply of money to shrink by about a third as banks failed, when it could have supported them. Governments clung to the gold standard, which tied their hands. In 1930 the United States sharply raised tariffs on imports, other countries retaliated, and world trade shrank. Each measure made the contraction deeper.
# The reforms
The crisis produced institutions that still exist. From 1933, the federal government insured bank deposits, which largely ended bank runs. A new agency was created in 1934 to regulate the securities markets and require companies to disclose accurate information. Rules limited how much investors could borrow to buy shares. The United States left the gold standard in stages, and recovery began in the countries that abandoned it earliest.
# The lessons that were applied
The central lessons were that financial panics feed on themselves and that central banks must act as lenders when everyone else is pulling back. When a severe financial crisis struck in 2008, the people in charge had studied the 1930s closely and responded by flooding the system with money and supporting banks. The downturn was painful, yet unemployment in the United States peaked at about 10 percent, not 25. The memory of 1929 had been put to use.

=== world | The Manhattan Project | Turning points
> In under three years, a secret programme turned a laboratory discovery into a weapon that ended one war and defined the politics of every decade since.
# A discovery in Berlin
At the end of 1938, chemists in Berlin found that bombarding uranium with neutrons produced a much lighter element. The physicist Lise Meitner, who had fled Nazi Germany, and her nephew Otto Frisch worked out what it meant. The uranium nucleus had split in two, releasing a large amount of energy. Physicists quickly realized that each split also released neutrons that could split further nuclei. A chain reaction, and with it a bomb of unprecedented power, was possible in principle.
# The letter
Refugee scientists in America feared that Germany would get there first. In August 1939, the Hungarian physicist Leo Szilard persuaded Albert Einstein to sign a letter to President Franklin Roosevelt warning of the danger and urging American research. The response was slow at first. After the United States entered the war, the effort became a crash programme. In 1942 it was placed under the Army, commanded by General Leslie Groves and code-named the Manhattan Project.
# The first reactor
On December 2, 1942, in a squash court beneath a stadium at the University of Chicago, a team led by the Italian physicist Enrico Fermi achieved the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction. Their reactor was a stack of graphite blocks and uranium, with control rods to stop it. It ran for a few minutes at a tiny power level. It proved that a chain reaction could be started and controlled, the essential step toward both bombs and nuclear power.
# An industrial effort
The hard part was producing the material. A bomb needed either a rare form of uranium, which had to be separated atom by atom from the common form, or plutonium, a new element made in reactors. Enormous plants were built at Oak Ridge in Tennessee and Hanford in Washington state. The bomb itself was designed at Los Alamos in New Mexico under the physicist Robert Oppenheimer. At its height the project employed well over a hundred thousand people and cost about two billion dollars.
# Trinity
Two designs emerged. The uranium bomb was simple enough that it was never tested. The plutonium bomb required a difficult arrangement of explosives to squeeze a sphere of metal inward, and it had to be tried. On July 16, 1945, in the New Mexico desert, the test code-named Trinity released the energy of roughly twenty thousand tons of conventional explosive. Germany had surrendered two months earlier. The war with Japan continued.
# Hiroshima and Nagasaki
On August 6, 1945, an American bomber dropped the uranium bomb on the city of Hiroshima. Three days later, a plutonium bomb was dropped on Nagasaki. Estimates of the dead by the end of that year are around 140,000 in Hiroshima and 70,000 in Nagasaki, most of them civilians, with more dying later from injuries and radiation. The Soviet Union declared war on Japan between the two bombings. Japan announced its surrender on August 15.
# The argument that has not ended
Whether the bombs should have been used has been debated ever since. Defenders argue that they ended the war quickly and avoided an invasion of Japan that would have cost very many lives on both sides. Critics argue that Japan was close to surrender, that the Soviet entry into the war was decisive, or that a demonstration should have been attempted first. Historians continue to disagree, and the surviving evidence supports more than one reading.
# What it began
The American monopoly lasted four years. The Soviet Union tested its own bomb in 1949, helped by information from spies inside the project. The arms race that followed produced tens of thousands of far more powerful weapons. The project also created the model of science organized on an industrial scale with government money, and it left many of its own scientists campaigning for control of what they had built. The questions it raised, about who should hold such weapons and how to prevent their use, have been part of world politics ever since.
