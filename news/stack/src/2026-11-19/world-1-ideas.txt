=== world | The printing press: when copies became cheap | Inventions that changed things
> Around 1450 a goldsmith in Mainz found a way to mass-produce the written word. Within two generations, Europe was a different place.
# Books before printing
Before the middle of the fifteenth century, every book in Europe was copied by hand. A scribe might take months over a single Bible. Books were therefore rare, expensive and concentrated in monasteries, universities and the houses of the rich. A scholar's personal library might hold a few dozen volumes. Copying also introduced errors, so that no two copies of a work were quite the same, and each generation of copies drifted a little further from the original. A large monastery library was a treasure counted in hundreds of books.
# What Gutenberg actually invented
Printing itself was not new. Chinese printers had used carved wooden blocks for centuries, and movable type made from clay and later metal existed in China and Korea well before 1450. Johannes Gutenberg's achievement was a complete, practical system. He devised a hand mould that could cast large numbers of identical metal letters quickly, an alloy of lead, tin and antimony that cast cleanly, an oil-based ink that stuck to metal, and a press adapted from those used for wine and olives.
# The first great book
Gutenberg's Bible was finished in Mainz around 1455. About 180 copies were made, some on vellum and most on paper, each with 42 lines to the page. It was a commercial venture and it ruined him. His financial backer, Johann Fust, sued for repayment as the project neared completion and took over the workshop. Gutenberg died in 1468 without having profited much from the invention that carries his name. Around fifty of the Bibles survive, in whole or in part.
# The speed of the spread
The technology escaped Mainz almost at once. By 1500, less than fifty years later, presses were operating in well over two hundred European towns, and historians estimate that something like twenty million volumes had been printed. The price of a book fell steeply. A press could turn out in a day what a scribe produced in months. For the first time, a scholar in Kraków and one in Lisbon could be reasonably sure they were reading the same text, page for page.
# Religion and revolt
The first large demonstration of the press's power was religious. When Martin Luther circulated his criticisms of the Church in 1517, printers reproduced them within weeks, and his later pamphlets sold in the hundreds of thousands. Earlier reformers had been contained because their ideas travelled no faster than a manuscript. Luther's could not be contained. The Reformation, and the wars that followed it, are hard to imagine without cheap print. The same presses served his opponents just as readily.
# Science and standard knowledge
Printing changed what knowledge was. Tables of numbers, maps and anatomical drawings could now be reproduced exactly, which they never could by hand. Errors could be corrected in a second edition and stay corrected. Scholars could build on one another's work across a continent, because they were all looking at the same pages. Copernicus on the heavens and Vesalius on the human body both published in 1543. The scientific revolution was, among other things, a consequence of dependable copies.
# The pattern to notice
The press is the classic case of a technology that makes copying cheap. The results were not all benign. It spread astrology, witch-hunting manuals and forged news alongside scripture and science, and authorities struggled for a century to control it with licences and lists of banned books. But the long-run effect was that ideas became far harder to suppress and literacy became worth having. When a later technology makes copying cheaper again, this is the precedent people reach for.

=== world | Germ theory: the invisible cause | How we know
> For most of history, nobody knew what made people sick. The discovery that tiny living things cause disease is barely 150 years old.
# Bad air
Until the late nineteenth century, the leading explanation for epidemic disease was miasma: poisonous vapour rising from rotting matter, swamps and filth. The word malaria means bad air. The theory was not foolish. Disease really did cluster where things smelled bad, and cleaning up cities really did help. But it was wrong about the mechanism, and so it gave no reason for a doctor to wash their hands or sterilize their instruments, since the air was the same for everyone.
# Semmelweis and the hands
In 1847, Ignaz Semmelweis, a Hungarian doctor in Vienna, noticed that women giving birth in the ward staffed by medical students died of fever far more often than those in the ward staffed by midwives. The students came to deliveries straight from dissecting corpses. He ordered them to wash their hands in a chlorine solution, and deaths in his ward fell from well over ten percent to around two. His colleagues took offence at the idea that they were the cause. He lost his post and died in an asylum in 1865.
# Snow and the pump
In 1854, cholera struck the Soho district of London. The physician John Snow plotted the deaths on a street map and found them clustered around a single public water pump on Broad Street. Workers at a nearby brewery, who drank beer, were spared. Snow persuaded the local authorities to remove the pump handle. He could not say what in the water was responsible, but he had shown the disease travelled by water and not by air. His map is regarded as a founding document of epidemiology.
# Pasteur's flasks
The decisive experiments came from the French chemist Louis Pasteur in the 1860s. He boiled broth in flasks with long, curved necks that let air in but trapped dust. The broth stayed clear indefinitely. When he broke a neck or tilted the flask so the liquid touched the trapped dust, it soon swarmed with microbes. Life was not arising spontaneously from the broth. It was arriving from outside. Pasteur went on to show that microbes spoil wine and milk, and that gentle heating prevents it.
# Lister in the operating theatre
The Scottish surgeon Joseph Lister read Pasteur and drew the obvious conclusion. If microbes cause rot, perhaps they cause the infections that killed so many patients after surgery. In the 1860s he began treating wounds, instruments and dressings with carbolic acid. Deaths after amputation on his wards dropped sharply. Surgeons of the time operated in coats stiff with old blood, a mark of experience. Within a generation, clean hands, sterile instruments and eventually gowns and gloves had become the rule.
# Koch names the culprits
The German doctor Robert Koch supplied the final step: proof that a specific microbe causes a specific disease. He identified the bacterium behind anthrax in 1876 and the one behind tuberculosis in 1882, then the cause of cholera. He set out a method that became known as Koch's postulates: find the organism in every case, grow it in pure culture, show that it causes the disease in a healthy animal, and recover it again. Medicine now had a way to hunt.
# What followed
Few ideas have saved more lives. Clean water and sewers, pasteurized milk, antiseptic surgery, vaccines and eventually antibiotics all rest on germ theory. Life expectancy in industrial countries roughly doubled in the century and a half that followed, and control of infectious disease accounts for a large part of that. It is worth remembering how recently it was resisted. The doctors who dismissed Semmelweis were not stupid. They were being asked to believe in a killer they could not see.

=== world | Plate tectonics: the ground is moving | How we know
> The continents drift at the speed your fingernails grow. The idea was ridiculed for half a century before the evidence from the sea floor settled it.
# A jigsaw puzzle on the map
Anyone who looks at a world map notices that the east coast of South America seems to fit against the west coast of Africa. In 1912, a German meteorologist named Alfred Wegener proposed that this was no coincidence. The continents, he argued, had once been joined in a single landmass, which he called Pangaea, and had since drifted apart. He gathered evidence for it over the following years and published his case in a book in 1915.
# Wegener's evidence
The fit of the coastlines was only the start. Fossils of the same land animals and plants turned up on continents now separated by thousands of kilometres of ocean, including a small freshwater reptile found in both Brazil and southern Africa. Mountain belts and rock formations matched across the Atlantic like torn newspaper. Deposits left by glaciers lay in places now tropical. Wegener argued that it was simpler to move the continents than to explain all of this any other way.
# Why he was dismissed
Geologists of the day mostly rejected the idea, some with open scorn. Wegener was an outsider to their field, and more importantly, he could not explain what force could push a continent through solid ocean floor. The mechanisms he suggested were shown to be far too weak. He died in 1930 on an expedition to the Greenland ice cap, his theory still regarded as a curiosity. He had the right answer and the wrong explanation for it.
# The sea floor speaks
The evidence that revived the idea came from the oceans. After the Second World War, ships with sonar mapped the sea bed in detail for the first time. Marie Tharp, working with Bruce Heezen in New York, drew maps that revealed a vast mountain range running down the middle of the Atlantic, with a deep valley along its crest. In the early 1960s, Harry Hess proposed that new sea floor was being created at such ridges and spreading outward on both sides.
# Magnetic stripes
The proof arrived in 1963. The Earth's magnetic field has reversed direction many times, and volcanic rock records the direction of the field at the moment it cools. Surveys found that the sea floor on either side of a ridge is striped with bands of alternating magnetism, and that the pattern on one side is a mirror image of the other. Only one explanation fits: rock forms at the ridge, records the field, and moves away as newer rock takes its place. By the late 1960s, the argument was over.
# How the plates behave
The modern picture is that the Earth's rigid outer shell is broken into a dozen or so large plates that ride on hotter, slowly flowing rock beneath. They move a few centimetres a year. Where they pull apart, new crust forms. Where they collide, one may dive beneath the other, producing deep trenches, volcanoes and the largest earthquakes, or the crust may crumple upward, as it did when India ran into Asia and raised the Himalayas. Where they slide past each other, as along California's San Andreas Fault, the ground lurches.
# One theory, many answers
Plate tectonics explains at a stroke why earthquakes and volcanoes occur in narrow belts, why the Pacific is ringed with both, why mountains stand where they do and why similar fossils appear on separate continents. It does for geology what evolution does for biology. The story also carries a lesson about science. A correct idea was rejected for decades for want of a mechanism, and it was new instruments and new data, not persuasion, that changed minds.

=== world | Natural selection in five minutes | Science ideas
> The most important idea in biology needs only three ingredients, and none of them involves intention. Here is what it claims and what it does not.
# The problem it solved
Living things are intricately suited to the lives they lead. An eagle's eye, a cactus's spines and a bat's sonar all look designed. For most of history, design was the only explanation on offer. In 1859, Charles Darwin's book On the Origin of Species proposed a process that produces the appearance of design with no designer. Alfred Russel Wallace had reached the same idea independently, and the two men's papers were presented together in London in 1858.
# Three ingredients
The argument rests on three observations. Individuals in any population vary: some are taller, faster or better camouflaged than others. Some of that variation is inherited, passed from parents to offspring. And more individuals are born than can survive and reproduce, so there is competition. Put those together and a conclusion follows. Individuals whose inherited traits help them survive and breed will leave more offspring, and those traits will become more common in the next generation. Darwin called the process natural selection, by analogy with the selection that farmers and pigeon fanciers practise deliberately.
# Small steps, deep time
In one generation the shift is slight. Darwin's insight was that slight shifts, repeated over an immense number of generations, can transform a population. He had seen breeders produce wildly different pigeons and dogs in a few centuries by choosing which animals to mate. Nature, he argued, does the same with no chooser, given millions of years. Geologists of his day had recently established that the Earth was vastly older than anyone had supposed, which gave the process the time it needed.
# Seen in action
Selection is not only inferred from fossils. It has been watched. On a small island in the Galapagos, the biologists Peter and Rosemary Grant measured finches year after year. In the drought of 1977, small soft seeds ran out and only birds with deeper beaks could crack the tough seeds that remained. Most of the finches died. The survivors had larger beaks, and so did their offspring. Bacteria evolving resistance to antibiotics are the same process on a faster clock.
# What it does not say
Several common beliefs about evolution are mistaken. It has no goal and no direction: it does not aim at intelligence or complexity. Fittest does not mean strongest. It means best at leaving offspring in a particular environment, and the environment can change. Individuals do not evolve during their lives. Populations change across generations. And humans did not descend from chimpanzees. The two species share a common ancestor that lived several million years ago and was neither.
# The missing piece
Darwin did not know how inheritance worked, and it troubled him. The answer had been found in his lifetime by Gregor Mendel, a monk breeding pea plants in what is now the Czech Republic, whose 1866 paper went unnoticed for decades. In the twentieth century, Mendel's genetics was joined to Darwin's selection, and the discovery of DNA showed what the inherited material is. Random changes in DNA supply the variation. Selection, which is anything but random, does the sorting.
# Why it matters outside biology
The logic of selection applies wherever there is variation, inheritance and competition. Engineers use it to evolve designs in software. Doctors use it to understand why cancers and infections become resistant to drugs, and why finishing a course of treatment matters. It explains why pests adapt to pesticides within years. Understanding it is not only a matter of knowing where we came from. It is a working tool for predicting how living things will respond to whatever we do to them.

=== world | DNA: the molecule that carries instructions | Science ideas
> Four chemical letters, arranged along a twisted ladder, hold the plans for every living thing. Here is how that works and how it was found.
# A long search for the gene
By the early twentieth century, biologists knew that traits were inherited in discrete units called genes and that genes sat on chromosomes in the cell nucleus. What genes were made of was unknown. Chromosomes contain both protein and a substance called DNA, first isolated in 1869. Most scientists bet on protein, which seemed complex enough for the job. Experiments in the 1940s and early 1950s, using bacteria and the viruses that infect them, showed that the genetic material was in fact DNA.
# The double helix
The structure was worked out in 1953 by James Watson and Francis Crick in Cambridge. They relied heavily on X-ray images of DNA made in London by Rosalind Franklin and her student, shown to them without her knowledge, and on measurements from Maurice Wilkins. Watson, Crick and Wilkins received a Nobel Prize in 1962. Franklin had died of cancer four years earlier, and the prize is not awarded after death. Her contribution has been far more fully acknowledged since.
# A ladder with four kinds of rung
DNA is shaped like a twisted ladder. The two side rails are chains of sugar and phosphate. The rungs are pairs of chemical units called bases, of which there are four: adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine, written A, T, G and C. They pair by a strict rule. A always joins with T, and G always joins with C. So the sequence along one rail fixes the sequence along the other. If one side reads A, G, T, the other must read T, C, A.
# Copying follows from the shape
That pairing rule explains how genetic information is copied. When a cell divides, the ladder unzips down the middle. Each half then serves as a template, and new bases attach according to the rule, producing two identical ladders where there was one. Watson and Crick noted this in a famously understated sentence at the end of their paper. The copying is extremely accurate but not perfect, and the rare errors, called mutations, are the raw material of evolution.
# From letters to proteins
The sequence of bases is a code. Read three letters at a time, each triplet specifies one of twenty amino acids, the building blocks of proteins. A gene is a stretch of DNA that spells out one protein, and proteins do most of the work in a cell, from digesting food to carrying oxygen. With four letters taken three at a time there are 64 possible triplets for 20 amino acids, so most amino acids have more than one spelling. Nearly all living things use the same code.
# The scale of it
The human genome is about three billion base pairs long. Stretched out, the DNA in a single human cell would measure roughly two metres, packed into a nucleus a few thousandths of a millimetre across. It contains around twenty thousand genes that code for proteins, far fewer than scientists expected, and a great deal of DNA that regulates when and where those genes are used. Any two people differ in only about one letter in a thousand.
# Reading and writing
The first full reading of a human genome, completed in the early 2000s, took over a decade and cost on the order of three billion dollars. Machines can now do it in about a day for well under a thousand dollars. That collapse in cost underlies ancestry tests, the identification of inherited diseases, forensic evidence and the tracking of viruses as they mutate. Tools for editing DNA have followed. Knowing the structure was the start of being able to read the instructions, and reading them was the start of changing them.

=== world | The periodic table: a map that predicted the future | Science ideas
> A Russian chemistry teacher arranged the known elements on cards and left gaps for ones nobody had found. He was right about the gaps.
# The problem of too many elements
By the 1860s, chemists had identified about sixty elements, substances that cannot be broken down into anything simpler. Each had its own weight, appearance and behaviour, and there seemed to be no order to them. Some patterns were known. Lithium, sodium and potassium all reacted violently with water. Chlorine, bromine and iodine formed similar salts. But nobody had a scheme that fitted every element, and textbooks simply listed them. Students had to memorize each one separately, with no way to guess how an unfamiliar element would behave.
# Mendeleev's cards
In 1869, Dmitri Mendeleev, a professor in St Petersburg writing a textbook, wrote each element on a card with its atomic weight and properties and laid the cards out in order of weight. He saw that the properties repeated at regular intervals. Arranged in rows so that similar elements fell in the same column, the elements formed a table. Others had noticed parts of the pattern. Mendeleev went further than any of them, and the reason is what he did next.
# The gaps
Where the pattern required an element that did not exist, Mendeleev left a blank and insisted that an undiscovered element belonged there. He went on to predict the properties of three of them in detail, including their weights and densities. Gallium was discovered in 1875, scandium in 1879 and germanium in 1886, and each matched his forecasts closely. A scheme that merely organizes known facts is a convenience. One that predicts unknown ones correctly is a discovery about nature.
# The real ordering principle
Mendeleev ordered by weight, and in a few places he had to swap elements against the weight order to keep the columns sensible. The reason became clear in 1913, when the young English physicist Henry Moseley showed that each element has a whole number, the atomic number, equal to the count of protons in its nucleus. Ordered by that number, the table has no exceptions. Moseley was killed in the First World War two years later, at the age of 27.
# Why the pattern repeats
The explanation for the repetition came from the physics of the atom. Electrons surround the nucleus in layers called shells, and an atom's chemistry is governed mainly by the electrons in its outermost shell. Elements in the same column have the same number of outer electrons, so they behave alike. Sodium and potassium each have one, which they give up readily. The noble gases on the far right have full outer shells, which is why helium and neon react with almost nothing.
# Reading the table
The rows are called periods and the columns groups. Metals occupy the left and centre, non-metals the upper right. Moving across a row, atoms hold their electrons more tightly. Moving down a column, atoms get larger and, among the metals, generally more reactive. From an element's position alone, a chemist can make a fair guess at whether it conducts electricity, what it combines with and in what proportions. That is an extraordinary amount of information for one chart.
# Still growing
The table now holds 118 elements. About ninety occur naturally on Earth. The rest have been made in laboratories and reactors, the heaviest of them a few atoms at a time, lasting only fractions of a second before decaying. The four most recent additions received their official names in 2016. Whether still heavier elements can exist long enough to be detected is an open research question. The chart on every classroom wall is unfinished at one end.
