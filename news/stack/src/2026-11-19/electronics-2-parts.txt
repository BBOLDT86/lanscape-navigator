=== electronics | Resistors: reading them and choosing them | The parts bin
> The cheapest part on the board and the one you will use most. How to read the stripes, pick a value and avoid the three usual mistakes.
# What a resistor is for
A resistor does one thing: it resists current by a known amount. That single ability covers a surprising range of jobs. It limits the current into an LED so the LED survives. It sets the gain of an amplifier. It holds an unused input pin at a definite level. It turns a current into a voltage so that a chip can measure it. A typical circuit board carries more resistors than any other part. They cost a fraction of a cent each in quantity, which is why designers reach for one whenever a current needs taming.
# Reading the colour bands
Through-hole resistors carry coloured bands because numbers would be too small to print. The colours stand for digits: black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, grey 8, white 9. On a four band part, the first two bands are digits and the third is the number of zeros to add. Yellow, violet, red reads 4, 7 and two zeros: 4,700 ohms, or 4.7 kilohms. The last band, set slightly apart, gives the tolerance. Gold means 5 percent, brown 1 percent.
# Surface mount codes
The tiny rectangular resistors on modern boards use printed numbers. A three digit code works like the bands: two digits and a count of zeros. So 103 means 10 followed by three zeros, 10,000 ohms. 472 is 4,700 ohms. The letter R marks a decimal point, so 4R7 is 4.7 ohms. A part marked 0 or 000 is a zero ohm link, a resistor shaped wire that lets a machine place a jumper. The smallest sizes carry no marking at all, and the only way to identify one is to measure it.
# Why the values look odd
Resistors come in values such as 10, 22, 33, 47 and 68, repeated at every power of ten. The numbers look arbitrary but are spaced so that each is roughly the same percentage above the last. With 5 percent parts, the ranges of neighbouring values just touch, so every possible resistance is covered by something on the shelf. The standard set is called the E series. You rarely need an exact value. Pick the nearest standard one, and if precision matters, pick a tighter tolerance.
# Tolerance and what it costs
A 5 percent, 1 kilohm resistor may really be anywhere from 950 to 1,050 ohms. For limiting LED current or pulling a pin high, that is irrelevant. For a voltage divider that sets a measurement, it matters, because the error passes straight into the reading. One percent parts cost almost nothing extra today and are the sensible default. Precision references and instrument inputs use 0.1 percent parts that also drift very little as they warm up. Buy accuracy only where the circuit's result depends on it.
# The power rating is the other number
Size tells you how much heat a resistor can shed. The common through-hole part handles a quarter of a watt. The tiny surface mount ones handle a tenth or less. Work out the power with voltage squared divided by resistance and stay under half the rating. A resistor asked to dissipate too much does not fail politely. It discolours, drifts upward in value, and eventually cracks or chars the board beneath it. If you find a scorched patch on an old circuit board, there was usually a hard working resistor above it.
# Three mistakes to skip
First, measuring a resistor while it is still in a powered circuit, which gives nonsense and can damage the meter. Power off first. Second, measuring it in circuit and trusting the number: other parts in parallel pull the reading down, so lift one leg if it matters. Third, holding both leads with your fingers on a high value part. Your skin is a resistor of a few hundred kilohms to a few megohms, and it is now in parallel with the thing you are trying to measure.

=== electronics | Capacitors: small tanks for charge | The parts bin
> A capacitor stores a little energy and gives it back quickly. That one behaviour smooths power, blocks DC, sets timing and keeps chips alive.
# Two plates and a gap
A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator. Push charge onto one plate and an equal charge is repelled from the other, and energy is stored in the electric field between them. The amount it can hold per volt is its capacitance, measured in farads. A farad is enormous, so real parts are rated in microfarads, nanofarads and picofarads, each a thousand times smaller than the last. Unlike a battery, a capacitor stores very little, but it can take in or give out that energy almost instantly.
# It resists changes in voltage
The useful way to think of a capacitor is that it hates a sudden change in the voltage across it. To change that voltage, charge must flow in or out, and that takes time. So a capacitor across a power supply fills the dips and absorbs the spikes, like a small reservoir on a water main. This is the most common use of all. Every digital chip has one or more small capacitors right next to its power pins for this reason, and removing them makes otherwise good circuits behave unpredictably.
# It passes AC and blocks DC
Apply a steady voltage and a capacitor charges up, then no more current flows. Apply a changing voltage and current keeps flowing in and out as the plates charge and discharge. So a capacitor in series with a signal lets the wiggle through while stopping any steady level underneath it. Audio equipment uses this to pass music between stages that sit at different DC voltages. The higher the frequency, the more easily it passes, which is also why capacitors are the basic ingredient of filters.
# Timing with a resistor
A capacitor charged through a resistor takes a predictable time to fill. Multiply the resistance in ohms by the capacitance in farads and you get the time constant in seconds, the time to reach about 63 percent of the supply. Ten kilohms and 100 microfarads gives one second. After five time constants the capacitor is essentially full. This simple pair sets delays, blink rates and the cut-off points of filters. The intermittent setting on windscreen wipers was first built from exactly this arrangement.
# The main types
Ceramic capacitors are small, cheap and not polarized, and they cover the range from picofarads to a few tens of microfarads. Electrolytic capacitors pack far more capacitance into a can, hundreds or thousands of microfarads, but they are polarized: one lead must stay more positive than the other. Film capacitors are stable and favoured in audio and mains circuits. Supercapacitors reach whole farads and can run a small circuit for minutes or hours. The type matters as much as the value, so read both before substituting one for another.
# Polarity and voltage ratings
An electrolytic capacitor connected backwards, or run above its voltage rating, can fail violently. The can is scored on top so that it splits open instead of bursting. The stripe on the side marks the negative lead. Always pick a voltage rating comfortably above what the circuit applies, usually by half again or more. Old electrolytics dry out with heat and age, and bulging tops on the capacitors near a power supply are one of the most common causes of dead monitors, routers and motherboards.
# Respect a charged capacitor
A capacitor holds its charge after the power is removed. Small ones are harmless. Large ones in power supplies, camera flashes, microwave ovens and tube amplifiers can hold a dangerous charge for minutes or longer. Technicians discharge them through a resistor before touching anything and then measure to confirm. Shorting one with a screwdriver works but makes a loud bang and pits the tool. If a device plugs into the wall and you are not sure what is inside, assume the big capacitors are charged until a meter says otherwise.

=== electronics | Inductors: the part that fights change in current | The parts bin
> Coil a wire and it gains inertia. Inductors make switching power supplies possible, and their kickback is why relays kill transistors.
# A coil with a memory
Any current creates a magnetic field around its wire. Wind the wire into a coil and the fields of the turns add together. Building that field takes energy, and collapsing it gives the energy back. The result is a part that resists changes in current the way a heavy flywheel resists changes in speed. Its strength is called inductance, measured in henries, with real parts usually in microhenries or millihenries. Adding an iron or ferrite core concentrates the field and multiplies the inductance many times over.
# The mirror image of a capacitor
A capacitor resists changes in voltage and passes fast signals easily. An inductor resists changes in current and passes steady current easily while opposing fast changes. Put simply, capacitors block DC and inductors block high frequencies. Used together they make filters that select or reject a band of frequencies. An old radio tuning dial moved the plates of a capacitor against a fixed coil to choose a station. The same pairing is still inside every radio, phone and wireless charger made today.
# Kickback
Because an inductor insists that its current keep flowing, interrupting that current abruptly has a dramatic effect. The collapsing field generates whatever voltage it needs to keep the current going, often hundreds of volts from a 12 volt circuit. This is how a car's ignition coil makes a spark. It is also how a relay or motor destroys the transistor that switches it. The cure is a diode placed backwards across the coil, called a flyback diode, which gives the current a harmless loop to die away in.
# How switching supplies use it
The inductor's stubbornness is what makes efficient power conversion possible. A switching regulator connects an inductor to the supply for a moment, storing energy in its field, then disconnects it and lets the inductor deliver that energy to the output. Doing this hundreds of thousands of times a second, and varying how long the switch stays on, produces a lower or higher voltage with very little wasted as heat. Phone chargers, laptop adapters and the converters on every motherboard work this way.
# Transformers are two inductors sharing a field
Wind two coils on the same core and a changing current in the first induces a voltage in the second. The ratio of the turns sets the ratio of the voltages. Ten times the turns on the output gives ten times the voltage and a tenth of the current. Because only a changing field induces anything, transformers work on AC and not on steady DC. They also isolate: no wire connects the input to the output, which is what makes a mains adapter safe to touch on its low voltage side.
# Ferrite beads and chokes
An inductor used simply to block unwanted high frequency noise is called a choke. The lump moulded onto many laptop and monitor cables is a ferrite sleeve doing this job, soaking up radio frequency interference travelling along the wire. On circuit boards, tiny ferrite beads sit in series with power lines to keep the noise of one section out of another. They cost almost nothing and are often the difference between a product that passes its radio emissions test and one that fails it.
# Reading an inductor's limits
Two ratings matter beyond the inductance. The saturation current is the point at which the core cannot hold any more magnetic field. Past it the inductance collapses and the current shoots up, a common cause of failed power converters. The resistance of the winding is the other, since it wastes power as heat. A physically larger inductor of the same value generally has a higher saturation current and lower resistance. When a design calls for a specific inductor, substitute by all three numbers, never by inductance alone.

=== electronics | Diodes: the one-way valve | The parts bin
> A diode lets current through in one direction and blocks it in the other. That simple trick protects, rectifies, regulates and makes light.
# A check valve for electrons
A diode has two leads, the anode and the cathode. Current flows easily from anode to cathode and hardly at all the other way. On the part, a stripe marks the cathode end. On a schematic, the symbol is an arrow pointing at a bar, and the arrow shows the direction current is allowed to flow. Inside is a junction between two differently treated regions of silicon. The junction is the foundation of nearly all semiconductor devices, and a diode is the simplest thing you can build from one.
# The forward drop
A diode is not a perfect valve. It needs a small voltage to open, and it keeps roughly that much for itself while conducting. For an ordinary silicon diode the figure is about 0.6 to 0.7 volts. Below it almost nothing flows. Above it the current rises steeply. That lost voltage becomes heat: a diode carrying 3 amps with 0.7 volts across it dissipates about 2 watts. Schottky diodes use a metal junction instead and drop only 0.2 to 0.4 volts, which is why they appear wherever efficiency matters.
# Rectifying AC
The classic job is turning AC into DC. One diode passes only the positive half of the wave. Four diodes arranged in a diamond, called a bridge rectifier, steer both halves so they emerge pointing the same way. Follow that with a capacitor and you have rough DC. Every device that plugs into a wall contains this arrangement somewhere near its input. Bridge rectifiers are sold as single four-legged blocks marked with a wave symbol on two legs and plus and minus on the others.
# Protecting against mistakes
A diode in series with a power input stops damage if the battery or adapter is connected backwards, at the cost of its forward drop. A diode across a relay coil absorbs the voltage spike when the coil is switched off. Two diodes from an input pin to the supply rails clamp any spike to a safe level, and most chips have these built in. Protection is unglamorous, but it is the reason modern gadgets survive static shocks and reversed plugs that would have destroyed electronics of fifty years ago.
# Zener diodes work backwards on purpose
Every diode breaks down and conducts if enough reverse voltage is applied. A Zener diode is built so that this happens at a precise, repeatable voltage, such as 5.1 or 12 volts, without harming it. Wired in reverse with a resistor in series, it holds a steady voltage across itself while the input varies. It makes a simple voltage reference or a clamp that keeps a line from rising too high. For precision, a dedicated reference chip is better, but a Zener is cheap and nearly indestructible.
# Light in, light out
Some diodes are built to interact with light. An LED emits light when current flows forward through it, with the colour set by the material. A photodiode does the reverse, producing a small current when light falls on it, and is the sensor in remote control receivers, smoke detectors and fibre optic links. A solar cell is a very large photodiode arranged to deliver power. All three are the same junction, optimized for a different job.
# Testing a diode
Most multimeters have a diode setting, marked with the diode symbol. It pushes a small current through the part and shows the forward voltage. A good silicon diode reads about 0.5 to 0.7 one way and shows open circuit the other way. A reading near zero both ways means it has failed short. Open both ways means it has failed open. The same test checks the junctions inside a transistor, and with an LED it usually makes the part glow faintly, which conveniently tells you which lead is which.

=== electronics | LEDs: what they need and why they die | The parts bin
> An LED is easy to light and easy to kill. The difference is one resistor and an understanding of why current, not voltage, is what you control.
# A diode that glows
A light emitting diode is a diode made from materials that release light when current crosses the junction. The colour depends on the material, not on any coating. Red and green LEDs appeared in the 1960s and 70s. An efficient blue one arrived only in the early 1990s, work that earned Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Shuji Nakamura the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics. Blue made white light possible, by coating a blue LED with a phosphor that converts part of its output to yellow.
# Voltage is set by colour
Each colour has its own forward voltage. Red runs at about 1.8 to 2.2 volts. Yellow and older green are similar. Blue, white and modern bright green need about 3 to 3.3 volts. This is a property of the part, and you cannot change it. It means a 3 volt coin cell lights a red LED easily and a blue one only barely, and that a red and a blue LED wired directly in parallel will not share: the red one takes nearly all the current.
# Why it needs a resistor
Once an LED reaches its forward voltage, a tiny further increase in voltage produces a huge increase in current. Connect one straight to a supply and nothing limits that current except the supply itself. The LED overheats and fails in a fraction of a second, sometimes with a pop. A series resistor absorbs the difference between the supply and the forward voltage and so fixes the current. Subtract the LED's voltage from the supply, divide by the current you want, and you have the value.
# How much current to give it
A standard indicator LED is rated for 20 milliamps, but modern ones are so efficient that 1 to 5 milliamps is plenty for a status light indoors. Running at a fraction of the rating saves power and extends life. High power LEDs for lighting are a different animal, running at 350 milliamps to several amps. Those need a constant current driver circuit and a heatsink. A resistor would waste too much power and would not hold the current steady as the LED warms up.
# Which leg is which
An LED only lights one way round. On a new through-hole part the longer leg is the anode, the positive side. The plastic rim usually has a flat spot beside the cathode, the negative side. Inside the lens, the larger metal piece is generally the cathode. Surface mount LEDs carry a green mark or a notch at the cathode end. Connecting an ordinary LED backwards at low voltage does not hurt it. It simply stays dark, which makes reversed polarity the first thing to check.
# Dimming without changing colour
Lowering the current dims an LED, but the colour of white LEDs shifts slightly as it drops. The preferred method is to switch the LED fully on and fully off very fast and vary the fraction of time it is on. Above a few hundred times a second the eye sees a steady, dimmer light. The technique is called pulse width modulation. Poorly designed dimmers switch too slowly, which is the flicker some people notice in cheap bulbs and car tail lights when their eyes move.
# What actually kills them
LEDs do not burn out like filaments. They fade, and heat is the cause. An LED's rated life, often 25,000 hours or more, is the time until its output falls to 70 percent, assuming it is kept cool. In a household bulb the LEDs themselves usually outlast the cramped, hot driver electronics packed in the base. That is why a bulb in an enclosed fixture fails early, and why the first sign of death is often flickering instead of darkness.

=== electronics | The transistor as a switch | The parts bin
> A small current controlling a large one. This is the idea underneath every relay driver, every logic gate and every processor ever made.
# Three legs, one job
A bipolar junction transistor has three terminals: base, collector and emitter. A small current flowing into the base allows a much larger current to flow from collector to emitter. With no base current, the path is closed. The ratio between the two currents is the gain, typically 100 or more for a small transistor. So a microcontroller pin that can supply only a few milliamps can, through a transistor, control a motor, a relay or a strip of lights drawing a hundred times as much.
# NPN and PNP
Transistors come in two polarities. An NPN transistor turns on when its base is driven positive relative to its emitter and is used to switch the ground side of a load. A PNP transistor is the mirror image, turning on when its base is pulled below its emitter, and switches the positive side. Beginners should start with NPN, since it matches the way logic outputs work: pin high, load on. The common 2N3904 and its cousin the 2N2222 are NPN parts that have been in production for more than half a century.
# The standard switch circuit
Connect the load between the positive supply and the collector. Connect the emitter to ground. Connect the control pin to the base through a resistor. When the pin goes high, current flows through the resistor into the base, the transistor conducts and the load turns on. The resistor is essential. The base behaves like a diode to ground, and without a resistor the pin would try to supply unlimited current into it. A value of 1 kilohm suits most small loads driven from 5 volts.
# Saturation: fully on
For switching, you want the transistor fully on, a state called saturation, where the voltage left across it is only about 0.2 volts. To guarantee that, supply noticeably more base current than the gain calculation strictly requires. A common rule is to assume a gain of only 10 or 20. For a 100 milliamp load, that means 5 to 10 milliamps into the base. A transistor that is only partly on drops several volts while carrying current, so it runs hot and may fail.
# Protecting it from the load
Loads with coils, such as relays, motors and solenoids, kick back with a high voltage spike when switched off. Place a diode across the load with its stripe toward the positive supply and the spike circulates harmlessly. Leave it out and the transistor may work for days before failing, which makes the fault hard to connect to its cause. This diode is the most often forgotten part in beginner circuits, and adding it costs a couple of cents.
# When to use something else
A small bipolar transistor is right for loads up to a few hundred milliamps. Beyond that, the base current needed becomes awkward and the 0.2 volt drop starts to waste real power. The usual step up is a MOSFET, which is controlled by voltage and wastes far less at high current. For switching mains voltage or providing complete electrical isolation, use a relay or a solid state relay, with the transistor driving the relay's coil.
# Billions of the same idea
The switch you can hold between two fingers is the same idea, in different form, as the ones in a processor. The first transistor was demonstrated at Bell Labs in December 1947 and was about the size of a thumb. A modern phone chip contains well over ten billion, each a switch turning the next one on or off. Logic gates, memory cells and arithmetic units are all arrangements of switches. Understand one transistor switching one LED and you hold the bottom rung of the ladder that leads to every computer.
