=== poker | Defending the big blind | Before the flop
> You have already paid for part of the pot. That discount changes which hands you should play, and misjudging it is one of the costliest leaks in the game.
# A forced discount
The big blind is the only seat that enters every hand with money already in the pot. When someone raises, you are not paying the full price to continue. You are paying the difference. Against a raise to two and a half big blinds from the button, with the small blind folding, there are four big blinds in the pot and it costs you one and a half more. You are getting better than 2.5 to 1, and you close the action, so nobody can raise behind you.
# The raw number
At that price you need only about 27 percent equity to break even on the call, if the hand simply ran to showdown. Against a minimum raise to two big blinds, the requirement drops to about 22 percent. Almost any two cards have that much against a wide button range. Seven two offsuit has roughly 30 percent against a typical late position opener. If raw equity were the whole story, you would defend almost everything.
# Why you cannot defend everything
The whole story includes what happens after the flop. You will be out of position on every street, acting first with less information. Weak hands frequently win less than their share of the pot in that spot. They are forced to fold to bets when they would have won, and they pay off when they make a second-best pair. This shortfall is called failing to realize equity, and offsuit, disconnected hands suffer from it most.
# Hands that play well
The hands that defend best are those that can make strong holdings or strong draws. Suited cards realize their equity far better than offsuit ones, because they can continue on more flops. Connected cards make straights. Pairs can flop sets. So queen six suited is a reasonable call against a button raise, while queen six offsuit is close to a fold. The two hands differ by only a few points of raw equity and by a great deal in practice.
# Size and position of the raiser
Your defending range should stretch and shrink with the situation. Against a small raise, defend wide. Against a raise to four big blinds, the price is much worse and you should tighten considerably. The raiser's seat matters as much. A raise from under the gun represents a strong, narrow range and deserves respect. A raise from the button could be nearly half of all hands, and folding too much against it lets that player profit with any two cards.
# Reraising from the blind
Calling is not the only way to defend. Reraising with your strongest hands builds a pot when you are ahead, and mixing in some bluffs with hands such as suited aces and suited kings punishes players who open too widely. Because you will be out of position, make these reraises large, around four times the opening raise. A blind that only ever calls is easy to play against. One that sometimes comes back over the top makes late position raisers think twice.
# With antes in play
In tournaments, every player or the big blind alone often posts an ante on top of the blinds. That extra money in the middle improves the price further. Facing a small raise with antes in the pot, the big blind may need less than 20 percent equity, and the correct defending range becomes very wide indeed. Players who learned in games without antes routinely fold far too much from the big blind in tournaments and bleed chips each orbit.
# After you defend
Defending widely only works if you play sensibly afterwards. Most of the time you will miss the flop and should simply check and fold to a bet. When you connect, lean toward checking and raising your strong hands and good draws instead of leading out. Do not pay off three streets with a weak top pair against a tight opponent. The aim from the big blind is modest: lose less than the one big blind that folding every time would cost.

=== poker | Stack-to-pot ratio: how deep the hand really is | After the flop
> One number, worked out on the flop, tells you how strong a hand needs to be before you are willing to play for everything.
# The ratio
Stack-to-pot ratio, usually shortened to SPR, is the smaller of the two players' remaining stacks divided by the size of the pot when the flop is dealt. If the pot holds 20 dollars and each of you has 200 behind, the SPR is 10. If the pot holds 60 and you each have 180, it is 3. The figure describes how much betting is still possible relative to what is already in the middle, and that governs how hands should be played.
# Where typical values come from
Suppose stacks are 100 big blinds. A raise to 3 and one call from the button, with the blinds folding, makes a pot of 7.5 big blinds with 97 behind, an SPR of about 13. Now suppose there is a reraise to 10 and a call. The pot is about 21 and the stacks 90, an SPR a little above 4. The reraise did not just make the pot bigger. It changed the kind of hand that is being played.
# Low ratios favour one pair
When the SPR is around 4 or lower, one or two bets will put the remaining money in. Hands such as an overpair or top pair with a good kicker are strong enough to commit with, because opponents will put their stacks in with worse pairs and draws. There is little room for an opponent to outplay you on later streets. In low SPR pots, the decision is largely made on the flop: either the hand is good enough to go with or it is not.
# High ratios demand more
When the SPR is 10 or more, there are three full streets of betting ahead. If all of that money goes in, the winning hand is rarely one pair. It is two pair, a set, a straight or a flush. Top pair becomes a hand to win a medium pot with, not to stack off with. Many players lose their biggest pots by treating an overpair in a deep pot as though it were the nuts.
# Speculative hands need depth
The same logic explains which starting hands gain or lose value. Small pairs and suited connectors are trying to make hidden, powerful hands and win a big pot. They need a high SPR to be paid enough when they hit. Big unpaired cards such as ace king prefer a low SPR, because their usual made hand is top pair, and top pair is happiest when the money goes in early.
# You can shape it
SPR is not something that merely happens to you. Your sizing before the flop sets it. Raising larger or reraising lowers it, which suits big pairs and big cards. Keeping the pot small with a call preserves a high SPR, which suits speculative hands in position. Good players think one step ahead: with this hand against this opponent, what kind of pot do I want to be playing after the flop?
# Commitment
A useful question on the flop is whether you are committed, meaning willing to put the rest of your stack in. With a low SPR and a strong pair, the answer is usually yes, and you should play to get the money in. With a high SPR and the same pair, the answer is no, and you should aim to control the size of the pot, often by checking one street. Deciding this early prevents the worst outcome: betting twice and then folding.
# A habit for every flop
Before acting on any flop, glance at the pot and the shorter stack and estimate the ratio. Under 4, play strong pairs fast. Between 4 and 10, proceed with care and let the board and the opponent guide you. Above 10, remember that big pots belong to big hands. The estimate takes a second. It prevents overplaying one pair when deep, and it prevents folding the best hand when the pot is already too large to give up.

=== poker | Reading the board: wet, dry and who it favours | After the flop
> Three cards land and the hand changes character. Learning to classify a flop quickly tells you who should be betting and how much.
# Dry boards
A dry flop is one with few possible draws: cards of three different suits that are far apart in rank, such as king, seven, two. On a board like this, hand values are fixed early. Whoever is ahead on the flop will usually still be ahead on the river, because there are few cards that change anything. Bets on dry boards can be small, since there are no draws to charge, and bluffs succeed often, since it is hard for anyone to have connected.
# Wet boards
A wet flop is rich in draws: two or three cards of one suit, or cards close together in rank, such as jack, ten, eight with two spades. Many hands have some piece of it, and the best hand now is vulnerable to a large share of the deck. Strong hands should bet larger to make drawing expensive. Bluffing is harder, because opponents have so many reasons to continue. The lead can change on every street.
# Paired and monotone boards
Two special cases deserve their own rules. On a paired flop, such as nine, nine, four, it is harder for anyone to hold a strong hand, since only two nines remain. These boards are bluffed at frequently, and small bets work well. On a flop of three cards in one suit, anyone holding a single high card of that suit has a strong draw and anyone without one is in trouble. Play tends to slow down, and big bets from players who lack the suit are risky.
# High cards favour the raiser
The player who raised before the flop holds more big cards and big pairs than the player who called. So a flop with an ace or a king generally helps the raiser's overall range more than the caller's. On those boards the raiser can bet frequently, even with nothing, and the caller should be cautious. This idea is called range advantage. It is about who holds the stronger collection of possible hands, not about any one hand.
# Low connected cards favour the caller
A flop such as seven, six, five tells the opposite story. A player who called a raise, especially from the big blind, holds many more small pairs, suited connectors and two-pair combinations that hit this board. The raiser's range of big cards has largely missed. Here the raiser should check more often, and the caller can attack. Betting automatically on such a flop because you raised before it is a common and expensive habit.
# Who can have the best hands
A related idea is nut advantage: which player can hold the very strongest hands. On a flop of ace, king, four, the preflop raiser can have pocket aces, pocket kings and ace king. The caller would usually have reraised with those and so rarely has them. A player with the nut advantage can bet very large, because the opponent can never be confident. A player without it should keep bets smaller, or check.
# Turn and river cards
Texture is not fixed on the flop. A dry board can become wet when the turn brings a second card of a suit and a straight possibility. A third flush card or a fourth card to a straight on the river transforms the value of every hand. Ask of each new card which player it helps. An ace on the turn usually favours the preflop raiser. A low card that completes a straight usually favours the caller.
# A quick routine
On every flop, run three questions. How many draws are possible? Whose range does this board hit harder? Who can hold the strongest hands? The answers set your plan before you look at your own two cards. With practice the routine takes a couple of seconds. It also stops the most common beginner error after the flop, which is to think only about what you hold and not about what the board means for both players.

=== poker | The continuation bet | After the flop
> You raised before the flop and the flop has come. Betting again is the most frequent play in poker, and knowing when not to is where the profit lies.
# What it is
A continuation bet, or c-bet, is a bet on the flop by the player who made the last raise before it. The raiser is continuing the story of strength. It works for a simple reason: most hands miss most flops. A player holding two unpaired cards fails to make a pair about two times in three. So whoever bets first often wins, and the preflop raiser, having shown strength already, is the natural candidate to do it.
# The price of a small bet
The arithmetic is generous. A bet of one third of the pot needs to succeed only one time in four to break even, even if you never win when called. A bet of half the pot needs one time in three. Against a single opponent who has missed the flop two thirds of the time, that looks like easy money, and for many years players simply bet every flop. Opponents eventually noticed, and automatic c-betting is now a leak that good players exploit.
# Best boards for it
C-bets work best on dry flops with high cards, such as ace, eight, three, or king, seven, two. These boards connect with the raiser's range of big cards and miss most of what a caller holds. A small bet, around a third of the pot, with nearly your whole range is a sound play there. Your opponent has to fold most of their hands, and you risk little on the occasions they have something.
# Boards to check
On low, connected and suited flops, such as eight, seven, five with two hearts, the caller's range is at least as strong as yours. A bet with nothing will be called or raised a large part of the time. Here it is better to check more of your range, including some reasonably strong hands, so that your checks are not an open invitation to bluff you. Betting should be reserved for strong hands and good draws, usually at a larger size.
# Count the opponents
The c-bet is a play for pots with one opponent. Against one player who misses two flops in three, a bluff has a fine chance. Against three players, the chance that all of them missed is small. In pots with several opponents, bet when you have a real hand or a real draw, and check the rest. Firing into a crowd with ace high is one of the fastest ways to lose chips at a loose table.
# Position changes the plan
With position, the c-bet is safer. If called, you get to see what your opponent does on the turn before you act. You can also check behind on the flop and take a free card. Out of position, a called c-bet leaves you first to act on the turn with a larger pot and no new information. For that reason, the out-of-position raiser should check more often and be more selective about which hands to bet.
# What to bet with
A balanced betting range has three parts. Strong hands that want to build a pot. Draws and hands with a little equity, such as two overcards or a gutshot, which can improve when called. And a few outright bluffs on boards that favour you. Medium hands, such as second pair or a weak top pair, often prefer to check. They are good enough to win at showdown but not strong enough to welcome a raise.
# After the flop bet
A c-bet is the first step of a plan, not a single shot. Before betting, decide what you will do on the turn. Good turn cards for a second bet are ones that improve your range or your actual hand: an ace or king, or a card that gives you a draw. If the turn is a blank that changes nothing and you have no equity, checking and giving up is fine. The discipline to stop is what separates a controlled bluff from a spew.

=== poker | Bet sizing: saying it with chips | After the flop
> How much you bet is as much a part of the play as whether you bet. A few principles cover most situations.
# Think in fractions of the pot
Poker bets are measured against the pot, not in dollars. A 50 dollar bet is tiny into a pot of 400 and enormous into a pot of 30. Players describe sizes as a third of the pot, half, two thirds, the full pot or an overbet. Thinking this way makes your decisions consistent across stakes and lets you connect the size directly to the odds you are offering your opponent, which is what a bet size really is.
# Size by board, not by hand
The worst habit in sizing is betting big with strong hands and small with weak ones, or the reverse. Observant opponents decode it within an hour. The remedy is to choose the size according to the situation, mainly the texture of the board, and use that same size with both your value hands and your bluffs. Then the amount gives nothing away. Your opponent knows what kind of board it is but not which part of your range you hold.
# Small on dry boards
On dry flops where you hold the range advantage, small bets of a quarter to a third of the pot are efficient. There are no draws to charge, the opponent must still fold their many missed hands, and when you are behind you lose little. A small bet with a wide range puts the opponent in an awkward spot with all of their weak holdings. This approach is one of the clearest lessons to come out of computer analysis of the game.
# Larger on wet boards
When the board is full of draws, your strong hands are vulnerable and your opponent has many hands willing to call. Bet larger, two thirds of the pot or more. This charges draws a price that is wrong for them and builds the pot while you are ahead. A flush draw facing a bet of a third of the pot is delighted. Facing three quarters of the pot with one card to come, it is making a mistake by calling.
# Planning three streets
Pots grow geometrically. If you bet the full pot on the flop, turn and river and are called each time, the pot becomes 3 times, then 9 times, then 27 times its starting size, and each player has put in 13 times the original pot. With bets of half the pot, the final pot is only 8 times the start. So with a strong hand and deep stacks, you must begin betting on the flop. You cannot get a whole stack in from the river alone.
# Overbets
A bet larger than the pot is an overbet. It is appropriate when you can hold the very strongest hands and your opponent's range is capped, meaning their earlier play has made it unlikely they hold anything huge. The big bet gets maximum value from your monsters and puts maximum pressure on their medium hands. It must be accompanied by some bluffs at the same size. Used only with the nuts, it quickly stops getting paid.
# Bluffs follow the same sizes
The size of a bluff determines how often it has to work. A bluff of a third of the pot must succeed one time in four. Half the pot, one in three. The full pot, one in two. A larger bluff forces more folds but costs more when it fails. The right size is the one your value hands would use in the same spot, because a bluff is only believable if it looks the same as the real thing.
# Against weaker players
Everything above assumes an opponent who is paying attention. Against players who are not, simplify. If someone calls any bet with any pair, bet your strong hands as large as they will call and do not bluff. If someone folds to every large bet, bluff large and bet small for value. Balanced sizing protects you against experts. Against everyone else, the most profitable size is the one that takes advantage of the specific mistake they keep making.

=== poker | Value betting: getting paid by worse hands | After the flop
> Most of the money in poker is won by betting good hands and being called by slightly worse ones. Missing those bets is an invisible leak.
# Where the profit comes from
Bluffs are memorable, but most of a winning player's income is value: betting the best hand and having a worse one call. At low and middle stakes, where opponents call too often, this is overwhelmingly true. A player who never bluffed but extracted full value from every strong hand would beat most casual games comfortably. A player who bluffs brilliantly but checks back winning hands on the river leaves money behind every session.
# The test
A value bet is correct when, among the hands that will call you, more than half are worse than yours. The question to ask before betting is concrete: which worse hands call this bet? If you can name several likely holdings that are behind you and would pay, bet. If the only hands that call are ones that beat you, the bet achieves nothing. Worse hands fold, better hands call, and you lose the most when you lose.
# Thin value
The skill that separates good players from average ones is betting hands that are only modestly strong. This is called thin value. Top pair with a middling kicker on the river, or second pair against an opponent who called twice with what looks like a draw that missed and a pair that did not. Average players check these hands, glad to reach showdown. Strong players bet a small amount and are called by weaker pairs often enough to profit.
# Why players miss it
The barrier is fear. Betting a medium hand feels risky because sometimes you will be raised or shown a better hand, and those moments are vivid. The many times a worse hand quietly calls a small bet are less memorable. It helps to accept in advance that a correct thin value bet will run into a better hand a fair share of the time. If you are never called by better when you bet the river, you are not betting enough hands.
# Size to the caller
The right amount is the largest your opponent's worse hands will pay. Against someone who hates folding any pair, bet big with strong hands, since the size barely affects whether they call. Against a cautious player, a smaller bet is called far more often and earns more on average. Think about what specific hands you are targeting. If you want a call from a weak top pair, do not bet an amount that only two pair would consider.
# Do not slow play by default
Checking a strong hand to trap is tempting and usually costs money. It gives free cards to hands that could beat you, and it fails to build a pot. A big hand wants a big pot, and pots are built by betting on every street. Slow playing has a place on very dry boards against aggressive opponents who will bet if you check. Against passive players who call but rarely bet, checking a strong hand just means winning less.
# The river check behind
The opposite error is betting a hand that cannot be called by worse. On the river with a medium-strength hand in position, such as a weak pair when the draws have missed, ask the test question honestly. If your opponent's worse hands are busted draws that will fold, and their better hands will call, then check and take the showdown. Betting there turns a hand with real value into a bluff that only gets called when it loses.
# A session drill
For one session, at every river where you hold a made hand and it is your turn to bet, silently name three worse hands that would call. If you can, bet. If you cannot, check. Afterwards, note the hands where you checked and won against something that would have paid. Those are your missed bets. Most players find several a night, and adding them back is one of the quickest ways to raise a win rate.
