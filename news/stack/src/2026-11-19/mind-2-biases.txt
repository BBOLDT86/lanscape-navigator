=== mind | Confirmation bias: the mother of all biases | Biases one at a time
> People do not weigh evidence evenly. We look for what agrees with us, and we are far better at spotting flaws in arguments we dislike.
# What it is
Confirmation bias is the tendency to search for, notice, interpret and remember information in ways that support what you already believe. It is not dishonesty. It operates before you are aware of it, shaping which facts feel relevant and which sources feel reliable. Psychologists regard it as among the most pervasive distortions in human reasoning, and it afflicts intelligent and educated people at least as much as anyone else, because they are more skilled at building a case.
# The number game
In 1960 the British psychologist Peter Wason gave people the sequence 2, 4, 6 and told them it followed a rule. They could propose other sequences and be told whether each fitted, then guess the rule. Most people assumed the rule was even numbers rising by two and tested sequences like 8, 10, 12. Each was confirmed, and they announced their rule with confidence. They were wrong. The rule was simply any three rising numbers. Almost nobody tried a sequence meant to fail.
# Why testing to fail works
The Wason task shows the logical heart of the problem. Examples that fit your theory can never prove it, because they may fit a broader theory too. A single example that should fail and does not would have exposed the error at once. Trying 1, 2, 3 or 10, 20, 500 would have been far more informative than another run of even numbers. Good scientists, detectives and engineers deliberately look for the observation that would prove them wrong.
# Same evidence, wider disagreement
In a well-known 1979 experiment at Stanford, people who supported or opposed the death penalty read two studies, one suggesting it deterred crime and one suggesting it did not. Both groups judged the study that agreed with them to be well conducted and found methodological faults in the other. After reading the same mixed evidence, each side was more convinced than before. More information does not automatically bring people together. It can push them apart.
# Asymmetric scrutiny
A useful way to catch the bias in yourself is to notice which question you are asking. When a claim supports what you believe, the question tends to be: can I believe this? Nearly anything passes. When a claim threatens your view, the question becomes: must I believe this? Nearly anything can be doubted. The standard of proof shifts without your noticing, and you come away feeling you have evaluated both sides fairly.
# How feeds amplify it
Modern information sources make the bias easier to indulge. You choose whom to follow, and recommendation systems then learn what holds your attention, which is mostly agreement and outrage at the other side. The result is a stream in which your existing views look like the obvious consensus. This is not unique to any political side or topic. The same mechanism surrounds a sports fan, an investor in a particular stock and a believer in a diet with constant reassurance.
# Counter-measures
Several habits help. Before deciding, ask what evidence would change your mind, and then go and look for exactly that. State the strongest version of the opposing view in terms its supporters would accept. Seek out a critic you respect and ask them to find the holes. When you find a source that confirms what you hoped, apply the same scrutiny you would give one that did not. None of these feels natural, which is rather the point.
# In ordinary life
The bias matters most where stakes are personal. A manager who has decided an employee is weak notices each mistake and overlooks successes. An investor who loves a company reads the good news and skims the bad. A person in an argument with their partner recalls every past example that supports their case. Asking what you would expect to see if you were wrong, and whether you are in fact seeing it, is a small discipline with large returns.

=== mind | Anchoring: the first number wins | Biases one at a time
> An arbitrary figure mentioned at the start of a judgement drags the final answer toward itself, even when everyone knows it is meaningless.
# The wheel of fortune
In a 1974 paper, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described spinning a wheel of fortune in front of volunteers. The wheel was rigged to stop at either 10 or 65. The volunteers were then asked what percentage of African countries were members of the United Nations. Those who saw 10 gave a median estimate of 25 percent. Those who saw 65 gave 45 percent. A number they knew to be random had moved their answers by 20 percentage points.
# How anchors work
Two mechanisms appear to be involved. In one, people start from the anchor and adjust away from it, but stop too soon, as soon as they reach a value that seems plausible. In the other, the anchor makes information consistent with it easier to bring to mind. Asked whether a house is worth more or less than a high figure, you think of its good features. Both effects pull in the same direction, and neither feels like being influenced.
# Experts are not immune
In one field study, experienced estate agents were shown around a house and given an information pack. The packs were identical except for the listing price. Agents given a higher listing price produced clearly higher valuations. Asked afterwards what had shaped their judgement, they mostly denied the listing price had played any part. Knowledge of a subject reduces anchoring somewhat. It does not remove it, and experts are often the last to notice.
# Dice and prison terms
A German study went further. Experienced judges read a description of a shoplifting case and then rolled a pair of dice that were loaded to total either 3 or 9. They were asked whether the sentence should be more or fewer months than that number, and then to give a sentence. Those who had rolled 9 gave on average about eight months. Those who had rolled 3 gave about five. The anchor was produced by dice in their own hands.
# In shops and salaries
Anchors are everywhere in commerce. A crossed-out original price makes the sale price look generous. A very expensive item at the top of a menu makes the second most expensive seem reasonable. A recommended retail price printed on the box does the same job, whether or not anyone ever paid it. In a salary negotiation, the first figure spoken frames everything that follows. This is why the advice to avoid naming a number first is not always sound: whoever names it sets the anchor.
# Using it deliberately
If you have good information about the realistic range, opening with a figure at the ambitious end of it tends to pull the final agreement in your favour. The anchor needs to be defensible. An absurd number can end the conversation or damage trust. Supporting it with a reason helps. When selling a car, a house or your own services, the opening figure deserves more thought than most people give it, since it does much of the work.
# Defending against it
When the other side sets an anchor, the worst response is to treat it as a starting point and haggle from there. Better to set it aside explicitly and return to independent grounds: comparable sales, market rates, what the thing is worth to you. Research suggests that deliberately generating reasons why the anchor is wrong weakens its pull. Deciding on your own figure before any discussion begins, and writing it down, is the simplest protection.
# Anchoring yourself
Some anchors are self-inflicted. The price you paid for a share becomes the reference for whether to sell it, though the market neither knows nor cares what you paid. Last year's budget anchors this year's. The first estimate of how long a project will take lingers after the facts have changed. Asking what you would decide if you were coming to the question fresh, with no history, is a good way to notice a number that has outstayed its usefulness.

=== mind | Loss aversion: why losing hurts more than winning feels good | Biases one at a time
> The pain of losing a sum is felt more sharply than the pleasure of gaining the same sum. That imbalance bends decisions about money, work and risk.
# The coin flip nobody takes
Suppose you are offered a bet on a fair coin. Heads you win 150 dollars, tails you lose 100. On average the bet is clearly in your favour. Most people refuse it. Asked how much the win would need to be before they accepted, the typical answer is around 200 dollars, twice the possible loss. This finding is central to prospect theory, the account of decisions under risk that Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky published in 1979.
# Gains and losses, not wealth
Older economic theory assumed people judge outcomes by their final level of wealth. Prospect theory showed that people instead judge changes relative to a reference point, usually where they are now. A gain and a loss of the same size are not mirror images. The loss weighs roughly twice as much. Later critics have argued that the effect is weaker or absent for small stakes, and the exact ratio is debated. For decisions that matter, the asymmetry shows up reliably.
# The endowment effect
One consequence is that people value things more once they own them. In a classic experiment, half of a group of students were given a university mug and then allowed to trade. Economic theory predicted that about half the mugs would change hands. Very few did. Owners demanded about twice as much to give up a mug as the others were willing to pay for one. Giving up the mug registered as a loss, and losses are expensive.
# Holding losers, selling winners
Investors show the pattern clearly. Studies of brokerage accounts have found that people are considerably more likely to sell a share that has risen than one that has fallen. Selling a loser turns a paper loss into a real one, which is painful, so they wait to get back to even. The market has no memory of what they paid. The result is often that winners are sold too soon and losers are kept too long, the reverse of what would serve them.
# Framing
Because reactions depend on whether something is seen as a gain or a loss, the same facts produce different choices depending on how they are described. A surgery with a 90 percent survival rate is chosen more often than one with a 10 percent mortality rate. A discount for paying cash is accepted where a surcharge for using a card causes resentment. The outcomes are identical. Only the reference point implied by the wording has moved.
# The status quo
Loss aversion also explains a general reluctance to change. Any change involves giving up some things to gain others, and the things given up loom larger. So people stay with their default pension fund, their current supplier and their existing job longer than a neutral calculation would suggest. Organizations that understand this set helpful defaults, such as enrolling staff in a savings plan automatically and letting them opt out, which raises participation dramatically.
# When it protects you
The instinct is not simply an error. For most of human history, a serious loss could mean starvation, while an equivalent gain meant only a better week. Caution about losses kept people alive. It still protects against ruinous bets. The trouble arises when the instinct fires over small, repeated risks where the odds favour you. Refusing every such bet means giving up a great deal over a lifetime in exchange for avoiding minor disappointments.
# Working around it
A few questions help. Would I buy this today at the current price if I did not already own it? If not, why am I keeping it? Am I judging this choice on its own, or as one of many similar choices I will make over the years, where the averages will work in my favour? And what exactly is my reference point, and is it still the right one? Naming the feeling as loss aversion often loosens its hold.

=== mind | The availability heuristic: vivid is not the same as likely | Biases one at a time
> We judge how common something is by how easily examples come to mind. That shortcut serves us well until memory is fed a distorted sample.
# A shortcut that usually works
When asked how frequent something is, the mind does not count. It checks how readily instances can be recalled and treats ease as a measure of frequency. Often this works, because common things do tend to be easier to remember. Tversky and Kahneman, who named the shortcut in 1973, called it the availability heuristic. The trouble is that many things besides frequency make an example easy to recall: how recent it was, how dramatic, how emotional, how personal.
# The letter K
In one early demonstration, people were asked whether English has more words that begin with the letter K or more words with K as the third letter. Most chose the first. In fact there are more with K in third place. It is simply much easier to search your memory for words by their first letter, so examples such as kitchen and kite arrive quickly, while words such as ask and like have to be dug out. Ease of retrieval was mistaken for abundance.
# Risk and the news
The largest distortions involve danger. News reports cover events that are unusual and dramatic, so plane crashes, shark attacks and kidnappings are far more available in memory than their true frequency warrants. Heart disease, falls and car accidents kill vastly more people and generate few headlines. People consistently overestimate deaths from spectacular causes and underestimate those from mundane ones. The result is fear directed at the wrong targets.
# A deadly example
After the attacks of September 2001, many Americans avoided flying and drove long distances instead. Driving is much more dangerous per mile than commercial flight. The psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer analysed road fatality figures for the following twelve months and estimated that around 1,600 more people died on the roads than would have been expected, a toll several times the number of passengers on the hijacked planes. A vivid fear had steered people toward a greater risk.
# Whose effort you remember
Availability also explains a familiar household dispute. When couples are each asked what percentage of the housework they do, the two answers typically add up to well over 100. Each person remembers their own contributions in detail, because they were present for all of them, and recalls the other's only partly. The same happens in teams at work. Nobody is lying. Each is drawing on a sample of memories that over-represents their own efforts.
# Recent and personal
One vivid personal experience can outweigh a mountain of statistics. Someone whose neighbour was burgled feels crime is rising whatever the figures say. An investor who lived through a crash stays out of the market for years. A manager who was once let down by a remote worker distrusts remote work. Marketers know that a single story about one customer persuades more people than a table of results from thousands. Stories are available. Numbers are not.
# Correcting for it
The remedy is to ask for the count instead of the example. How often does this actually happen, per year, per thousand people? Where would I find that number? Then ask why the example came to mind so easily. Was it on the news, did it happen to someone I know, was it frightening? If the answer is yes, ease of recall is telling you about the storytelling, not about the world. Base your decision on the frequency.
# Turning it to good use
The same mechanism can work for you. If you want to remember to do something, make it vivid and visible. If you want people to take a real risk seriously, give them a concrete story alongside the statistics. And if you want an accurate view of your own life, keep a record. People who write down what went well each week are often surprised when they read it back, because memory had kept the frustrations and let most of the rest go.

=== mind | Base rates: start with how often it happens | Decisions under uncertainty
> Before judging a specific case, ask how things usually turn out for cases like it. Ignoring that step produces confident and badly wrong answers.
# A positive test
A disease affects 1 percent of people. A test detects it 90 percent of the time when it is present, and gives a false alarm 9 percent of the time when it is not. You test positive. What is the chance you have the disease? Most people, including many doctors in published studies, answer somewhere near 90 percent. The correct answer is about 9 percent. The error comes from ignoring how rare the disease was to begin with.
# Count people, not percentages
The answer becomes obvious if you imagine 1,000 people. Ten have the disease, and nine of those test positive. Of the 990 who are healthy, 9 percent test positive anyway, which is about 89 people. So 98 people have a positive result, and only 9 of them are ill. That is roughly one in eleven. The psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer has shown that presenting risks as counts of people in this way sharply improves the accuracy of patients and physicians alike.
# What a base rate is
The base rate is the underlying frequency of something in the relevant population: 1 percent in the example. Specific evidence, such as a test result or a description, should adjust that starting figure. It should not replace it. People tend to do the opposite. Faced with vivid, particular information about a case, they forget the background frequency entirely. Psychologists call this base rate neglect, and it appears in medicine, law, hiring and investing.
# The stereotype trap
In a classic experiment, people read a description of a man who is shy, tidy and fond of detail, and were asked whether he is more likely to be a librarian or a farmer. Most said librarian, because he fits the picture. They ignored the fact that farmers vastly outnumber male librarians. Even if the description fits librarians several times better, there are so many more farmers that he is still more likely to be one of them.
# The inside view and the outside view
Daniel Kahneman described two ways to forecast. The inside view looks at the specifics of your own case: your plan, your abilities, your circumstances. The outside view asks how similar projects have turned out for other people. He told of a team writing a school textbook who estimated two more years of work. Asked about comparable teams, one member admitted that most took seven to ten years and many never finished. Theirs took eight.
# Why plans run late
That story illustrates the planning fallacy. People building a schedule imagine the work going according to plan and add a small allowance. They do not consider the many unrelated ways things can go wrong, because no single one is likely. Collectively they are nearly certain. Large construction and software projects overrun as a rule. The reliable correction is to find out how long similar projects actually took and start from that figure, however special yours feels.
# Finding the reference class
Using base rates requires choosing a comparison group. For a new restaurant, it might be all restaurants opened in your city in recent years and the share that survived. For a job applicant, the track record of past hires with similar backgrounds. For a home renovation, what contractors' projects of that size typically cost in the end. The class will never match perfectly. An imperfect outside view still tends to beat a detailed inside view built on optimism.
# A two-step habit
The practical method has two steps. First, establish the base rate: for things like this, what usually happens? Write the number down. Second, adjust for the particulars of this case, and adjust less than you feel inclined to. If you believe your case is an exception, list the specific reasons and ask whether the people who failed believed the same about theirs. Most of them did. Being special is the most common thing in the world.

=== mind | The pre-mortem: finding the failure before it happens | Decisions under uncertainty
> Imagine the project has already failed, then explain why. Ten minutes of this exercise surfaces risks that weeks of planning politely overlook.
# Planning is biased toward hope
When a team commits to a plan, the mood turns against doubt. People who have reservations keep quiet, because raising problems feels disloyal or negative once the decision is made. The plan is examined mainly by those who wrote it, who see how it is meant to work. Overconfidence builds. Afterwards, when things have gone wrong, everyone can list the causes, and many will say they saw it coming. The knowledge was in the room and never spoken.
# The method
The psychologist Gary Klein proposed a simple remedy and called it a pre-mortem. Before the project starts, gather the people involved. Ask them to imagine that it is a year from now and the project has failed badly. Not might fail: has failed. Then give everyone a few minutes, working alone and in silence, to write down every reason they can think of for the failure. Finally, go round the room collecting one reason from each person until the lists are exhausted.
# Why imagining it done works
The trick lies in the tense. Research on what is called prospective hindsight found that people asked to explain an event as though it had already happened produced noticeably more reasons, and more specific ones, than people asked why it might happen. Certainty frees the imagination. Explaining a fact is easier than speculating about a possibility, and the mind supplies concrete causes: the supplier who was late, the key person who left.
# It makes dissent safe
The second benefit is social. In an ordinary meeting, pointing out a weakness means criticizing a colleague's plan. In a pre-mortem, finding weaknesses is the assigned task, and the person with the most alarming scenario is being the most helpful. Junior people and quiet people, who often see operational problems the leaders do not, are given a turn. Writing alone first matters. It prevents the group from converging on the first idea spoken by the most senior person.
# What to do with the list
A long list of ways to fail is only useful if it changes something. Group the reasons and pick the handful that are both likely and damaging. For each, ask three questions. Can we prevent it? Can we detect it early? What will we do if it happens anyway? Some risks lead to a change of plan. Some get an owner and a warning sign to watch. A few are accepted knowingly, which is very different from being surprised by them.
# Use it on personal decisions
The exercise works for one person as well as for a team. Before taking a job, moving city or making a large purchase, picture yourself a year on, regretting it. Write the story of what went wrong. The reasons that come easily are the ones to investigate before you commit. Often they point to a question you have been avoiding asking, such as why the last person left the role, or what the commute is really like in winter.
# The opposite exercise
A useful companion is to imagine that the project succeeded beyond expectations and to explain why. This reveals what the plan truly depends on: the two or three things that must go right. Sometimes the two lists together show that success rests on a single assumption nobody has tested. That is worth discovering in a meeting room. It is much cheaper than discovering it eight months and most of the budget later.
# Keep it short and regular
A pre-mortem need not be elaborate. Thirty minutes at the start of a project is enough, and ten will do for a smaller decision. It can be repeated at major milestones, when circumstances have changed and new failure modes have appeared. The aim is not to talk yourself out of acting. Most plans survive the exercise, modified and with a few risks now being watched. The aim is to act with your eyes open.
