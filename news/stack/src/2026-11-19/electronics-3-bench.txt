=== electronics | MOSFETs: switching real power with almost no effort | The parts bin
> The transistor inside nearly every modern device is controlled by voltage, not current. That difference is why it runs cool while switching amps.
# A switch worked by voltage
A MOSFET has three terminals: gate, drain and source. Current flows between drain and source, and the voltage on the gate decides whether that path is open or closed. The gate is separated from the rest of the device by a thin layer of glass-like oxide, so no steady current flows into it. A bipolar transistor needs base current for as long as it is on. A MOSFET needs only a voltage, held there like pressure on a button.
# The threshold
An N-channel MOSFET begins to conduct when the gate is a few volts above the source. That starting point is the threshold voltage, and it is often misread. At the threshold the device is barely on. To turn it on fully, the gate needs to be well above that. Standard power MOSFETs want about 10 volts. Parts described as logic level are built to be fully on at 4.5 volts or even 2.5, which is what a microcontroller pin can supply.
# On-resistance
When fully on, a MOSFET behaves like a very small resistor between drain and source. The datasheet gives this figure, the on-resistance, and for power parts it is measured in thousandths of an ohm. That is why MOSFETs dominate at high current. Ten amps through one hundredth of an ohm dissipates just one watt. A bipolar transistor carrying the same current would waste several watts and need a substantial heatsink to survive.
# The basic low-side switch
The standard circuit puts the load between the positive supply and the drain, with the source connected to ground. Drive the gate high and the load turns on. Two small resistors complete it. One of about 100 ohms in series with the gate limits the brief rush of current as the gate charges. One of 10 kilohms or more from gate to ground holds the gate low when the controlling pin is not yet driving, so the load does not twitch on at power-up.
# The gate is a capacitor
Although no steady current enters the gate, it has capacitance, and that capacitance must be charged and discharged each time the device switches. During the moment it is partly on, the MOSFET dissipates a lot of heat. Switch it once a second and this is irrelevant. Switch it tens of thousands of times a second, as in a motor controller, and it matters greatly. That is why fast circuits use a dedicated driver chip to push the gate up and down quickly.
# N-channel and P-channel
N-channel MOSFETs turn on with the gate above the source and are used to switch the ground side of a load. P-channel devices turn on with the gate below the source and are used to switch the positive side. N-channel parts are cheaper and have lower resistance for their size, so designers use them wherever they can. Inside logic chips, the two types are paired so that one is always off, which is the arrangement known as CMOS.
# Handle with a little care
The oxide under the gate is extremely thin, and a static discharge from your finger can punch through it. Most gates are rated for only about 20 volts either way. Modern parts have some protection, but it is sensible to touch something grounded before handling them and to keep them in their conductive packaging until needed. A MOSFET damaged by static may fail at once or may work for a while and then die without warning.
# The built-in diode
Every power MOSFET contains a diode between source and drain as a by-product of how it is made. It points the opposite way to normal current flow. This means a MOSFET cannot block current in the reverse direction: connect the supply backwards and the diode conducts. Sometimes the diode is useful, carrying the kickback current from a motor. Either way, you need to know it is there. It appears on the schematic symbol as a small diode drawn across the transistor.

=== electronics | Op-amps: two rules that explain almost everything | The parts bin
> The operational amplifier is the universal building block of analog electronics. Two simple rules let you analyse most circuits built around one.
# A very high gain amplifier
An op-amp has two inputs, marked plus and minus, and one output. It amplifies the difference between the inputs by an enormous factor, typically 100,000 or more. On its own that is nearly useless, because the tiniest difference slams the output against one supply rail or the other. The cleverness lies in feeding part of the output back to the minus input. This is called negative feedback, and it tames the huge gain into something precise and predictable.
# The two golden rules
With negative feedback in place, two rules describe what happens. First, the inputs draw no current. Second, the output does whatever it must to make the voltages at the two inputs equal. Neither is exactly true, but both are close enough for nearly all design work. With those two statements and Ohm's law, you can work out the behaviour of amplifiers, filters, adders and many other circuits without knowing anything about the transistors inside the chip.
# The follower
The simplest circuit connects the output straight back to the minus input and puts the signal on the plus input. By the second rule, the output must equal the input. That sounds pointless, but it is very useful. The input draws no current, so it does not disturb a delicate source such as a sensor, while the output can drive a load. This arrangement is called a buffer or voltage follower. It solves the loading problem that spoils a plain voltage divider.
# Setting gain with two resistors
Add a pair of resistors and you get amplification. In the non-inverting amplifier, a divider feeds a fraction of the output back to the minus input, and the gain is one plus the ratio of the two resistors. A 9 kilohm and a 1 kilohm resistor give a gain of exactly ten. In the inverting amplifier, the gain is simply the ratio of the feedback resistor to the input resistor, with the signal flipped. The chip's own enormous gain drops out of the result.
# Limits you will meet
Real op-amps have boundaries. The output cannot swing beyond the supply voltages, and with many parts it stops a volt or two short of them. Types described as rail to rail come much closer. Speed is limited too. A figure called the gain bandwidth product says how much gain is available at a given frequency. A part rated at 1 megahertz can provide a gain of 100 only up to about 10 kilohertz. Ask for more and the output lags and distorts.
# The comparator
Without feedback, an op-amp simply compares its inputs. If the plus input is higher, the output goes to the positive rail. If lower, to the negative. That is a comparator, useful for detecting when a voltage crosses a threshold, such as a battery falling below a safe level or a room getting dark. Dedicated comparator chips do the job faster and more cleanly, but an ordinary op-amp will serve at low speeds.
# Classic parts
A handful of op-amps have been in production for decades. The 741, introduced by Fairchild in 1968, is the textbook example and is still sold. The LM358 contains two amplifiers, runs from a single supply and costs a few cents. The TL072 is a low-noise favourite in audio equipment. Modern designs offer lower noise, lower power and faster response, but the pin layout of the common eight-pin dual op-amp has barely changed, so newer parts usually drop into old sockets.
# Where they turn up
Op-amps condition the tiny signals from sensors before a microcontroller measures them. They are the heart of audio mixers, guitar pedals and equalizers. They form active filters that select or reject ranges of frequency. They regulate, integrate, add and subtract. The name comes from their original job in analog computers of the 1940s, performing mathematical operations on voltages. If you open almost any device with a microphone or a sensor, you will find at least one.

=== electronics | The multimeter: the one tool to buy first | On the bench
> If you own a single piece of test equipment, this is it. Knowing its four main functions, and its one dangerous mistake, covers most of what you need.
# What it measures
A digital multimeter measures the three basic quantities: voltage, current and resistance. Most also check continuity with a beep and test diodes, and many add capacitance, frequency or temperature. A perfectly serviceable one costs about the price of a restaurant meal. The black lead goes in the socket marked COM and stays there. The red lead goes in the socket marked for volts and ohms for almost everything you will do.
# Voltage: across the part
To measure voltage, set the dial to volts, DC or AC as appropriate, and touch the probes to the two points you want to compare. The meter sits in parallel with the circuit, which stays powered and undisturbed. For most work, clip the black lead to ground and probe with the red. Because a meter in this mode has a very high resistance, typically 10 megohms, it draws almost nothing and rarely affects the circuit being measured.
# Resistance: power off
To measure resistance, set the dial to ohms and touch the probes to each end of the part. The meter passes a small current of its own through the part and measures the result. This only works if the circuit is unpowered. Other parts connected in parallel will lower the reading, so for an accurate value, disconnect one end of the component first. Touching the probe tips together should read close to zero, which checks your leads.
# Continuity: the beep
The continuity setting sounds a tone when the resistance between the probes is very low. It is the fastest way to answer everyday questions. Is this wire broken? Are these two pins connected? Has this fuse blown? Is this switch working? Because you can listen instead of looking at the display, you can keep your eyes on the probes. Like resistance, it must be used with the power off. Many experienced technicians use this function more than any other.
# Current: through the meter
Current is the awkward one. The meter must become part of the circuit, so you break the path and connect the probes across the gap, making the current flow through the meter. You must also move the red lead to the socket marked for amps or milliamps. Start on the higher current range if you are unsure. For many jobs it is easier to measure the voltage across a known resistor and calculate the current with Ohm's law.
# The classic mistake
In current mode the meter is nearly a dead short between its probes. If you leave the red lead in the amps socket and then try to measure a voltage, you place that short across the supply. On a small battery this blows the meter's internal fuse. On a car battery or mains it can be violent. Make a habit of returning the red lead to the volts socket as soon as you finish any current measurement.
# Safety ratings
Meters carry a category rating printed near the sockets, from CAT I to CAT IV, describing the electrical environments they are built to survive. Low-voltage electronics work needs nothing special. Measuring household mains calls for a meter genuinely rated for it, with properly fused inputs and insulated probes, from a reputable maker. Very cheap meters often claim ratings they do not meet. If you are not trained to work on mains wiring, the right setting is not to measure it at all.
# Getting the most from it
A few habits make the tool far more useful. Check the meter on a known source, such as a fresh battery, before trusting a surprising reading. Replace its battery when the low-battery symbol appears, since readings drift before the display dims. Buy a set of clip-on probe tips so your hands are free. And remember what the numbers mean. A reading is a clue about the circuit, and the skill lies in knowing what value you expected to see.

=== electronics | Soldering that works the first time | On the bench
> A good solder joint takes three seconds and lasts decades. A bad one looks almost the same. The difference is where you put the heat.
# What solder is
Solder is a metal alloy that melts at a low temperature and bonds to other metals as it cools, forming an electrical and mechanical connection. The traditional alloy is 63 percent tin and 37 percent lead, which melts at 183 degrees Celsius. Lead-free solder, required in most commercial products, is mostly tin with a little silver and copper and melts closer to 220 degrees. Lead-free is somewhat harder to work with. Beginners practising at home often find leaded solder more forgiving.
# Flux does the chemistry
Metal surfaces exposed to air are covered in a thin layer of oxide, and solder will not stick to oxide. Flux is a mild chemical that removes it at soldering temperature and lets the molten metal flow and wet the surface. Electronics solder has flux in its core, which is where the smoke comes from. For surface mount work or reworking old joints, adding extra flux from a pen or syringe makes an enormous difference to how easily the solder flows.
# The iron
A temperature-controlled iron is worth the modest extra cost over a plain one. Set it to around 320 to 350 degrees Celsius for leaded solder and a little hotter for lead-free. Keep the tip clean and shiny by wiping it on brass wool and melting a little fresh solder onto it, a step called tinning. A dull, blackened tip transfers heat poorly, and poor heat transfer is behind most beginner frustration. Never file a tip, since the plating is thin.
# Heat the joint, not the solder
The technique has one essential rule. Touch the tip of the iron so that it heats both the component lead and the copper pad at once. After a second or so, feed solder into the junction of lead and pad, not onto the iron. The solder should melt against the hot parts and flow around them. Remove the solder, then the iron. The whole operation takes two to four seconds. Longer than that risks lifting the pad or overheating the part.
# What good looks like
A good joint on a through-hole board is a small, smooth cone that climbs the lead and spreads across the whole pad, with a slightly concave profile. With leaded solder it is bright and shiny. Lead-free joints are naturally duller, so judge them by shape. A ball perched on the pad means the pad never got hot. A joint that looks grainy or cracked was disturbed while it cooled. Either is called a cold joint, and it may conduct only intermittently.
# Fixing mistakes
Too much solder, or a bridge between two neighbouring pins, is easily cured. Desoldering braid is a ribbon of fine copper that soaks up molten solder when pressed onto the joint with the iron. A spring-loaded pump sucks it away. For a bridge between closely spaced pins, adding flux and drawing the clean iron tip along the pins often clears it. Reheating a doubtful joint with a touch of fresh solder, which brings fresh flux, repairs most cold joints.
# Safety and habits
The iron will burn skin instantly, so always return it to its stand and never try to catch one that falls. The smoke is vaporized flux, which irritates the lungs, so work with a window open or a small fan drawing it away from your face. Wear glasses, because clipped leads fly and molten solder occasionally spits. If you use leaded solder, wash your hands before eating. Unplug the iron when you leave the bench.
# How to practise
Skill at soldering comes quickly with repetition. Buy a cheap practice kit or use a piece of prototype board and a handful of resistors, and make fifty joints in an evening. Inspect each one under good light. Then try removing a few parts cleanly, which teaches as much as attaching them. Within a couple of sessions the timing becomes automatic. After that, surface mount work is the same skill with tweezers, more flux and a steadier hand.

=== electronics | Breadboards: building without solder | On the bench
> A plastic block full of holes lets you assemble a circuit in minutes and change it in seconds. Knowing how the holes connect saves hours of confusion.
# What is under the holes
A solderless breadboard is a plastic block with hundreds of small holes, each sitting above a metal spring clip. Push a component lead or a wire into a hole and the clip grips it. The clips are joined in groups beneath the surface, so two leads pushed into the same group are electrically connected with no solder. Pull a part out and the connection is gone. The name comes from early radio hobbyists who built circuits on wooden boards meant for slicing bread.
# The pattern of connections
The main area is divided into short rows of five holes. The five holes in each row are connected to one another and to nothing else. A groove runs down the centre of the board, and rows on opposite sides of the groove are not connected. Along each long edge run one or two strips, usually marked with red and blue lines, connected along their whole length. These are the power rails, intended for the supply voltage and ground.
# Why the centre groove exists
The groove is spaced to fit a standard chip in a dual in-line package, the kind with two parallel rows of legs. Place the chip straddling the groove and each leg lands in its own row of five, with four free holes beside it for connections. If you put a chip anywhere else, its legs on one side would be shorted together. Small push buttons are sized to straddle the groove too.
# The split rail trap
On many full-size boards, the power rails are broken in the middle, so the left half and right half are separate. There is often a visible gap in the red and blue lines at that point, but not always. Beginners regularly power one end of the board and wonder why chips at the other end are dead. If your board has the break, add a short jumper wire across it on each rail. Check with the continuity beep if unsure.
# Wire and parts that fit
Use solid core wire of about 22 gauge, or ready-made jumper leads with pins on the ends. Stranded wire splays and will not go in. Thick leads, such as those on large diodes or screw terminals, can permanently stretch the clips so that they no longer grip thinner wires afterwards. Keep wires short and flat against the board where you can, and use consistent colours, red for the supply and black for ground. Tidy boards are far easier to debug.
# What breadboards are bad at
The clips and long metal strips add a small amount of resistance and stray capacitance to every connection. For slow signals and modest currents this does not matter. For fast signals, radio circuits or anything above a few megahertz, it can stop a correct design from working. Currents much above an amp will heat the clips. Breadboards are also unreliable over time, as wires work loose. They are for trying ideas, not for anything you intend to keep.
# The usual faults
When a breadboard circuit misbehaves, the cause is usually mechanical. A wire is one row off. A lead is not pushed fully home. A power rail is not connected. A chip is in backwards. Before doubting your design, check that every chip has both power and ground at its pins using a meter, then trace each wire from end to end. Building in small stages and testing after each one makes these errors easy to find.
# Moving on from the breadboard
Once a circuit works, there are several ways to make it permanent. Stripboard and perfboard are cheap copper-clad boards you solder parts onto and wire by hand. Some prototype boards copy the breadboard's row layout exactly, so you can transfer a design hole for hole. For anything you want more than one of, a custom printed circuit board, drawn in free software and ordered online, now costs only a few dollars for a small batch.

=== electronics | How to read a schematic | On the bench
> A schematic is a map of connections, not a picture of a circuit. Once you know the conventions, any diagram becomes readable.
# A map, not a photograph
A schematic shows which parts are connected to which. It deliberately says nothing about where those parts sit on a board or how long the wires are. Two symbols drawn side by side may be at opposite ends of the physical device. That freedom is what makes schematics readable. The designer arranges the symbols to make the logic of the circuit clear, and a separate drawing, the board layout, deals with physical placement.
# The common symbols
A resistor is a zigzag line in American drawings and a small rectangle in European ones. A capacitor is two parallel lines, with one curved or marked with a plus sign if it is polarized. An inductor is a row of loops. A diode is a triangle pointing at a bar. A transistor is a circle or bare lines with three leads, one carrying an arrow. Chips are drawn as boxes with numbered pins. There are perhaps twenty symbols you need to recognize at sight.
# Lines, dots and crossings
A line is a wire, or more precisely an electrical connection. Where two lines meet and are joined, a dot marks the junction. Where two lines cross without a dot, they are not connected. Good drawings avoid four-way junctions entirely because a missing or smudged dot changes the meaning. If you see lines crossing and cannot tell whether they join, look for the dot. No dot means they simply pass over each other.
# Labels instead of wires
On anything but the simplest circuit, drawing every wire would produce a tangle. So designers use labels. Two wire ends carrying the same name, such as RESET or SDA, are connected, even if they are on different pages. Power and ground get their own symbols. Ground is usually a small triangle or a set of three shrinking horizontal lines. Supply rails appear as an arrow or bar with a name such as 5V or VCC. Every identical symbol is the same connection.
# Reference designators
Each part carries a short code. R is a resistor, C a capacitor, L an inductor, D a diode, Q a transistor, U a chip, J a connector, SW a switch and F a fuse. The number after the letter identifies the individual part: R1, R2, C7. The same code is printed on the circuit board beside the part, which is how you get from the diagram to the physical component. Beside each designator is the part's value or type number.
# The usual layout
Schematics follow reading conventions. Signals generally flow from left to right, with inputs on the left and outputs on the right. Higher voltages are drawn toward the top of the page and ground toward the bottom, so current flows downward. Large designs are split into functional blocks, such as power supply, processor and audio output, often one per page. Knowing these habits lets you orient yourself in an unfamiliar diagram within seconds.
# How to approach a new one
Start with the power. Find where it enters, what regulates it and which voltages are produced. Next find the main chips and look up what each one does. Then trace a single signal from input to output, one stage at a time, asking what each part contributes. Do not try to absorb the whole sheet at once. Most circuits are a chain of familiar building blocks: a divider here, a filter there, a transistor switch at the end.
# Drawing your own
When you design or modify a circuit, draw it before you build it. Free programs such as KiCad let you place symbols, wire them and check for errors such as unconnected pins. Keep the drawing tidy, label every signal that leaves a block, and write values beside every part. A clear schematic is the single best aid when something does not work, and it is the document you will thank yourself for six months later when you have forgotten how the thing was wired.
