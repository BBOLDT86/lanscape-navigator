=== electronics | How to read a datasheet | On the bench
> Every part comes with a document that says exactly what it does and what kills it. Knowing which five sections to read turns forty pages into five minutes.
# What a datasheet is
A datasheet is the manufacturer's official description of a component. It states what the part does, how to connect it, what it can tolerate and how it performs. Datasheets are free, and you find one by searching for the part number printed on the component. They range from two pages for a resistor to hundreds for a microcontroller. Nobody reads them from beginning to end. The skill is knowing where to look.
# The first page
Page one is a summary written partly by the marketing department. It gives a one-paragraph description, a list of headline features and typical applications. This is enough to decide whether the part is even a candidate. Treat the numbers here with caution. The headline figure is usually the best case under ideal conditions. The figures you can rely on are in the tables further in, with the test conditions spelled out.
# Absolute maximum ratings
This table lists the limits beyond which the part may be permanently damaged: the highest supply voltage, the highest voltage on any pin, the most current, the temperature range. These are not operating conditions. A part run at its absolute maximum may survive, but nothing about its performance is promised, and its life is shortened. Find this table before you connect power to anything. It tells you which mistakes are fatal.
# Recommended operating conditions
The next table gives the range in which the part is designed to work properly. A chip might have an absolute maximum supply of 6 volts and a recommended range of 2.7 to 5.5. Design to stay inside the recommended range under all circumstances, including a fresh battery, a hot day and a noisy supply. The gap between the recommended and absolute figures is a safety margin for accidents, not extra room for the design.
# Minimum, typical, maximum
The electrical characteristics tables usually have three columns. The typical value is what an average part does at room temperature. It is not guaranteed. The minimum and maximum columns are what the manufacturer promises for every part across the stated conditions. A design that only works with typical values will fail on some units. Always design to the worst-case column that applies, and read the footnotes giving the conditions under which each figure was measured.
# Pinout and package
The pin diagram shows which pin is which, viewed from the top unless stated otherwise. Pin 1 is marked on the physical part by a dot, a notch or a bevelled corner. The same chip often comes in several packages with different pin arrangements, so check that the diagram matches the version you hold. A table lists each pin's name and function. Getting the pinout wrong is the most common way to destroy a part on first power-up.
# The graphs
Further in are charts showing how performance changes with temperature, voltage, current and frequency. These answer the questions the tables cannot. How much does the output voltage drop as the load increases? How does an LED's brightness vary with current? How much does a battery's capacity fall in the cold? When your operating point differs from the single test condition in the table, the graphs are where the real answer lives.
# The application circuit
Most datasheets for chips include a typical application circuit: a complete, working schematic with component values. This is the manufacturer showing you how they expect the part to be used. Start from it. Copy the recommended values and, for power and radio parts especially, follow the layout advice. A great many problems in hobby and professional designs alike come from departing from the reference circuit without a good reason.

=== electronics | The oscilloscope: seeing voltage move | On the bench
> A multimeter tells you a number. An oscilloscope shows you a picture of how that number changes thousands or millions of times a second.
# What it draws
An oscilloscope plots voltage against time. The vertical axis is voltage and the horizontal axis is time, sweeping from left to right. A steady DC voltage appears as a flat horizontal line. A signal that varies shows its shape: a smooth sine wave, the square edges of a digital signal, a brief spike. A meter would average all of that into a single reading. The scope shows what is actually happening, which is often the only way to find a fault.
# The two main knobs
Two controls set the view. The vertical scale, in volts per division, sets how many volts each grid square represents. The horizontal scale, in time per division, sets how much time each square covers. Adjust them until the signal fills a good part of the screen and a few cycles are visible. Most digital scopes have an auto-set button that makes a reasonable first attempt, which is a fine place to start before adjusting by hand.
# The trigger
Without a trigger, a repeating signal would be redrawn at random starting points and smear across the screen. The trigger tells the scope to start each sweep at the same point on the waveform, typically when the voltage crosses a chosen level while rising. The picture then stands still. For a signal that happens only once, single-shot triggering waits for the event and freezes it. Learning to set the trigger is the step that makes a scope useful.
# Probes
The probe is part of the measurement. Standard probes have a switch for times one and times ten. In the times ten position, the signal is reduced tenfold and the probe presents ten times more resistance and less capacitance to the circuit, disturbing it less. Use times ten by default. Each probe also has a small trimmer that should be adjusted, using the square-wave test terminal on the scope, until the edges of the square look clean and flat.
# Bandwidth
Every scope has a bandwidth rating in megahertz. At that frequency, a sine wave is already displayed at only about 70 percent of its true height. Digital signals contain frequencies far above their basic rate, in the sharp edges. A common rule is to choose a scope with a bandwidth at least five times the highest frequency you care about. For audio and most microcontroller work, an entry-level instrument of 50 to 100 megahertz is ample.
# The ground clip warning
The ground clip on a probe is connected, through the scope's chassis, to the earth pin of its mains plug. It is not a floating reference like the black lead of a battery multimeter. If you clip it to a point in a mains-powered circuit that is not at earth potential, you create a short circuit through the scope. For battery-powered and low-voltage projects, clipping to circuit ground is fine. Do not probe mains circuits without proper training and isolation.
# What it reveals
Many faults are invisible to a meter and obvious on a scope. Noise riding on a power rail that resets a microcontroller. A button that produces a dozen pulses when pressed once. A data line with slow, rounded edges. A regulator that oscillates. A signal that arrives at the right voltage but the wrong time. Seeing the shape tells you what kind of problem you have, and half of fixing a circuit is knowing that.
# Getting one
Scopes used to cost as much as a car. A capable four-channel digital bench scope is now within the budget of a keen hobbyist, and pocket-sized or USB instruments cost far less, with limits on speed. For serial data, an inexpensive logic analyser that decodes the bits is often a better tool. If you are deciding whether you need a scope, the usual sign is that you have started guessing at what a signal is doing.

=== electronics | Debugging a dead circuit | On the bench
> Circuits fail for boring reasons. A fixed order of checks finds most faults in minutes and stops you from changing things at random.
# Look before you measure
Start with your eyes and nose, with the power off. Look for a part in backwards, a wire in the wrong hole, a solder bridge between two pins, a joint that never flowed, a component missing entirely. Look for scorch marks, bulging capacitors and cracked parts. Smell for the sharp odour of burnt electronics. Compare the build against the schematic, one connection at a time. A large share of faults are found at this stage without any instrument.
# Power first, always
If nothing obvious appears, apply power and measure the supply. Is the voltage present at the input? Is it present after the regulator, and is it the right value? Is it present at the power pin of every chip, measured at the pin itself and not at the supply? Is ground really connected everywhere? Experienced technicians check power first every time, because a missing or sagging supply produces symptoms that look like a dozen other faults.
# Feel for heat
With the circuit powered, carefully check for anything getting hot. A chip or regulator that is too hot to keep a finger on is usually being asked for too much current, often because of a short downstream or a part fitted backwards. Use the back of a finger and be cautious, since a shorted part can burn. A part that should be warm and is stone cold is also information: it may not be getting power at all.
# Split the circuit in half
For a circuit with several stages, do not check them in order. Check the middle. If the signal is correct at the midpoint, the fault lies in the second half. If not, it is in the first. Then split the faulty half again. This approach finds a fault in a chain of sixteen stages in four measurements. It is the same strategy as looking up a word in a dictionary by opening it in the middle.
# Know what you expect
A measurement is only useful if you have a prediction to compare it with. Before touching the probe to a point, decide what the voltage should be there and why. If the reading matches, move on. If it does not, you have found something. People who probe without expectations collect numbers and learn nothing. Working out what each point ought to read is also how you come to understand the circuit properly.
# Change one thing at a time
When you think you have found the cause, make one change and test. If you change three things and the circuit starts working, you do not know which mattered, and if it gets worse, you do not know which to undo. Keep notes of what you measured and what you changed. Twenty minutes into a difficult fault, it is easy to forget which combinations have been tried, and you start repeating yourself.
# Suspect your assumptions
The hardest faults are the ones where something you were sure about is wrong. The new part is faulty. The wire you trust is broken inside its insulation. The chip is a different version with a different pinout. The supply is fine at rest and collapses under load. When nothing makes sense, list what you have assumed without checking and test each item. Explaining the problem aloud to someone else, or to nobody, often exposes the hidden assumption.
# The usual suspects
Faults cluster. In new builds: wiring errors, reversed parts, bad solder joints, missing ground connections and wrong values. In old equipment: dried-out capacitors, cracked solder joints near connectors and anything that gets hot, corroded contacts and worn switches. In anything with a microcontroller: the code. Before concluding that you have found a rare and interesting failure, rule out the common dull ones. They are the cause nearly every time.

=== electronics | Batteries: chemistry, capacity and care | Power
> A battery is a chemical reaction packaged to deliver electrons on demand. The chemistry inside decides its voltage, its capacity and how it should be treated.
# Voltage comes from chemistry
A battery cell produces a voltage set by the materials inside it, not by its size. An alkaline cell gives about 1.5 volts whether it is a small AAA or a large D. A nickel metal hydride rechargeable gives about 1.2. A lithium-ion cell gives a nominal 3.6 or 3.7. A lead-acid cell gives about 2.1, and six in series make the familiar 12 volt car battery. To get more voltage, cells are stacked in series. Size affects only how long the cell lasts.
# Capacity
Capacity is measured in amp hours or milliamp hours: current multiplied by time. A good AA alkaline holds roughly 2,000 to 3,000 milliamp hours at low drain. A common cylindrical lithium-ion cell holds 2,500 to 3,500. A coin cell of the CR2032 type holds only about 220. To compare batteries of different voltages fairly, multiply capacity by voltage to get watt hours, which is the stored energy.
# Voltage sags as you use it
A battery's voltage is not constant. An alkaline cell starts above 1.5 volts and declines steadily toward 1.0 as it empties, so devices that need a firm 1.5 volts quit while much of the energy remains. Lithium-ion holds a flatter voltage through most of its discharge, from 4.2 volts when full to about 3.0 when empty. This is why a fuel gauge based on voltage alone works tolerably for alkaline cells and poorly for lithium ones.
# Internal resistance
Every battery behaves as if it had a small resistor in series with it. When you draw current, some voltage is lost across that internal resistance, and the battery warms. Alkaline cells have fairly high internal resistance, so they perform poorly in high-drain devices such as camera flashes, delivering a fraction of their rated capacity. Rechargeable cells have much lower internal resistance and handle heavy loads far better, despite their lower nominal voltage.
# The C rate
Charge and discharge currents are often quoted relative to capacity. A rate of 1C means a current that would empty the battery in one hour. For a 2,000 milliamp hour cell, 1C is 2 amps, and half that rate is 1 amp. Each cell type has limits. Ordinary lithium-ion cells are typically charged at half C to 1C. Cells built for power tools and drones can discharge at 10C or more. Exceeding the rating overheats the cell.
# Lithium needs respect
Lithium-ion cells store a great deal of energy in a small space, several times more per kilogram than lead-acid. They must be kept within strict limits. Charged above about 4.2 volts, discharged below about 2.5 to 3, short-circuited or punctured, they can overheat and catch fire. Nearly every pack includes a protection circuit, and they need a charger designed for the chemistry. Do not charge them when they are below freezing. A swollen cell should be retired at once.
# Making them last
Rechargeable batteries wear out with use and with time. For lithium-ion, heat is the main enemy, followed by being held at full charge. A laptop kept plugged in at 100 percent in a warm room ages faster than one cycled between 30 and 80 percent. For long storage, leave lithium cells at around half charge in a cool place. Nickel metal hydride cells of the low self-discharge type hold most of their charge for a year on the shelf.
# Choosing for a project
For a device that sips power for months, such as a remote control or a clock, alkaline cells are cheap and simple. For anything used daily with moderate drain, low self-discharge nickel metal hydride cells pay for themselves quickly. For compact, high-energy designs, lithium-ion or lithium polymer with a proper charging and protection module is standard. For large amounts of energy where weight does not matter, lead-acid remains cheap and forgiving.

=== electronics | Logic levels, pull-ups and floating pins | Digital
> Digital circuits treat voltages as ones and zeros. The rules about where one ends and the other begins explain a whole family of mysterious faults.
# Voltages as bits
A digital signal carries information as one of two states, high or low, usually written 1 and 0. Physically, these are voltage ranges. In a 5 volt system, a voltage near 5 is high and near zero is low. In a 3.3 volt system, high is near 3.3. Most modern microcontrollers and sensors use 3.3 volts or lower, because lower voltages save power and suit smaller transistors. Older designs and many hobby boards still use 5.
# The thresholds
A chip does not require exactly 5 volts or exactly zero. It has thresholds. For a typical 3.3 volt part, anything above about 2 volts is guaranteed to be read as high and anything below about 0.8 volts as low. Between them lies an undefined band where the result is not promised. The gap between what an output delivers and what an input requires is the noise margin, and it is what makes digital circuits tolerant of interference.
# Floating inputs
An input pin connected to nothing is not at zero. It is floating. Modern inputs draw almost no current, so the pin's voltage is set by stray electric fields from nearby wires, the mains and even your hand moving past. It drifts unpredictably between high and low. A circuit with a floating input may work on the bench and fail when you lean over it. Every input must be driven to a defined level at all times.
# Pull-up and pull-down resistors
The standard cure is a resistor that gently holds the pin at a known level when nothing else is driving it. A pull-up connects the pin to the supply. A pull-down connects it to ground. A push button then needs only to connect the pin to the opposite level when pressed. The usual value is around 10 kilohms: low enough to hold the pin firmly, high enough that little current is wasted while the button is held.
# Built-in pull-ups
Most microcontrollers contain optional pull-up resistors on their input pins, switched on in software. They are typically in the range of 20 to 50 kilohms. Enabling one saves an external part. The consequence is that the logic reads backwards from what beginners expect. With a pull-up, the pin reads high when the button is not pressed and low when it is. This arrangement is called active low and is extremely common.
# Mixing 5 and 3.3 volts
Connecting parts that run at different voltages needs care in one direction. A 3.3 volt output driving a 5 volt input often works, since 3.3 volts is above the high threshold of many 5 volt inputs, though not all. A 5 volt output driving a 3.3 volt input can damage it, unless the datasheet states that the pin tolerates 5 volts. In that direction, use a voltage divider for slow signals or a level shifter chip for fast or two-way ones.
# Outputs that only pull down
Some outputs are open drain, meaning they can connect the line to ground but cannot drive it high. They rely on a pull-up resistor to bring the line up when released. This allows several devices to share one wire safely, since none can fight another by driving high while it drives low. The I2C bus works this way. If a line of this kind is stuck low or never goes high, a missing pull-up is the first thing to check.
# How much a pin can drive
A logic output is a signal source, not a power supply. A typical microcontroller pin can safely source or sink something on the order of 10 to 20 milliamps, with a lower limit on the total across all pins. That is enough for an LED with its resistor and not enough for a motor, a relay coil or a strip of lights. Anything heavier needs a transistor or driver chip between the pin and the load.

=== electronics | What is inside a microcontroller | Digital
> A complete computer on one chip, for the price of a coffee. Knowing its parts explains what it can do and why it differs from the processor in a laptop.
# A computer in one package
A microcontroller combines a processor, memory for the program, working memory and a set of input and output circuits on a single chip. It is designed to be embedded in a product and do one job indefinitely: run a washing machine, read a thermostat, blink a bike light. A microprocessor, by contrast, is only the processor and needs separate memory and support chips. A modern car contains dozens of microcontrollers, and a typical home holds hundreds.
# The processor core
The core fetches instructions from memory and executes them one after another. Small microcontrollers use simple cores, 8 bits or 32 bits wide, running at a few million to a few hundred million instructions per second. That is slow next to a laptop and more than enough to read a sensor and decide what to do thousands of times a second. The core in the chip at the heart of the classic Arduino Uno is an 8-bit design clocked at 16 megahertz.
# Three kinds of memory
Flash memory holds the program and keeps it when power is removed. RAM holds variables while the program runs and is wiped at power-off. Many chips add a small amount of EEPROM for settings that must survive. The quantities are tiny by computer standards. The Arduino Uno's chip has 32 kilobytes of flash and just 2 kilobytes of RAM. Writing software for such a device means being careful with every byte.
# Pins that do many jobs
General purpose input and output pins are the chip's contact with the world. Each can be set by software as an input, to read a switch or sensor, or as an output, to drive a signal high or low. Most pins have alternative functions as well, selected in software: a serial port, an analog input, a timer output. This is why the same chip can serve in thousands of different products with only the program changed.
# Timers and counters
Hardware timers count clock ticks independently of the main program. They measure intervals, generate precisely timed pulses, produce the pulse-width signals that dim LEDs and steer servo motors, and wake the chip at set times. Because they run in hardware, their timing stays exact even while the processor is busy. A good deal of embedded programming consists of configuring timers correctly, and they are the first peripheral worth learning in depth.
# Talking to analog and digital parts
An analog to digital converter turns a voltage on a pin into a number, which is how a microcontroller reads a temperature sensor or a battery level. Serial interfaces let it exchange data with other chips over two to four wires, using standards called UART, SPI and I2C. With those two capabilities, one small chip can read nearly any sensor and control displays, radios, memory cards and motor drivers.
# Interrupts and sleep
An interrupt lets an event, such as a pin changing or a timer expiring, pause the main program and run a short routine immediately. This lets the chip respond in microseconds without constantly checking. Combined with sleep modes, in which most of the chip is switched off, it is the key to long battery life. A well-designed sensor node may sleep at a few millionths of an amp and wake for a few thousandths of a second each minute.
# Choosing one
For learning, pick a board with good documentation and a large community before worrying about specifications. Classic 8-bit Arduino boards are simple and forgiving. Boards based on 32-bit chips offer far more speed and memory for similar money. Some, such as the ESP32 family, include Wi-Fi and Bluetooth radios on the chip. The skills transfer between all of them. Pins, timers, converters and serial ports work on the same principles everywhere.
