=== life | Sleep: what it is doing while you are out | The body
> You spend a third of your life asleep, and it is not downtime. Knowing the machinery makes the standard advice easier to follow.
# Two systems set your sleep
Sleep is governed by two processes working together. The first is pressure. A chemical called adenosine builds up in the brain during every hour you are awake, and the more of it there is, the sleepier you feel. Sleep clears it. The second is your body clock, a roughly 24-hour rhythm that raises alertness during the day and lowers it at night regardless of how long you have been awake. You fall asleep most easily when high pressure meets a falling clock.
# Light sets the clock
The clock is tuned mainly by light reaching the eyes. Bright light in the morning pulls it earlier and makes you sleepy at a sensible hour. Bright light late in the evening pushes it later. As darkness falls, the brain releases melatonin, a signal that night has begun. Indoor lighting and screens are much dimmer than daylight but still enough to delay that signal. Getting outside soon after waking is one of the most effective and least used ways to sleep better.
# The night has a structure
Sleep runs in cycles of about ninety minutes. Each cycle passes through light sleep, deep sleep and a stage called REM, when most vivid dreaming occurs. The mix changes through the night. Deep sleep dominates the first half and appears to matter most for physical recovery. REM dominates the second half and is closely tied to memory and emotion. This is why cutting a night short by two hours does not cost a quarter of each stage. It removes a large share of your REM.
# How much is enough
Sleep organizations recommend that most adults get at least seven hours a night, and many people function best with eight or a little more. The need varies somewhat between individuals but less than people like to think. Those who insist they are fine on five hours usually show measurable deficits when tested. A hallmark of chronic short sleep is that you stop noticing it. Your sense of your own performance levels off while your actual performance keeps falling.
# What a short night costs
After 17 to 19 hours awake, reaction time and judgement in laboratory tests are impaired about as much as with a blood alcohol level of 0.05 percent, and after a full 24 hours it is worse. That is the state in which many people drive home from a long shift. Sleep also consolidates what you learned during the day, so a late night of study followed by little sleep is partly wasted. Persistent short sleep is associated with weight gain, raised blood pressure and lower mood.
# The usual suspects
Caffeine works by blocking adenosine, and half of a dose is typically still in your system five or six hours later, so a late afternoon coffee is still active at bedtime. Alcohol makes you drowsy but breaks up sleep in the second half of the night and suppresses REM. A warm room interferes too, since body temperature needs to fall for sleep to begin. Most people sleep best in a room that feels slightly cool, with the feet and hands free to shed heat.
# The habits that matter most
The single most useful rule is a consistent wake time, including weekends, because it anchors the clock. After that: daylight in the morning, caffeine only in the first half of the day, a dark and cool bedroom, and a bed used for sleep, not for work or scrolling. If you cannot sleep, get up and do something quiet until you feel sleepy. Loud snoring with pauses in breathing, or tiredness that persists despite enough time in bed, is worth raising with a doctor.

=== life | Strength training: the short version | The body
> Lifting heavy things on purpose is among the best-supported habits for a long and capable life. The principles fit on one page.
# Why bother
Muscle is not just for appearance. It moves you, protects joints, stores and burns fuel, and keeps you independent in old age. From roughly the fourth decade of life, adults who do nothing lose muscle steadily, a few percent each decade and faster after sixty. Strength falls faster still. Resistance training slows and partly reverses that decline at any age, including in people in their eighties and nineties. Health guidelines recommend working all the major muscle groups at least twice a week.
# The one principle
Everything in strength training follows from a single idea called progressive overload. The body adapts to demands slightly greater than those it is used to. Ask a muscle to do a little more than last time, give it time to recover, and it comes back slightly stronger. Ask it to do the same thing forever and it stays the same. More can mean more weight, more repetitions, more sets or better control. If the numbers in your training log never change, neither will you.
# A handful of movements
You do not need dozens of exercises. Most of the body can be trained with a few basic patterns: a squat, a hip hinge such as a deadlift, a push such as a press-up or bench press, a pull such as a row or pull-up, and carrying something heavy. These movements use several joints and many muscles together, which makes them efficient. Machines, free weights, resistance bands and body weight all work. The best equipment is whichever kind you have access to and will actually use. A pair of adjustable dumbbells and a doorway pull-up bar are enough to train for years at home.
# How hard, how much
A set should finish with the last few repetitions being genuinely difficult, leaving perhaps one to three more in reserve. Research shows muscle can be built across a wide range of repetitions, from about five to thirty, as long as the effort is high. Lower repetitions with heavier weights favour maximal strength. For most people, two to four sets of each movement, two or three times a week, is enough to make steady progress for years.
# Recovery is when you grow
Training provides the signal. The adaptation happens afterwards. A muscle group generally needs about two days before it is trained hard again, which is why programmes alternate body parts or schedule rest days. Sleep and sufficient protein matter as much as anything you do in the gym. Soreness a day or two after a new exercise is normal and fades as the body adapts. It is not a measure of how effective the session was.
# Technique and safety
Learn each movement with a light load and add weight only when you can control it through the full range. Progress slowly. Adding a small amount each week accumulates into a large change over a year. Sharp or joint pain, as opposed to the dull effort of working muscle, is a signal to stop and reassess. A session or two with a qualified coach at the start is money well spent. Anyone with a heart condition or other medical concern should check with a clinician first.
# What to expect
Beginners improve quickly. Much of the early gain is the nervous system learning to use muscle you already have, which is why strength rises before any visible change. After several months, progress slows and patience becomes the main requirement. The people with the best results after five years are seldom those who followed the cleverest programme. They are the ones who kept turning up twice a week, wrote down what they lifted and tried to do slightly more.

=== life | Cardio: how much, how hard | The body
> Your heart and lungs adapt to what you ask of them. The guidelines are modest, the benefits are large, and most of the work should feel easy.
# What fitness means here
Cardiorespiratory fitness is your body's ability to take in oxygen and deliver it to working muscles. It is commonly expressed as VO2 max, the greatest amount of oxygen you can use in a minute of all-out effort. Large studies following people for many years have found it to be one of the strongest predictors of how long they live, comparable to or stronger than better-known risk factors. The largest improvement in outlook comes from moving out of the least fit group, not from becoming an athlete.
# The guideline
The World Health Organization and national health bodies recommend that adults get 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity each week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, or a mixture. Moderate means brisk walking, easy cycling or anything that raises your breathing while still allowing conversation. Vigorous means running, fast cycling or anything that reduces you to a few words at a time. One minute of vigorous effort counts for about two of moderate.
# The talk test
You do not need a monitor to judge intensity. If you can speak in full sentences but could not sing, you are working at a moderate level. If you can only manage short phrases, it is vigorous. If you can sing, it is light. For those who do track heart rate, a common estimate of maximum is 220 minus your age, though for any individual that figure can be off by ten beats or more. Treat it as a rough guide, not a limit.
# Easy most of the time
Endurance athletes do the bulk of their training at a comfortable, conversational effort, often called zone 2. Work at this level builds the heart's capacity to pump, increases the number of small blood vessels in muscle and multiplies the mitochondria that turn fuel into energy. It can be repeated day after day with little risk of injury or burnout. Beginners often go too hard in every session, which feels productive and makes it difficult to do enough total volume.
# Hard some of the time
A smaller amount of high-intensity work raises the ceiling. Intervals, such as four minutes hard followed by a few minutes easy, repeated several times, are a well-studied way to increase VO2 max. One or two such sessions a week is plenty for most people. A split often quoted by coaches is about 80 percent easy and 20 percent hard. The easy work builds the base and the hard work sharpens it. Either alone does less than the combination.
# Sitting and moving
Formal exercise is only part of the picture. Long unbroken hours of sitting are associated with poorer health even among people who exercise, and breaking them up with a few minutes of walking helps. Everyday movement counts: stairs, errands on foot, gardening. Studies of step counts suggest the benefits rise steeply from very low numbers and begin to level off well before the famous figure of ten thousand, which began as a marketing slogan for a Japanese pedometer in the 1960s.
# Getting started
If you are doing nothing now, begin with a ten-minute brisk walk each day and add five minutes a week. Pick something you do not hate, because the best exercise is the one you repeat. Increase your weekly total gradually, by about a tenth at a time, to let tendons and joints keep pace with your lungs. Chest pain, unusual breathlessness or dizziness during exertion are reasons to stop and seek medical advice, as is starting from a long-standing medical condition.

=== life | Protein: how much you need and why | Food
> Protein is the one nutrient your body cannot store for later. The basics of how much, when and from what are simpler than the supplement aisle suggests.
# What it does
Proteins are chains of smaller units called amino acids. Your body uses them to build and repair muscle, skin, organs, enzymes, hormones and antibodies. Unlike fat and carbohydrate, protein has no dedicated storage depot. Tissue is constantly being broken down and rebuilt, and the amino acids must be resupplied by food. Of the twenty amino acids the body uses, nine are called essential because it cannot make them. Those must come from what you eat. A diet short of protein for a long period shows up as lost muscle, slow healing and, in children, poor growth.
# The official minimum
The recommended dietary allowance in the United States and many other countries is 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. For a person weighing 70 kilograms, that is 56 grams. It is important to understand what this number is. It is the amount estimated to prevent deficiency in nearly all healthy adults. It was not designed as a target for people who are active, building muscle, losing weight or growing older.
# When more helps
Research on people doing resistance training points to a higher figure for building muscle. A widely cited analysis of dozens of trials found that the benefit of extra protein levelled off at around 1.6 grams per kilogram per day, with some individuals possibly gaining from a little more. Older adults appear to need more than the minimum to maintain muscle, because ageing muscle responds less strongly to a given dose. People eating less to lose weight also preserve more muscle on a higher intake.
# Sources
Animal foods such as meat, fish, eggs and dairy contain all nine essential amino acids in useful proportions. Most plant sources are lower in one or more of them, but the gaps differ from food to food, so eating a variety covers them. Beans with rice is the classic pairing. Soy is close to complete on its own. People eating only plants can meet their needs comfortably. They simply need somewhat more total protein and a mix of sources across the day.
# Spread it out
The body can only use so much protein for building muscle at one sitting. Many researchers suggest that around 20 to 40 grams per meal, depending on body size, is enough to stimulate that process fully. Typical eating patterns put little protein at breakfast and a great deal at dinner. Shifting some of it earlier in the day is an easy improvement. An egg or two, yoghurt or leftovers from the night before will do it. The total across the day still matters most, so treat the timing as fine tuning.
# What 30 grams looks like
It helps to have a few reference points. A chicken breast or a similar piece of fish the size of your palm has about 30 grams. Three eggs have about 18. A cup of Greek yoghurt has roughly 20. A cup of cooked lentils has about 18. A glass of milk has 8. Protein powders are a convenience, not a necessity. They are simply food in concentrated form, and useful mainly when whole foods are impractical.
# Sensible limits
For healthy people, intakes well above the minimum have not been shown to harm the kidneys. People with existing kidney disease are a different case and should follow medical advice. Protein also keeps you full for longer than the same calories from carbohydrate or fat, which is one reason higher-protein diets help with weight control. The practical summary is short: include a palm-sized portion of a protein-rich food at each meal, and a little more if you train hard or are past sixty.

=== life | Caffeine: how the world's favourite drug works | The body
> Most adults take it every day. It does not give you energy. It hides the signal that you are tired, which is useful if you know the rules.
# It blocks a brake
While you are awake, a substance called adenosine accumulates in the brain and attaches to receptors that make you feel progressively sleepier. Caffeine has a similar shape. It fits into the same receptors without activating them, so the adenosine cannot deliver its message. You feel more alert because a brake has been disabled, not because fuel has been added. The adenosine is still there. When the caffeine clears, it floods back, which is the afternoon crash.
# How long it lasts
Caffeine is absorbed quickly and reaches its peak in the blood within about an hour. The body then removes it slowly. The half-life, the time for half of a dose to be cleared, averages about five hours in healthy adults and ranges widely, from roughly two to ten. Genetics, smoking, pregnancy and some medicines all shift it. So a coffee at 4 in the afternoon may leave a quarter of its caffeine still at work at 2 in the morning.
# How much is in what
A typical mug of brewed coffee contains somewhere around 80 to 100 milligrams of caffeine, though strength varies widely between cafes and brewing methods. A single shot of espresso has about 60 to 65. A cup of black tea has 40 to 50, and green tea somewhat less. A can of cola has 30 to 40. Energy drinks range from about 80 to more than 300 per can, so the label is worth reading. Decaffeinated coffee is not caffeine free, containing a few milligrams a cup. Dark chocolate contributes a little as well, and so do some pain relief tablets.
# The usual ceiling
The United States Food and Drug Administration cites 400 milligrams a day, roughly four cups of brewed coffee, as an amount not generally associated with harmful effects in healthy adults. Sensitivity varies a great deal. Some people feel jittery and anxious on one cup. Advice for pregnancy is lower, and anyone pregnant or with a heart rhythm problem should follow their clinician's guidance. Pure caffeine powder is dangerous, because a lethal amount fits on a spoon.
# Tolerance and withdrawal
With daily use, the brain compensates by making more adenosine receptors. The same dose then produces less effect, and regular drinkers need their morning cup simply to feel normal. Stop suddenly and the surplus receptors have nothing blocking them. The result is a headache, fatigue and irritability beginning within about a day and lasting up to a week or so. Cutting down gradually over a couple of weeks avoids most of it. Weekend headaches in people who drink a lot of coffee at work and little at home are often withdrawal.
# Performance
Caffeine is one of the few supplements with solid evidence behind it. It improves endurance, reaction time and alertness, and it is permitted in sport. The effect is clearest when you are tired or under-slept. It does not replace sleep, however. It masks sleepiness while judgement and memory remain impaired, which matters if you are driving. A short nap taken right after a coffee, timed so the caffeine arrives as you wake, is a well-tested trick for long drives.
# Using it well
A few rules get most of the benefit. Have your last caffeine at least eight hours before bed. Wait an hour or so after waking for the first cup if you find it helps you avoid an afternoon slump. Do not use it to push through chronic tiredness, which is a problem to solve, not to cover. And take an occasional week at a lower dose, which restores the effect of the amount you normally drink.

=== life | Water: how much you actually need | The body
> Eight glasses a day is a rule nobody can trace to solid evidence. Your body already has a precise system for this, and it mostly works.
# Where the rule came from
The advice to drink eight glasses of water a day is repeated everywhere and has no clear scientific origin. One likely source is a 1945 American recommendation that adults need about 2.5 litres of water daily. The next sentence, usually forgotten, said that most of this is contained in prepared foods. Reviews by physicians looking for evidence behind the eight-glass rule have come up empty. It is not harmful advice for most people. It is simply not a requirement. Carrying a large bottle everywhere became fashionable long after the rule appeared, not because new evidence arrived.
# What the body needs
Needs vary with body size, activity, climate and diet. The United States National Academies have suggested an adequate total intake of about 3.7 litres a day for men and 2.7 for women. The important word is total. That figure includes water from all drinks and from food, which typically supplies around a fifth of it. Fruit, vegetables, soup and even bread contain a good deal of water. Nobody needs to drink the whole amount from a bottle.
# Thirst works
Your body regulates its water content tightly. When the concentration of your blood rises by even one or two percent, the brain triggers thirst and tells the kidneys to hold water back. For healthy people in ordinary conditions, drinking when thirsty and with meals is enough. The claim that by the time you feel thirsty you are already dangerously dehydrated is not supported. Thirst arrives well before any harm, which is precisely what it evolved to do.
# Coffee and tea count
Caffeine is a mild diuretic, which led to the belief that coffee and tea dehydrate you. In people who drink them regularly, the effect is small, and the water in the cup far outweighs it. Studies comparing moderate coffee intake with the same volume of water have found no meaningful difference in hydration. Milk, juice and sparkling water all count as well. Alcohol is the real exception, since it suppresses the hormone that tells the kidneys to conserve water.
# A simple check
The colour of your urine is a practical gauge. Pale yellow, like straw, indicates you are well hydrated. Dark yellow or amber suggests you need more fluid. Completely clear usually means you are drinking more than you need. Some vitamins and foods change the colour, so it is a rough guide. Other signs of needing water are a dry mouth, headache, tiredness and passing urine only a few times in a day. A mild headache late in a busy afternoon is quite often nothing more than a morning without a drink.
# When to drink more
Thirst is less reliable in some situations. Older adults often have a weaker thirst response and can become dehydrated without noticing. Hard exercise, hot weather, fever, vomiting and diarrhoea all raise losses quickly. In those cases, drink on a schedule. During long, sweaty exertion, some salt matters too, because sweat carries sodium out with the water. Persistent vomiting or diarrhoea, especially in a small child or an elderly person, needs medical attention.
# Too much is possible
Drinking far more than you lose can dilute the sodium in your blood to dangerous levels, a condition called hyponatraemia. It has killed marathon runners and military recruits who were told to drink as much as possible. The modern advice for endurance events is to drink to thirst, not to a fixed plan. For everyday life, there is no benefit in forcing down water you do not want. Keep a glass within reach, drink when you feel like it and let the kidneys do the arithmetic.
