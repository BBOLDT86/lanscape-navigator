=== poker | Counting combinations: how many ways can they have it | The math you need
> You cannot see their cards, but you can count how many versions of each hand exist. That count is what turns a guess into an estimate.
# The deck is finite
There are 1,326 different two-card starting hands in hold'em. They are not equally represented. Any specific pocket pair, such as two kings, can be dealt 6 ways. Any specific unpaired hand, such as ace king, can be dealt 16 ways: 4 of them suited and 12 offsuit. So before any cards are seen, an opponent is dealt ace king nearly three times as often as a pair of aces. Hand reading begins with these three numbers: 6, 4 and 12.
# How often premium hands arrive
The same counting tells you how rare good hands are. You will be dealt a pair of aces once in 221 hands. Any pocket pair comes about once in 17 hands. Ace king, suited or not, arrives roughly once in 83. Two suited cards show up a little under a quarter of the time. A player who complains of never getting aces is usually describing an ordinary night, since at a live table of about 30 hands an hour, aces are due about once every seven hours.
# Cards you see remove combinations
Every visible card shrinks the count. If you hold an ace, only three aces remain, and the ways an opponent can hold two of them drop from 6 to 3. The ways they can hold ace king fall from 16 to 12. If an ace is also on the board, pocket aces drop to a single combination. This effect is called card removal, and the cards in your own hand that reduce an opponent's strong holdings are called blockers.
# Sets against top pair
Counting changes how a board looks. On a flop of king, seven, two, a player might hold a set. There are three ways each to hold kings, sevens or twos, nine in all. The same player could hold ace king, top pair with the best kicker, in 12 ways. So even before considering how they played the hand, top pair hands outnumber sets. When an opponent raises and you fear the monster, counting reminds you that the monster is usually the less likely holding.
# Draws are numerous
On a board with two hearts, think about how many flush draws exist. An opponent could hold any two of the eleven remaining hearts, which is 55 combinations, though most players only play a fraction of those. Straight draws add more. This is why boards with connected or suited cards are called wet: they multiply the number of hands that want to continue. On a dry board such as king, seven, two of three suits, almost no draws exist, and a bet represents a made hand or a pure bluff.
# Value hands against bluffs
Combination counting matters most on the river. Suppose an opponent's line is consistent with 10 combinations of strong hands and 5 combinations of missed draws they would bluff with. A third of the time you face a bluff. If the bet is half the pot, you need to be right only 25 percent of the time, so calling is correct. Shift the count to 15 value hands and 2 bluffs and the same call loses badly. The decision comes from the ratio.
# Practise on hands you already played
You will not count precisely during play. Train afterwards. Take one hand a session, write down the hands your opponent could plausibly hold, and count the combinations of each. Within a few weeks you start to see boards differently. A scary card stops being scary when you realise it completes only four combinations. A harmless-looking card becomes dangerous when it completes thirty. That shift, from picturing one hand to weighing many, is what experienced players mean by thinking in ranges.

=== poker | Variance and bankroll: surviving your own luck | The math you need
> A winning player can lose for weeks. Knowing how wide the swings run, and keeping enough money to ride them, is part of the skill.
# Skill and luck on different clocks
In one hand, luck decides nearly everything. Over a session, luck still dominates. Over tens of thousands of hands, skill takes over. This mismatch is the central fact of poker life. Your edge is small and the swings around it are large. A good cash game player might win a few big blinds per hundred hands on average, while the typical up and down movement over the same hundred hands is many times larger. The signal is real, but it is buried.
# What a downswing looks like
Because of that ratio, long losing stretches are normal even for strong players. Simulations of winning players routinely show losing runs of twenty buy-ins or more somewhere in a large sample of hands. A live player seeing about 30 hands an hour needs years to play a hundred thousand hands. So a live player can run badly for months without doing anything wrong, and can also run well for months while playing poorly. Neither stretch proves much. Online players, who can see several hundred hands an hour across multiple tables, reach meaningful samples far sooner, which is one reason the online game produced so many strong players so quickly.
# The bankroll is a tool
A bankroll is money set aside only for poker, separate from rent and savings. Its job is to absorb variance so that a bad run does not end your ability to play. Think of it as a carpenter thinks of tools: working capital, not spending money. If losing your poker money would affect your life, you will play scared, avoid correct but risky plays and make worse decisions. A properly sized bankroll buys the freedom to play well.
# Common guidelines
For no-limit cash games, a widely used rule is to keep at least 20 to 30 full buy-ins for the stakes you play. In a game with a 200 dollar buy-in, that is 4,000 to 6,000 dollars. Tournaments swing far more, because most of the prize money goes to the top few places, so serious tournament players keep 100 buy-ins or more. These are conventions, not laws. A recreational player with a job who can reload from income needs less than someone paying bills from winnings.
# Move down without shame
The other half of bankroll management is dropping to a smaller game when the roll shrinks. If you fall to 15 buy-ins for your usual stake, move down until you rebuild. Pride makes this hard, and refusing to do it is how talented players go broke. Moving down is not an admission that you cannot beat the bigger game. It is an acknowledgment that variance does not care how good you are and that a player with no chips has no edge at all.
# Keep records
Memory is a terrible accountant. People remember big wins and forget steady losses. Write down every session: date, stakes, hours, result. After a few months you will know your real hourly rate and whether particular games, times of day or session lengths cost you money. Many players discover they win in the first three hours and give it back in the fifth and sixth. Without records you are guessing about your own results, and most guesses flatter the guesser.
# Judging yourself honestly
With so much noise, how do you know if you are any good? Not from one month's result. Look at large samples, and look at decisions. Review hands, especially the ones you won, and ask whether you would play them the same way again. Ask stronger players. A healthy attitude holds two ideas together: results in the short run are mostly luck, and results in the long run are entirely your responsibility.

=== poker | Position: the seat is worth more than the cards | Before the flop
> Acting last is the single largest structural advantage in hold'em. Good players build their whole strategy around it.
# Who acts when
Hold'em is played in a circle, and a small disc called the button marks the nominal dealer. After the flop, the player on the button acts last on every round of betting. The two players to the button's left post the forced bets, the small blind and the big blind, and must act first after the flop. Everyone else sits somewhere between. Seats that act early are called early position, and the seats just before the button are late position, the cutoff and the hijack.
# Information is the advantage
Acting last means you see what everyone else does before you decide. If they check, they have told you something. If they bet, you know the price before you commit. The player acting first has to decide blind to all of that. Over a single hand the difference is modest. Over thousands of hands it is enormous. Tracking databases from online poker show the same pattern for nearly every player: the button is their most profitable seat and the blinds are where they lose.
# Control over the pot
Position also gives you control of the size of the pot. With a strong hand, you can bet when checked to and build it. With a medium hand, you can check behind and take a free card or a cheap showdown. With a draw, you decide whether to pay or to see the next card for nothing. Out of position you have none of these choices. If you check, your opponent decides. If you bet, you may be raised and forced into a guess.
# Tight early, wide late
The practical result is that the hands worth playing change with your seat. In early position at a full table, with many players left to act behind you, a sound opening range is narrow, on the order of one hand in seven or eight. On the button, with only the blinds left to act, good players open something like four hands in ten. King nine suited is a clear fold under the gun and a routine raise on the button. The cards are identical. The seat changed their value.
# Why the blinds lose
The blinds are a double handicap. You have put money in without seeing your cards, and you will be out of position for the rest of the hand. Even the best players in the world lose money from the blinds over time. The goal there is not to win but to lose less than the full blind you were forced to post. Defending too many weak hands from the blinds, on the theory that you already have chips invested, is a classic and costly habit.
# Stealing
Because the button and cutoff act late, they often face a pot in which everyone has folded and only the blinds remain. Raising there with a wide range, called stealing the blinds, wins the pot outright a good share of the time. When it does not, the stealer still gets to play the hand in position. A player who folds the button too often is passing up the best seat at the table, and one who never defends the blinds is giving the stealer free money.
# The simple rule
If you remember one thing about position, make it this: play more hands when you will act last and fewer when you will act first. When in doubt about a marginal hand, let your seat break the tie. Many players improve noticeably just by folding the weakest third of the hands they used to play from early position and adding that many on the button. No new skill is required. They are simply playing more of their pots with the advantage.

=== poker | Starting hands: what you are really choosing | Before the flop
> Choosing which hands to play is the first decision and the most frequent. The right answer depends on what kind of hand it is, not just how pretty it looks.
# Most hands miss
Start with a sobering fact. Two unpaired cards will make a pair or better on the flop only about one time in three. Two thirds of the time you have nothing but high cards. That is why starting hand selection matters so much. If you enter pots with weak cards, you will usually miss, and when you do hit, you will often hold a second best hand. Folding before the flop is the cheapest decision in the game, and good players do it most of the time.
# Big pairs
Aces, kings and queens are the premium hands, strong enough to win unimproved. Their weakness is that they are usually still just one pair after the flop, and one pair loses big pots when the board turns ugly and the money goes in. The standard advice is to raise and reraise them before the flop, building a pot while you are a clear favourite and thinning the field. Slow playing aces against several opponents feels clever and often ends with a small win or a large loss.
# Big cards and the kicker problem
Hands such as ace king and ace queen make top pair with a strong second card, called the kicker. That is their job. The danger lies in weaker versions. King jack and ace ten look good but are often dominated, meaning an opponent holds the same high card with a better kicker. When both players make top pair, the dominated hand pays off. Hands like these are where average players lose the most money, because the hand is too good to fold and not good enough to win.
# Suited and connected
Being suited adds only a few percentage points to a hand's raw chance of winning. You will flop a flush just under one time in a hundred, and a flush draw about one time in nine. The value of suited connected cards such as 8 7 of hearts lies elsewhere. They make hidden straights and flushes that win big pots, and when they miss, they are easy to fold. They need deep stacks and good position to earn their keep.
# Small pairs
Pairs from twos through sixes play one main way. You hope to flop three of a kind, which happens about one time in eight and a half. When it does, the hand is strong and well disguised. When it does not, you usually hold a low pair on a board of higher cards and should let it go. The discipline is the difficult part. A small pair that does not improve is rarely worth more than one cheap look.
# Trouble hands
Certain hands have earned the name. Ace with a small unsuited card, king ten offsuit, queen jack offsuit against a raise from early position. They win small pots and lose large ones, because the hands that give them action are the hands that beat them. This does not make them unplayable. On the button, with everyone folded, they are fine raises. Against a raise from a tight player in early position, they are folds, however good they look.
# A workable plan
Until you have studied ranges in detail, a simple scheme serves well. From early seats, play big pairs, ace king and ace queen. From the middle, add medium pairs and suited big cards. From the cutoff and button, add small pairs, suited connectors and suited aces. Enter the pot with a raise when you are first in. Fold the rest without regret. This plan is not optimal, but it avoids the common disasters, and it puts you ahead of most casual tables.

=== poker | Raise or fold: why limping loses | Before the flop
> Just calling the big blind feels cheap and safe. It is the mark of a losing player, and the reasons are structural.
# What a limp is
To limp is to enter the pot before the flop by calling the big blind, not raising. Players do it because it costs little and lets them see a flop with a hand they like but do not love. It is the most common play at casual tables and nearly absent from strong ones. When the first player into the pot has a hand worth playing, the better play is almost always a raise. When the hand is not worth a raise, it is usually not worth playing.
# A raise can win now
The first argument is simple. A limp cannot win the pot before the flop. A raise can. Every time everyone folds, the raiser collects the blinds without seeing a card. Those small pots add up to a real share of a winning player's profit. A limper gives up that entire source of income and must always make the best hand, or bluff later at greater expense, to win anything at all.
# Initiative
The player who made the last aggressive action before the flop holds what is called the initiative. Opponents tend to check to that player on the flop, and a bet from them is believable, because raising before the flop represents a strong hand. This lets the raiser win many pots with a single follow-up bet when nobody has connected. A limper has shown no strength, so a later bet tells a weaker story, and opponents call it more freely.
# Fewer opponents, clearer hands
A raise thins the field. Against one opponent, a pair of kings wins about four times in five against a random hand. Against four opponents holding random hands, it wins only about half the time. Limping invites a crowd, and in a crowd even good hands shrink, because somebody usually makes two pair or better. A raise also narrows what the callers can hold, since most players continue against a raise with only their better hands. That makes the rest of the hand easier to read.
# Limps invite attacks
At a table with observant players, a limp is a target. It announces a hand that is good enough to play but not good enough to raise. Players behind can raise with a wide range, knowing the limper will usually fold or call and then play out of position with a marginal hand. Over time, limpers pay the blind repeatedly and surrender it repeatedly. A limp is too weak to defend and too expensive to keep abandoning. Watch any table for an hour and note who limps most often. It is rarely the player with the biggest stack at the end of the night.
# How much to raise
A standard opening raise is between two and a half and three times the big blind. In live games where players call more loosely, slightly larger is common. If players have already limped in front of you, add about one big blind for each of them. Keep your raise size the same whether you hold aces or a speculative hand. Varying your bet with the strength of your cards gives attentive opponents a free read on your hand.
# The exceptions
There are narrow cases where limping is defensible. In the small blind, with only the big blind left to act, a mixed approach of calls and raises is sound. In very passive games where nobody ever raises, limping behind other limpers with a small pair or suited connector can be profitable, because you are getting a good price in a multi-player pot. These are exceptions for specific situations. As a default, the rule holds: if you are the first to enter the pot, raise or fold.

=== poker | The 3-bet: reraising before the flop | Before the flop
> A reraise before the flop is the strongest ordinary move in the game. Used well, it wins pots immediately and sets up the rest of the hand.
# Why it is called a 3-bet
The big blind counts as the first bet. An opening raise is the second. A reraise over that is therefore the third bet, or 3-bet. A further raise is a 4-bet. Decades ago, players reraised before the flop only with aces and kings, and everyone knew it. Modern players 3-bet a much wider set of hands, some for value and some as bluffs. Learning to do it, and to respond to it, is one of the clearest dividing lines between beginners and experienced players.
# For value
The obvious reason to 3-bet is that you hold a hand that beats most of what the raiser has. With aces, kings, queens and ace king, you want more money in the pot while you are ahead. Just calling with these hands keeps the pot small and lets other players enter cheaply behind you, which reduces your chance of winning. Against opponents who raise many hands and dislike folding, the value range can widen to jacks, tens and ace queen.
# As a bluff
If you only reraise with premium hands, good opponents simply fold everything but their own premiums. So strong players add bluffs. The best candidates are hands that are not quite good enough to call but have useful features. Small suited aces such as ace five of spades are the classic choice. The ace in your hand makes it less likely your opponent holds aces or ace king, and if you are called, the hand can still make the best flush or a hidden straight.
# Sizing
A 3-bet should be large enough that calling is unattractive. A common guide is about three times the original raise when you will have position after the flop, and about four times when you will be out of position, because acting first is a handicap you want to offset by ending the hand sooner. If the opener raised to 3 big blinds, that means roughly 9 from the button and 12 from the blinds. Tiny reraises simply give the opener a great price to call.
# Who to target
The 3-bet works best against players who open too many hands and fold too many of them to pressure. Someone raising from late position with a wide range cannot defend most of it against a reraise. It works poorly against players who only raise strong hands, since their range is already full of hands that will not fold, and poorly against players who call everything, where you should reraise only for value and skip the bluffs entirely.
# Facing a 3-bet
When you are the one reraised, three questions sort it out. How often does this player 3-bet? Against a rare 3-bettor, fold everything but the very top of your range. Will you have position? Calling out of position with a marginal hand is expensive. And how deep are the stacks? With deep stacks, calling with pairs and suited connectors is reasonable. With shallow stacks, the choice narrows to fold or move all in, because there is no room to manoeuvre afterwards.
# What changes after the flop
A 3-bet pot is larger relative to the stacks behind, so decisions come faster and for more money. A single bet on the flop and one on the turn can put a player all in. Top pair is often strong enough to commit with, which it would not be in a small pot. The player who 3-bet also has the stronger range, so bets from that player carry more weight on boards with high cards. All of this follows from one reraise before the flop.
