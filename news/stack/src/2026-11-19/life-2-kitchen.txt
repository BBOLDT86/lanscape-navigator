=== life | Knife skills: the fifteen-minute upgrade | In the kitchen
> One good knife, held properly and kept sharp, makes cooking faster and safer than a drawer full of gadgets.
# One knife does most of it
A chef's knife with a blade of about 20 centimetres, or eight inches, handles nine tenths of kitchen cutting. Add a small paring knife for work in the hand and a serrated knife for bread and tomatoes, and you have all a home cook needs. Large blocks of matched knives mostly go unused. Money is better spent on one comfortable chef's knife and on keeping it sharp than on a dozen mediocre blades.
# The pinch grip
Most people hold a knife by the handle like a hammer. Cooks hold it further forward. Pinch the blade itself between your thumb and the side of your index finger, just in front of the handle, and wrap the other three fingers around the handle. This puts your hand over the balance point and gives far more control of the tip. It feels odd for a day or two. After that, the hammer grip feels clumsy.
# The claw
The other hand matters as much, because it is the one at risk. Curl your fingertips under, so that your knuckles are the part nearest the blade, and tuck your thumb behind your fingers. The flat side of the knife rests lightly against the knuckles, which guide it. As you cut, the claw walks backwards along the food. With fingertips curled away, the worst a slip can do is brush a knuckle with the flat of the blade.
# Let the knife move
A knife cuts by sliding, not by pressing straight down. For most vegetables, keep the tip on the board and rock the blade forward and down, lifting the heel between cuts. For softer foods, draw the blade back toward you in a single long stroke. If you find yourself pushing hard or sawing, the knife is blunt or the motion is wrong. A sharp blade used properly needs very little force.
# Sharp is safe
A blunt knife is the dangerous one. It needs more force, and it slides off the smooth skin of an onion or tomato instead of biting in, heading toward your hand. Test an edge by slicing a ripe tomato or a sheet of paper. If it crushes or tears instead of cutting cleanly, it needs attention. Most kitchen knives in most homes are well past that point, and their owners have simply got used to it.
# Honing and sharpening
The steel rod that comes with knife sets does not sharpen. It straightens an edge that has bent over slightly with use, and a few light strokes each time you cook keeps a knife cutting well for longer. Sharpening actually grinds away metal to form a new edge, using a whetstone or a guided sharpener, and is needed a few times a year. Many kitchen shops and hardware stores will do it for a small charge.
# Board and care
Cut on wood or plastic. Glass, stone and ceramic boards ruin an edge in days. Put a damp cloth under the board to stop it sliding. Wash knives by hand and dry them straight away, since dishwashers knock edges against other items and are hard on handles. Store them on a magnetic strip, in a block or in blade guards, not loose in a drawer, where the edges are damaged and fingers are at risk.
# Practise on an onion
The onion is the standard exercise. Cut it in half through the root and peel it, leaving the root end intact to hold the layers together. Lay a half flat. Make a few horizontal cuts toward the root, then a row of vertical cuts, all stopping short of the root. Then slice across, and even dice falls away. Do this with a few onions a week and within a month your hands will know the grip, the claw and the motion.

=== life | Browning: the chemistry that makes food taste good | Kitchen science
> The crust on a steak, the colour of toast and the smell of roasting coffee come from the same reaction. Controlling it is the core of savoury cooking.
# What the reaction is
When proteins and certain sugars are heated together, they react to form hundreds of new compounds, many of them brown and intensely flavoured. The process is named after Louis-Camille Maillard, the French chemist who described it in 1912. It is responsible for the taste of seared meat, bread crust, roasted coffee, fried onions, dark beer and chocolate. It is distinct from caramelization, which is the browning of sugar alone and needs higher temperatures.
# The temperature threshold
The Maillard reaction proceeds quickly only above roughly 140 degrees Celsius, about 285 Fahrenheit. That figure explains a great deal about cooking. Water boils at 100 degrees and cannot get hotter, so as long as the surface of a food is wet, its temperature is stuck well below the browning range. Boiled and steamed foods never brown. Before a surface can colour, its moisture must first be driven off.
# Dry the surface
The practical rule follows directly. Pat meat and fish dry with paper towels before they go in the pan. Salt meat well ahead, or just before cooking, since salt briefly draws moisture to the surface. Leaving a steak or a chicken uncovered in the refrigerator overnight dries the outside further and gives a markedly better crust. Every drop of surface water has to boil away before browning starts, and during that time the food beneath is overcooking.
# Do not crowd the pan
Food releases steam as it cooks. If pieces are packed tightly, the steam cannot escape and the pan temperature collapses. The food sits in its own liquid and turns grey instead of brown. Leave space between pieces, cook in batches if necessary, and let the pan come back up to heat between them. Mushrooms show this most clearly. Given room in a hot pan they turn deep brown. Crowded, they stew.
# Heat the pan first
A pan that is not hot enough has the same effect. Preheat it until a drop of water skitters and evaporates at once, then add oil, then the food. Choose a fat that tolerates high heat. Once the food is in, leave it alone. Meat that sticks is usually not ready to turn. When a crust has formed, it releases from the pan naturally. Constant prodding and flipping interrupts contact and slows browning.
# Searing does not seal
A nineteenth-century idea, still repeated, holds that searing meat seals in its juices. It does not. Careful tests show that seared meat loses as much moisture as unseared meat, sometimes slightly more. Searing is worth doing for flavour and texture alone. Juiciness is governed by the final internal temperature, which is why a thermometer is more useful than any searing technique, and by resting the meat for a few minutes before cutting.
# Speeding it up
Because the reaction runs faster in alkaline conditions, a small pinch of baking soda accelerates browning. It is a common trick for onions, which otherwise take the better part of an hour to turn deep brown, and it is why pretzels are dipped in an alkaline bath before baking. Use very little, since too much leaves a soapy taste. A little sugar on the surface, as in many marinades, also supplies more fuel for the reaction.
# Too far
Past a certain point, browning becomes burning and flavours turn bitter. Very dark browning of starchy foods such as chips and toast also produces a compound called acrylamide, which food safety agencies advise limiting. Their practical advice is modest: aim for golden yellow, not dark brown, when frying, baking or toasting starchy foods. For the cook, the goal is the same as for flavour. Stop at deep golden, before the edges turn black.

=== life | Cast iron: care without the superstition | In the kitchen
> A cast iron pan can outlive its owner and needs less fuss than its reputation suggests. Most of the rules people repeat about it are out of date.
# What seasoning is
The dark surface on a cast iron pan is not a coating applied at the factory in the usual sense, and it is not a layer of old grease. It is oil that has been heated until it changed chemically, linking into a hard, plastic-like film bonded to the metal. This process is called polymerization. A well-built layer protects the iron from rust and gives a surface that releases food easily, though it is never quite as slippery as a modern non-stick coating.
# How to build it
To season a pan, rub it all over with a very thin film of cooking oil, then wipe off as much as you can, as if you had made a mistake. Bake it upside down in a hot oven, around 230 degrees Celsius or 450 Fahrenheit, for an hour, and let it cool. Repeating this a few times builds a durable base. Thick layers of oil turn sticky and flake. Thin ones cure hard. After that, ordinary cooking with fat adds to it.
# Soap is fine
The best-known rule is never to use soap. It dates from when soap contained lye, which does attack seasoning. Modern washing-up liquid is a mild detergent and will not harm a properly cured surface, because polymerized oil is no longer oil. Wash the pan with a little soap and a brush or sponge. What you should avoid is leaving it to soak or putting it in the dishwasher, both of which promote rust.
# Dry it and oil it
Water is the real enemy. After washing, dry the pan at once with a towel, then set it over a low flame for a minute to drive off the last moisture. While it is still warm, wipe on a few drops of oil and rub until the surface looks dry. This takes under two minutes and is the whole of routine maintenance. A pan treated this way improves with every use.
# How it handles heat
Cast iron is often said to heat evenly. It does not. Iron conducts heat more slowly than aluminium or copper, so a pan placed on a burner develops a hot spot over the flame. What it does well is hold heat, because it is thick and heavy. Once fully hot, it stays hot when cold food is added, which is why it sears so well. The answer is patience: preheat it over moderate heat for five to ten minutes.
# What to cook, and what to wait on
A new pan's seasoning is thin. Start with foods that are forgiving and add fat: bacon, sausages, shallow-fried potatoes, cornbread, seared meat. Hold off on delicate eggs and fish until the surface has built up. Long simmering of acidic foods, such as tomato sauce or wine, can wear thin seasoning and leave a metallic taste. A quick acidic pan sauce does no harm. Once the pan is well seasoned, it tolerates nearly anything.
# Rust and rescue
A rusty or neglected pan is not ruined. Scrub the rust away with steel wool or coarse salt, wash, dry thoroughly and season it again from scratch. Pans found at flea markets in dreadful condition are regularly restored this way, and many cooks prefer older ones for their smoother cooking surface. Short of cracking it or warping it by plunging it hot into cold water, there is very little you can do to cast iron that cannot be undone.
# Is it worth it
Cast iron is cheap, nearly indestructible and moves from hob to oven to campfire. It gives a crust on meat and cornbread that thin pans cannot match. Its drawbacks are weight and slowness to heat. It is not the best tool for everything. For quick sauces or boiling, lighter pans respond faster. As one pan among several, used for searing, roasting and baking, it earns its space for decades.

=== life | Cooking temperatures: the numbers that keep you safe | In the kitchen
> Colour and time are unreliable guides to whether food is safe. A cheap thermometer and four numbers remove the guesswork.
# Why temperature
Harmful bacteria are killed by heat, and the kill depends on reaching a high enough temperature at the centre of the food. You cannot judge that by eye. Chicken can look white while still undercooked, and a burger can turn brown well before it is safe. Cooking times in recipes are estimates that depend on your oven and the thickness of the food. An instant-read digital thermometer costs little and settles the question in seconds.
# The four numbers
The United States Department of Agriculture publishes minimum internal temperatures. Poultry, whole or ground, should reach 165 degrees Fahrenheit, which is 74 Celsius. Ground beef, pork and lamb should reach 160 Fahrenheit, or 71 Celsius. Whole cuts of beef, pork and lamb, such as steaks, chops and roasts, should reach 145 Fahrenheit, or 63 Celsius, and then rest for three minutes. Fish should reach 145 Fahrenheit. Leftovers and casseroles should be reheated to 165.
# Why mince is different
A steak can be eaten pink while a burger should be cooked through, and the reason is where the bacteria are. On a whole cut, contamination sits on the surface, which is exposed to the highest heat. The interior is essentially sterile. Grinding mixes the surface throughout the meat, so the centre of a burger may carry the same bacteria as the outside. That is why the safe temperature for ground meat is higher.
# Using the thermometer
Insert the probe into the thickest part of the food, away from bone, fat and the pan, all of which give false readings. For a whole bird, check the inner thigh and the thickest part of the breast. For thin foods such as burgers, slide the probe in from the side. Wash the probe between checks. Food continues to rise a few degrees after it leaves the heat, which is part of the reason for resting meat.
# The danger zone
Bacteria multiply fastest between about 40 and 140 degrees Fahrenheit, or 4 and 60 Celsius. Food safety agencies call this the danger zone. The rule of thumb is that perishable food should not sit in that range for more than two hours in total, or one hour in hot weather above about 32 Celsius. That includes time on the counter before cooking, on the table after, and in a bag on the way home from the shop.
# Cooling and storing
Refrigerators should be at or below 40 Fahrenheit, 4 Celsius, and freezers at 0 Fahrenheit, minus 18 Celsius. Put leftovers away promptly. Large pots of hot food cool slowly in the middle, so divide them into shallow containers first. Thaw frozen food in the refrigerator, in cold water or in the microwave, not on the counter, where the outside warms into the danger zone long before the centre thaws.
# Keep raw and cooked apart
Many cases of food poisoning come from cross-contamination, not undercooking. Juices from raw meat reach a salad by way of a chopping board, a knife, a plate or a pair of hands. Use a separate board for raw meat, wash hands with soap after handling it, and never put cooked food back on the plate that held it raw. Food safety agencies also advise against rinsing raw chicken, since splashing spreads bacteria around the sink.
# Who needs extra care
Healthy adults usually recover from food poisoning in a few days. Young children, older adults, pregnant women and people with weakened immune systems are at much higher risk of serious illness, and for them the guidelines should be followed strictly. When cooking for a group that includes anyone in those categories, use the thermometer and cook to the published temperatures. Severe or prolonged symptoms, or any sign of dehydration, call for medical advice.

=== life | How to read a nutrition label | Food
> The panel on the back of the packet answers most questions about a food in under a minute, if you know which three lines to read first.
# Start with the serving size
Every number on a nutrition label refers to one serving, and the serving is stated at the top. It is frequently smaller than what people actually eat. A bottle of drink may contain two and a half servings. A packet of crisps that looks like one portion may be listed as three. Before reading anything else, check the serving size and the number of servings in the pack, and multiply if you plan to eat more.
# Calories in context
The calorie figure is the energy in one serving. On American labels, the percentages down the right-hand side are based on a daily intake of 2,000 calories, a round reference figure and not a personal recommendation. Your own needs depend on size, age and activity. The figure is most useful for comparison. Two similar-looking granola bars can differ by a factor of two, and the label is the only way to tell.
# The five and twenty rule
The percent daily value column lets you judge quickly whether a food is high or low in a nutrient. As a rule of thumb used by the Food and Drug Administration, 5 percent or less per serving is low, and 20 percent or more is high. For things most people should limit, such as saturated fat, sodium and added sugars, look for low numbers. For things most people lack, such as fibre, calcium and potassium, look for high ones.
# Added sugars
Current American labels separate added sugars from the sugars naturally present in milk and fruit. Dietary guidelines advise keeping added sugars below 10 percent of daily calories, which is about 50 grams on a 2,000 calorie diet. A single can of a sugary soft drink contains close to 40 grams. Flavoured yoghurts, breakfast cereals, sauces and breads often contain more than their taste suggests, which is why this line is worth a look.
# Sodium
Most of the salt in a typical diet comes from packaged and restaurant food, not from the salt shaker. The general guidance for adults is to stay below about 2,300 milligrams of sodium a day. Soups, cured meats, sauces, bread and ready meals are the usual sources, and a single serving of some can supply half the daily figure. Comparing brands of the same product often reveals large differences, with no difference in taste that most people would notice.
# The ingredients list
Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight. The first three make up most of the product. If sugar or a refined flour appears among them, that is what you are mainly eating. Sugar also travels under many names: corn syrup, dextrose, maltose, cane juice, fruit concentrate, honey. A manufacturer can use several kinds so that none appears first. A short list of recognizable ingredients is generally, though not always, a good sign.
# Claims on the front
The front of the packet is marketing. The back is regulated information. Phrases such as natural, wholesome or made with real fruit have loose meanings or none. Even regulated terms can mislead. A product labelled reduced fat needs only to have less fat than the original version, and may have more sugar. One labelled multigrain may be mostly refined flour. When the front and the back seem to disagree, believe the back.
# A sixty-second routine
For any unfamiliar product: check the serving size, glance at calories, then read three lines, added sugars, sodium and fibre. Scan the first three ingredients. Compare with the product next to it on the shelf. That is enough for nearly every decision. Label formats differ between countries, and some use colour-coded panels on the front, but the same handful of numbers carries most of the information wherever you shop.

=== life | Cleaning chemistry: what works and what must never be mixed | Around the house
> Most household cleaning comes down to four kinds of chemical. Knowing which does what saves money, and knowing what not to combine prevents real harm.
# Detergents lift grease
Water alone cannot remove oily dirt, because oil and water do not mix. Soaps and detergents solve this with molecules that have one end attracted to water and the other to oil. They surround a particle of grease and allow it to be rinsed away. Washing-up liquid is the most versatile cleaner in the house for exactly this reason. Diluted in warm water, it handles most everyday dirt on most surfaces.
# Acids dissolve minerals
The white crust in a kettle, the film on a shower door and the ring in a toilet bowl are mineral deposits left by hard water. They are alkaline and dissolve in acid. Vinegar and citric acid are the mild household options. Commercial descalers and many toilet cleaners use stronger acids. Acids should be kept off marble, limestone and unsealed grout, which are made of the same kind of mineral and will be etched.
# Alkalis break down fats
At the other end of the scale, alkaline cleaners attack fats, oils and burnt-on food by turning them into soap-like substances that wash away. Baking soda is the mildest and doubles as a gentle abrasive. Washing soda is stronger. Oven cleaners and drain openers contain lye, which is powerful enough to burn skin and eyes. With anything stronger than baking soda, wear gloves, and with oven cleaner or drain opener, wear eye protection too.
# Bleach disinfects
Household bleach is a solution of sodium hypochlorite. It kills bacteria, viruses and mould and removes stains by destroying coloured molecules. It is not a cleaner in the sense of lifting dirt, and dirt uses up its strength, so clean a surface first and then disinfect it. Disinfectants also need time to work. The label gives a contact time, often several minutes, during which the surface must stay wet.
# Never mix bleach and ammonia
Bleach combined with ammonia produces chloramine gases, which damage the lungs. Ammonia is found in some glass and multi-surface cleaners, and in urine, which is why cleaning a cat litter tray or a toilet with strong bleach in a closed room can cause trouble. Exposure brings coughing, burning eyes and shortness of breath. If this happens, leave the area for fresh air at once. Serious breathing difficulty is an emergency.
# Never mix bleach and acid
Bleach combined with an acid releases chlorine gas, used as a weapon in the First World War. The acid can be vinegar, a descaler, some toilet bowl cleaners or a rust remover. People do this by accident when one product fails and they pour in another. The safe rule is to never mix bleach with anything except water, and to rinse a surface thoroughly before switching from one product to another.
# Other bad combinations
Two different drain cleaners should never be used one after the other, since one may be a strong acid and the other a strong alkali, and they can react violently. Hydrogen peroxide and vinegar combined in one container form a corrosive acid. In general, mixing cleaning products does not make a stronger cleaner. It makes an unpredictable one. Keep products in their original containers with their labels, out of reach of children.
# A small sensible kit
Four or five products cover a household. Washing-up liquid for general cleaning. An acid such as white vinegar or citric acid for scale. Baking soda for scrubbing and odours. A disinfectant for the places that need one, such as toilets and surfaces that have held raw meat. Add microfibre cloths and ventilation. Most of the specialized bottles in the cleaning aisle are one of these four types with fragrance, colouring and a higher price.
