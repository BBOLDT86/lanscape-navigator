=== money | Compound interest: the curve that bends | Your money
> Growth on top of growth looks like nothing for years and then looks like magic. The arithmetic is simple, and time is the ingredient you cannot buy later.
# Interest on interest
Simple interest pays you on the amount you put in. Compound interest pays you on the amount you put in plus everything it has already earned. Put 10,000 dollars away at 7 percent a year. The first year earns 700. The second year earns 749, because the 700 is now working too. The difference seems trivial. Leave it alone for 30 years and the 10,000 becomes about 76,000. Leave it for 40 and it is about 150,000. The final ten years added as much as the first thirty.
# The rule of 72
To estimate how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the annual rate of return. At 6 percent, money doubles in about 12 years. At 8 percent, about 9. At 3 percent, 24. The rule is an approximation, but a good one across the range of rates you meet in ordinary life. It also makes long horizons easy to picture. Forty years at 7 percent is roughly four doublings: one becomes two, four, eight, sixteen. The rule turns an exponent into a count on your fingers.
# Why starting early beats saving more
Suppose one person invests 500 dollars a month from age 25 to 65 at a 7 percent annual return. They contribute 240,000 dollars and end with roughly 1.3 million. A second person starts at 35 and invests the same 500 a month. They contribute 180,000 and end with about 610,000. The late starter put in three quarters as much money and finished with less than half. The first ten years of deposits, given the longest time to grow, ended up being worth more than the following thirty.
# The curve is flat at the start
The frustrating part of compounding is that it gives little encouragement early on. For the first decade, most of your balance is money you put in yourself. Growth is a small slice. Somewhere in the second or third decade, the growth in a single year begins to exceed what you contribute, and after that the gap widens quickly. Most people who abandon a savings plan do so in the flat part, before the curve has had the chance to show them what it does.
# It works against you too
The same arithmetic runs in reverse on debt. A credit card charging 22 percent a year doubles an unpaid balance in a little over three years. A 5,000 dollar balance paid off at 100 dollars a month at that rate takes more than eleven years and costs about 13,700 dollars in total. Raise the payment to 200 a month and it is gone in under three years for about 6,750. Compounding is neutral. Whether it builds your wealth or someone else's depends on which side of the loan you stand.
# Fees compound as well
A fee of 1 percent a year sounds small. Over 40 years, it is not. Ten thousand dollars growing at 7 percent becomes about 150,000. The same money growing at 6 percent, because a point is lost to fees each year, becomes about 103,000. That single percentage point consumed nearly a third of the final sum. This is the main argument for low-cost funds: you cannot control what markets return, but you can control how much of the return you hand away each year.
# What to do with this
Three habits follow from the arithmetic. Start as early as you can, even with small amounts, because time does more of the work than money does. Leave the money alone, because every withdrawal removes not just the dollars but all the growth they would have produced. And pay attention to rates on both sides: chase down high-interest debt first, and keep the costs on your investments low. None of this requires skill at picking investments. It requires patience, which is rarer.

=== money | Index funds: buying the whole haystack | Your money
> Instead of hunting for the winning stocks, own all of them. It sounds like giving up. Over long periods it has beaten most of the professionals.
# What an index fund is
A stock index is a list of companies chosen by a rule, such as the 500 large American companies in the S&P 500. An index fund simply buys every company on the list in the same proportions and holds them. It makes no attempt to pick winners or avoid losers. The first index fund open to ordinary investors was launched by John Bogle at Vanguard in 1976. Competitors mocked it as a guarantee of mediocrity. It became one of the most influential financial products ever created.
# The arithmetic of average
The case rests on a piece of logic. All investors together own the whole market, so before costs, the average investor must earn the market's return. After costs, the average investor earns the market's return minus what they paid in fees and trading. An index fund charges very little, often a few hundredths of a percent a year. A typical actively managed fund charges far more. So the low-cost index fund must beat the average actively managed dollar, simply by subtraction.
# What the scorecards show
The data matches the logic. S&P publishes a regular scorecard comparing actively managed funds with their benchmark indexes. Over periods of fifteen years or longer, the large majority of American stock funds have trailed the index, typically more than eight in ten. Some managers do win. The trouble is that it is very hard to tell in advance which ones, and funds that led in one period have shown little tendency to lead in the next.
# Most stocks are duds
There is a deeper reason picking is hard. A well-known study by the finance professor Hendrik Bessembinder examined American stocks from 1926 to 2016. More than half of all stocks did worse over their lifetimes than short-term government bills. Just over 4 percent of companies accounted for all of the market's net gain. The market's return comes from a small number of huge winners. Owning everything guarantees you hold them. Picking a handful makes it likely you miss them.
# What it does not protect you from
An index fund is not safe in the sense of never falling. It falls exactly as far as the market does. American stocks lost roughly half their value in the crisis of 2007 to 2009 and index investors lost with them. What the fund removes is the extra risk of having chosen badly: the single company that collapses, the manager who loses their touch. The risk that remains is the risk of the market itself, and the only tools for that are time and a mix that includes safer assets.
# The behaviour gap
The hardest part of index investing is doing nothing. Studies of fund investors repeatedly find that people earn less than the funds they own, because they buy after prices have risen and sell after they have fallen. An index fund cannot stop you from doing that. The investors who actually collect the market return are those who keep buying on a schedule and do not look too often. The strategy is simple to understand and difficult to follow, which is different from being complicated.
# How people use them
A common approach is a small number of broad funds: one covering domestic stocks, one covering international stocks and one covering bonds, in proportions that suit your age and nerves. Target date funds package this mix and adjust it as retirement approaches. The details vary by country and by account type, and they are worth getting right with a qualified adviser if the sums are large. The underlying idea does not change: own broadly, pay little, and let time do the work.

=== money | Risk, volatility and why diversification works | Your money
> Spreading money around is the one move in investing that lowers risk without necessarily lowering return. Here is the mechanism behind the slogan.
# Two kinds of risk
A share of a single company can fall for reasons specific to that company. A product fails, an accounting scandal breaks, a rival wins. It can also fall because the whole market falls, in a recession or a panic. The first kind is called specific risk and the second market risk. The distinction matters because one of them can be removed nearly for free. Hold enough different companies and the individual disasters and windfalls largely cancel out. Market risk remains however many you hold.
# The only free lunch
The economist Harry Markowitz showed in the 1950s that the risk of a portfolio depends not just on how risky each holding is but on how the holdings move in relation to one another. Two assets that do not rise and fall together produce a combination that is steadier than either alone. He later shared a Nobel Prize for the work, and the idea is often summed up in a line attributed to him: diversification is the only free lunch in investing.
# An umbrella and sunscreen
Picture a beach stall that sells only sunscreen. It does well in sunny weeks and badly in wet ones. A stall selling only umbrellas has the opposite problem. A stall that sells both earns a steadier income, though neither product has become any less dependent on the weather. That is diversification in miniature. The benefit comes from the two not moving together. Owning ten companies in the same industry is like stocking ten brands of sunscreen and calling it variety.
# What counts as different
Real diversification means holding things that respond differently to the same events. Companies in different industries. Companies in different countries. Different kinds of asset: shares, bonds, property, cash. High-quality government bonds have often risen when shares fell sharply, which is why the two are so commonly paired, though this has not held in every period. In 2022, for example, both fell together as interest rates rose quickly. Diversification improves the odds. It does not issue guarantees.
# Volatility is not the whole of risk
Finance usually measures risk as volatility, how much a price bounces around. It is a convenient yardstick but an incomplete one. For a person, the real risks are needing money when prices are down, and being frightened into selling at the bottom. A portfolio that swings wildly is only dangerous if you will be forced, by circumstance or by nerves, to sell during a swing. This is why money needed within a few years does not belong in shares, however good the long-run averages look.
# Concentration cuts both ways
Almost every great fortune was built through concentration: one company, one property, one idea. That is what survivorship looks like. For each founder who became rich holding a single stock, many others held a single stock that went to zero, and you do not hear from them. Employees who keep their savings in their employer's shares are doubly exposed, since a bad year can take the job and the savings at once. Concentration is how wealth is made. Diversification is how it is kept.
# Putting it to use
The practical version is undramatic. Hold broad funds instead of a few names. Mix assets according to when you will need the money. Check once or twice a year whether one part has grown to dominate, and if so, trim it back toward your intended mix, a habit called rebalancing. Accept that a diversified portfolio will always contain something that is doing badly. That is not a flaw in the design. It is the evidence that the design is working.

=== money | Inflation: the slow leak in every dollar | Your money
> Prices rise a little each year, and a little each year becomes a lot over a working life. Understanding the leak changes how you think about saving.
# What inflation is
Inflation is a general rise in prices over time, which is the same thing as a fall in what each unit of money can buy. It is measured by tracking the cost of a broad basket of goods and services. If the basket costs 3 percent more than a year ago, inflation is 3 percent. Individual prices move for their own reasons. Televisions get cheaper while tuition gets dearer. Inflation is the average drift of the whole basket, and your own experience can differ from it depending on what you buy.
# How fast money halves
The rule of 72 works here too. Divide 72 by the inflation rate to find how long it takes for money to lose half its buying power. At 2 percent, a rate many central banks aim for, that takes 36 years. At 3 percent, 24 years. At 7 percent, barely 10. A person who retires at 65 on a fixed income and lives to 89 will see their purchasing power roughly halve at 3 percent inflation. Nothing dramatic happens in any one year, which is exactly why it is easy to ignore.
# Nominal and real
Economists separate nominal figures, the plain number of dollars, from real figures, adjusted for inflation. If your savings account pays 2 percent and prices rise 3 percent, your nominal return is plus 2 and your real return is about minus 1. You have more dollars and can buy less with them. The same goes for pay. A 3 percent raise in a year of 4 percent inflation is a real pay cut. Thinking in real terms is the single most useful habit for judging any long-term financial number.
# Who wins and who loses
Inflation moves wealth around. People holding cash or receiving fixed payments lose, because each payment buys less. Borrowers with fixed-rate debts gain, because they repay with money that is worth less than what they borrowed. A fixed-rate mortgage is the classic example: the payment stays the same while wages and prices generally rise around it. Owners of real things, such as property and businesses, tend to be protected over time, since what they own can be repriced as the general price level climbs.
# Why some inflation is intended
Most central banks aim for low, steady inflation, commonly about 2 percent, not zero. Falling prices sound pleasant but can be damaging. If people expect things to be cheaper next year, they delay spending, businesses cut back, and debts become harder to repay because the money owed is growing in value. A small positive rate also gives room for wages and prices to adjust smoothly. High and unpredictable inflation is the damaging kind, because nobody can plan around it.
# When it runs away
History holds extreme cases. In Germany in 1923, prices doubled every few days and workers were paid twice daily so they could spend the money before it lost its value. Zimbabwe in 2008 and Hungary in 1946 saw even faster collapses. These episodes share a pattern: a government unable to pay its bills prints money to cover them, and confidence in the currency evaporates. They are rare, but they explain why central banks guard their independence and why the public's expectations matter so much.
# What it means for you
Three practical points follow. Money you will not need for many years should be invested in something expected to grow faster than prices, since cash guarantees a slow loss. Long-term goals should be stated in today's dollars and then adjusted, because a million dollars in thirty years will not buy what it buys now. And any promise of a fixed payment far in the future, from a pension to an insurance policy, should be judged by whether it rises with prices or stays flat.

=== money | The emergency fund: boring money that buys freedom | Your money
> A few months of expenses in plain cash earns almost nothing and is still one of the best financial decisions most people can make.
# What it is for
An emergency fund is money held in cash for one purpose: to cover you when something goes wrong. A job ends. A car fails. A roof leaks. A medical bill arrives. These events are not rare. Across a decade, nearly everyone meets several. The fund is not an investment and should not be judged as one. Its return is measured in the expensive things you avoid doing: running up a credit card, borrowing at a high rate, or selling long-term investments at whatever price the market happens to offer that week.
# How much
The usual guidance is three to six months of essential expenses. The word essential matters. Count rent or mortgage, food, utilities, insurance, transport and minimum debt payments, not your full current spending. Where you fall in the range depends on how exposed you are. A household with two steady incomes can sit near three months. A single earner, a freelancer with uneven income or someone in a volatile industry should lean toward six or more. If the full target feels unreachable, a first goal of one month still changes a great deal.
# Where to keep it
The fund needs to be safe and quickly reachable, in that order of priority over yield. A savings account at an insured bank is the standard home. Keeping it in a separate account from everyday spending helps, because money you can see in your checking balance tends to get spent. It should not sit in shares, where it might be down by a third at the very moment a recession costs you your job. Those two things tend to happen together, which is the entire problem.
# The cost of not having one
Without a cushion, a small shock becomes an expensive one. A 1,500 dollar repair put on a card at 22 percent and paid off slowly can cost several hundred dollars in interest. Missing a payment adds fees and damages your credit record, which raises the cost of future borrowing. People without savings also make worse decisions under pressure: accepting the first job offered, or keeping a failing car because they cannot fund the alternative. Cash buys time, and time usually buys a better choice.
# Fund or debt first
A common puzzle is whether to build savings or pay down debt. A sensible order many advisers suggest is this. First build a small starter fund, perhaps a month of expenses, so that the next surprise does not go straight onto a card. Then attack high-interest debt aggressively, because nothing safe pays as much as that debt costs. Then return to finish the full fund. Throughout, take any employer matching contribution to a retirement plan, since that is an immediate return you cannot get elsewhere.
# What counts as an emergency
The fund fails if it becomes a general spending account. An emergency is unexpected, necessary and urgent. A holiday is not, however much you need one. Neither is a sale. Annual bills that you know are coming, such as insurance premiums or car registration, are not emergencies either. They are predictable and deserve their own small savings pot. Keeping the definition strict is what ensures the money is still there on the day a real emergency arrives.
# The part nobody mentions
The largest benefit is psychological. Knowing that you could cover several months without income changes how you behave. You can negotiate at work without fear, leave a bad situation, or take a sensible risk. Financial stress narrows attention and worsens judgement, and a cushion relieves it. People who have built one often describe the feeling as lighter, not richer. For money that sits in an account doing almost nothing, that is a considerable return.

=== money | Getting out of debt: avalanche, snowball and the minimum trap | Your money
> There are two well-known orders in which to pay off debts. One is cheaper. The other is easier to stick with. Both beat the default.
# The default is the trap
Credit card statements show a minimum payment, usually a small percentage of the balance. Paying only that keeps the account in good standing and keeps you in debt for a very long time. Because the minimum shrinks as the balance shrinks, most of each payment goes to interest, and the balance barely moves. A balance of a few thousand dollars repaid at the minimum can take well over a decade to clear and cost more in interest than the original purchases. The minimum is designed for the lender's benefit.
# List everything first
Any plan starts with a list. Write down every debt: who is owed, the balance, the interest rate and the minimum payment. People often avoid this step because the total is unpleasant to look at. It is still the step that makes the rest possible. The list usually contains surprises, such as a store card charging more than 25 percent or a small loan that could be cleared in two months. Add up the minimums. Whatever you can pay above that total is your weapon.
# The avalanche
In the avalanche method, you pay the minimum on every debt and put all extra money toward the one with the highest interest rate. When that is gone, you move to the next highest. Mathematically this is the cheapest and fastest route, because each extra dollar is cancelling the most expensive interest available. The drawback is that the highest-rate debt may also be a large one, and you can work for many months without the satisfaction of closing an account.
# The snowball
In the snowball method, you ignore interest rates and attack the smallest balance first. When it is cleared, you roll its payment into the next smallest. It costs somewhat more in interest than the avalanche. Its advantage is momentum. Accounts close quickly, the list gets shorter, and each success frees a larger payment for the next. Research on people repaying real debts has found that closing accounts keeps them motivated, which matters because the cheapest plan is worthless if you abandon it.
# Choosing between them
If your debts have widely different rates, say a card at 24 percent and a car loan at 6, the avalanche saves real money and is worth the patience. If the rates are similar, the two methods cost nearly the same and you may as well take the motivational boost of the snowball. A reasonable blend is to clear one or two tiny balances for the quick win and then switch to highest rate first. The best method is the one you will still be following in eighteen months.
# Lowering the rate
While paying down, look for ways to cut the interest itself. A balance transfer card with a low introductory rate can help if you can clear the balance before the offer ends and you account for the transfer fee. A personal loan at a lower fixed rate can replace several cards with one payment. Calling a lender and asking for a lower rate sometimes works. None of these fix anything unless the spending that built the debt changes too. Otherwise the cleared cards simply fill again.
# Not all debt is equal
A mortgage at a low fixed rate and a credit card at 22 percent are different creatures. The urgent target is high-interest consumer debt, where no ordinary investment can match what the debt costs you. Low-rate, long-term debt can reasonably sit alongside saving and investing. A rough test is to compare the rate on the debt with what you could realistically earn elsewhere. If the debt costs clearly more, paying it off is the best guaranteed return available to you.
