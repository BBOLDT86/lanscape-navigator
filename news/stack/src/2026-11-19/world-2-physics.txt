=== world | Entropy: why time has a direction | Science ideas
> Eggs break and do not unbreak. Coffee cools and never reheats itself. One law of physics explains why the film of the world only runs one way.
# The law
The second law of thermodynamics says that in an isolated system, a quantity called entropy never decreases. Left to themselves, things move from ordered states to disordered ones. Heat flows from hot to cold. Gases spread to fill their containers. Structures decay. Nearly every other law of physics works equally well run forwards or backwards in time. This one does not, and it is the reason the past looks different from the future.
# Where it came from
The idea grew out of practical engineering. In 1824 a young French engineer, Sadi Carnot, asked how much work could be extracted from a steam engine. He showed that an engine needs a hot source and a cold sink, and that some heat must always be discarded to the cold side. The German physicist Rudolf Clausius later turned this into a general law and, in 1865, coined the word entropy for the quantity that keeps rising.
# Counting arrangements
The Austrian physicist Ludwig Boltzmann gave entropy its modern meaning. He realized it is a count of the number of microscopic arrangements that look the same from outside. A shuffled deck of cards can be in any of an astronomically large number of orders, about 8 followed by 67 zeros. Only one of them is perfectly sorted. Shuffle a sorted deck and it becomes disordered, not because disorder is favoured by any force, but because there are so many more ways to be disordered.
# Probability, not prohibition
This makes the second law a statement about overwhelming odds. Nothing forbids the air molecules in your room from all gathering in one corner. It is simply so improbable, given the number of molecules involved, that it would not be expected to happen once in many lifetimes of the universe. A broken cup could in principle reassemble. The number of ways for the pieces to lie scattered is so vastly greater that it never does.
# The limit on every engine
The second law sets a ceiling on the efficiency of any engine that turns heat into work. The ceiling depends only on the temperatures of the hot source and the cold sink, measured from absolute zero. Steam at 550 degrees Celsius with cooling water at 27 degrees allows a theoretical maximum of about 64 percent. Real power stations manage roughly 35 to 45 percent, and petrol car engines turn only around a quarter to a third of their fuel's energy into motion.
# Does life break the law
Living things build intricate order from simple ingredients, which seems to defy the trend. It does not, because a living thing is not an isolated system. An organism maintains its own order by taking in energy and expelling heat and waste, increasing the entropy of its surroundings by more than it decreases its own. The Earth as a whole does the same, absorbing concentrated sunlight and radiating disordered heat to space. Local order is paid for with greater disorder elsewhere.
# The arrow of time
Because entropy rises, we can tell which way time is running. A film of a glass shattering looks right. The same film reversed looks absurd. This raises a deep puzzle. If entropy has been increasing ever since, the universe must have started in a state of remarkably low entropy. Why the early universe was so orderly is not explained by the second law itself and remains one of the open questions of physics.
# Everyday consequences
The law has plain practical meanings. No machine can run forever without an energy input, which is why every perpetual motion design fails. Useful energy is degraded each time it is used, ending as low-temperature heat that can do no more work. Keeping anything ordered, a house, a body, a database, requires a continuing supply of energy and effort. Tidiness is temporary and maintenance is a law of nature, not a personal failing.

=== world | Relativity in five minutes | Science ideas
> Einstein's two theories changed what space, time and gravity mean. The core ideas can be stated without mathematics, and your phone depends on them.
# The puzzle of light
By 1900, experiments had shown something strange. Light always seemed to travel at the same speed, about 300,000 kilometres a second, regardless of how the source or the observer was moving. This contradicts everyday experience. A ball thrown forward from a moving train travels faster than one thrown from the platform. Light refuses to behave that way. In 1905 Albert Einstein, then a patent clerk in Bern, decided to take the result at face value.
# Special relativity
Einstein began with two assumptions. The laws of physics are the same for everyone moving at a steady speed. And the speed of light is the same for all such observers. If both hold, something else has to give, and what gives is time and space. Observers moving relative to each other will disagree about how much time passes between two events and about how long objects are. Neither is wrong. There is no universal clock.
# Moving clocks run slow
The most famous consequence is time dilation. A clock moving relative to you ticks more slowly than one at rest beside you. At ordinary speeds the effect is far too small to notice. Near the speed of light it becomes large. Particles called muons, created high in the atmosphere, should decay long before reaching the ground. They arrive in large numbers, because at their speed time passes more slowly for them. Atomic clocks flown on aircraft have confirmed the effect directly.
# Mass is energy
The same theory produced the best-known equation in science: energy equals mass times the speed of light squared. Mass is a concentrated form of energy, and because the speed of light is so large, a tiny mass corresponds to a vast amount of energy. Converting a single gram of matter entirely to energy would release roughly the yield of the atomic bomb dropped on Nagasaki. Nuclear reactors and the Sun both run on this conversion, turning a small fraction of mass into heat and light.
# General relativity
Special relativity left gravity out. Einstein spent ten more years on it and published the general theory in 1915. Its central claim is that gravity is not a force pulling across empty space. Mass and energy bend space and time, and objects move along the straightest available paths through that curved geometry. The Earth orbits the Sun because the Sun has curved the space around it, much as a heavy ball deforms a stretched rubber sheet.
# The tests
The theory made predictions that could be checked. It accounted for a small unexplained drift in the orbit of Mercury. It predicted that starlight passing near the Sun would be bent by a specific amount, and an expedition led by Arthur Eddington measured the bending during the solar eclipse of 1919, making Einstein world famous. It predicted that clocks run slower in stronger gravity. A century later, in 2015, detectors recorded gravitational waves from two colliding black holes, another of its predictions.
# Your phone knows
Satellite navigation is a daily test of both theories. The satellites move fast, which slows their clocks by about 7 millionths of a second a day. They also sit in weaker gravity than clocks on the ground, which speeds them up by about 45 millionths. The net effect is a gain of about 38 millionths of a second a day. Uncorrected, position errors would grow by roughly ten kilometres daily. The system is built to compensate.
# What it does not cover
Relativity describes the large-scale universe with great accuracy: orbits, black holes, the expansion of space. It does not include quantum mechanics, the theory that governs atoms and particles, and the two have not been successfully combined. Inside black holes and at the first instant of the universe, both should apply, and the equations break down. Finding a theory that unites them is one of the central unsolved problems in physics.

=== world | The double slit: the experiment that breaks intuition | Science ideas
> Send particles one at a time through two openings and they build a pattern only waves can make. Check which opening each used and the pattern vanishes.
# Waves through two gaps
Around 1801, the English scientist Thomas Young passed light through two narrow slits and let it fall on a screen. He did not see two bright bands. He saw a row of many bright and dark stripes. This is what waves do. Where the crests from one slit meet crests from the other, they reinforce. Where crests meet troughs, they cancel. Water ripples passing through two gaps in a barrier produce the same pattern. Young's result persuaded physicists that light is a wave.
# Then light turned out to be lumpy
A century later, the picture grew complicated. Experiments showed that light also arrives in discrete packets of energy, later called photons, and that a detector registers each one as a single click at a single spot. Einstein's explanation of this in 1905 earned him his Nobel Prize. Light seemed to be a wave when travelling and a particle when detected. Then physicists discovered that electrons, which everyone regarded as particles, could produce interference patterns as well.
# One at a time
The crucial version of the experiment sends particles through the slits singly, with long gaps between them, so that there is never more than one in the apparatus. Each one lands on the screen as a single dot. At first the dots appear random. As thousands accumulate, the striped interference pattern emerges. Experiments with single electrons, performed in the 1970s and 1980s, show this build-up clearly. Each particle somehow contributes to a pattern that requires both slits.
# Watching changes it
The obvious question is which slit each particle went through. So experimenters place a detector at the slits to find out. It works: each particle is found at one slit or the other. But the interference pattern disappears, and the screen shows two plain bands, as if the particles were tiny bullets. Remove the detector and the stripes return. Obtaining the path information, by any means that could in principle reveal it, destroys the interference.
# It is not clumsiness
It is tempting to suppose the detector simply knocks the particle off course. The effect runs deeper than that. Experiments have been designed in which the which-path information is recorded very gently, or recorded and then erased before anyone looks. Interference disappears when the information exists and reappears when it is truly erased. What matters is not the disturbance but whether the path could be known. That is a statement about information, and it is very strange.
# What the theory says
Quantum mechanics handles all of this with a mathematical object called the wave function. It spreads out, passes through both slits and interferes with itself. Its size at each point on the screen gives the probability that the particle will be detected there. The theory does not say where a given particle will land, only the odds, and those odds match experiment to extraordinary precision. It predicts the pattern perfectly and declines to describe what the particle is doing in between.
# Not just tiny things
The effect has been demonstrated with objects far larger than electrons. In 1999 a team in Vienna obtained interference with molecules made of sixty carbon atoms, shaped like footballs. Later experiments have used molecules containing around two thousand atoms. There is no agreed size at which quantum behaviour stops. In practice, large warm objects interact with their surroundings so constantly that the delicate interference is washed out almost instantly.
# What physicists make of it
A century on, physicists agree completely on how to calculate and disagree on what it means. Some hold that the wave function is only a tool for prediction. Some hold that every possible outcome occurs in separate branches of reality. Others propose hidden variables guiding each particle. The experiments cannot yet tell these apart. The double slit remains the cleanest demonstration that the world, at its smallest scale, does not work the way everyday objects suggest.

=== world | How GPS knows where you are | How it works
> Your phone finds its position by listening to clocks in orbit. It never sends a signal, and the method depends on timing to billionths of a second.
# The constellation
The Global Positioning System is a fleet of around thirty satellites operated by the United States, orbiting about 20,000 kilometres above the Earth. Each completes two orbits a day. They are spread across six orbital planes so that from almost any point on the ground, at least four are above the horizon at once. Every satellite carries several atomic clocks and continuously broadcasts a radio signal that says, in effect, who it is, where it is and what time it is.
# Distance from time
A receiver works out its distance from each satellite by timing. The signal carries the moment it was sent. The receiver notes the moment it arrived. Radio waves travel at the speed of light, so multiplying the delay by that speed gives the distance. A signal from a satellite directly overhead takes about 67 thousandths of a second to arrive. The measurement has to be extremely fine, since an error of one billionth of a second is an error of 30 centimetres.
# Where the spheres meet
Knowing your distance from one satellite places you somewhere on an imaginary sphere centred on it. A second satellite gives a second sphere, and the two intersect in a circle. A third narrows it to two points, one of which is usually out in space and can be discarded. In principle three satellites fix your position. In practice a fourth is needed, for a reason that has to do with the clock in your phone.
# The fourth satellite
The satellites carry atomic clocks worth a small fortune. Your phone has a cheap quartz clock that may be off by a great deal. Since distance is computed from time, a bad clock would ruin the answer. The solution is elegant. The receiver treats its own clock error as a fourth unknown, alongside its three coordinates, and uses a fourth satellite to solve for all four at once. As a bonus, every GPS receiver becomes a very accurate clock.
# Relativity is built in
The system would not work without Einstein. Clocks on the satellites tick slightly slower because of their speed and slightly faster because gravity is weaker at their altitude. The combined effect is a gain of about 38 millionths of a second per day compared with clocks on the ground. That sounds negligible. Left alone, it would add roughly ten kilometres of position error each day. The satellite clocks are deliberately set to run slow by the right amount before launch.
# It only listens
A common misunderstanding is that GPS tracks you. The satellites do not know your receiver exists. They broadcast in all directions, like a radio station, and receivers only listen. Your location is computed inside your device. If your position is shared with anyone, that is done by an app sending it over the mobile network or the internet, which is a separate matter entirely from the positioning system itself.
# How accurate
In open sky, a phone typically locates itself to within a few metres. Tall buildings degrade this, because signals bounce off walls and arrive late, making the satellite seem further away. Indoors the signals are often too weak to use. Phones compensate by also using nearby mobile masts and wireless networks. Surveyors and farmers use correction services from fixed ground stations to reach accuracies of a few centimetres.
# From military to everyday
GPS was built by the American military, with the first satellite launched in 1978 and the full system operating by 1995. After a Korean airliner strayed into Soviet airspace in 1983 and was shot down, the United States announced the system would be available for civilian use. Until May 2000 the public signal was deliberately degraded. Russia, the European Union and China now operate their own constellations, and modern phones combine signals from all of them.

=== world | How we know the universe had a beginning | How we know
> The claim that everything began about 13.8 billion years ago rests on three independent lines of evidence that all point the same way.
# The idea
The Big Bang theory says that the universe was once extremely hot and dense and has been expanding and cooling ever since. It is not a description of an explosion into empty space. Space itself is stretching, carrying galaxies apart, and there is no centre and no edge to the expansion. Run the film backwards and everything we can observe was once packed into a far smaller, hotter region. The evidence for this picture accumulated across the twentieth century.
# Galaxies are receding
In the 1920s astronomers found that the light from distant galaxies is shifted toward the red end of the spectrum, a sign that they are moving away. In 1929 Edwin Hubble showed that the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it recedes. The Belgian priest and physicist Georges Lemaître had already proposed, two years earlier, that the universe is expanding, and he argued that it must have begun from a compact initial state he called the primeval atom.
# A rival theory
Not everyone accepted this. In the 1940s the British astronomer Fred Hoyle and others proposed that the universe had always existed and always looked much the same, with new matter appearing to fill the gaps as it expanded. It was Hoyle who, on a radio programme in 1949, coined the phrase big bang to describe the idea he opposed. The two theories made different predictions, and for some years there was no observation that could settle the matter.
# The afterglow
If the universe began hot, the radiation from that era should still be around, cooled by expansion to a faint glow of microwaves. Physicists predicted this in 1948. In 1964 two radio astronomers at Bell Laboratories in New Jersey, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, found a persistent hiss in their antenna that came from every direction in the sky. They even cleaned out pigeon droppings in case that was the cause. It was the afterglow, and it won them a Nobel Prize.
# What the afterglow shows
This cosmic microwave background has since been mapped in great detail by satellites. Its temperature is about 2.7 degrees above absolute zero, nearly identical in every direction, with tiny variations of a few parts in a hundred thousand. Those faint ripples are the seeds from which galaxies later grew. The steady-state theory had no natural way to produce such radiation, and after 1965 it lost nearly all of its support.
# The lightest elements
The third line of evidence is chemical. In the first few minutes, the universe was hot enough for nuclear fusion. Calculations predict that this period should have turned about a quarter of the ordinary matter, by mass, into helium, leaving nearly all the rest as hydrogen, with traces of a few other light elements. When astronomers measure the composition of the oldest gas clouds and stars, they find those proportions. Stars alone could not have made so much helium.
# The age
Combining the measured expansion rate with the detailed pattern in the microwave background gives an age for the universe of about 13.8 billion years. The oldest stars that have been dated come out slightly younger than that, as they must. The figure is not a guess. It is derived from several kinds of measurement that agree to within a few percent, though a stubborn small disagreement between methods of measuring the expansion rate is an active research problem.
# What is still unknown
The theory describes how the universe evolved from a hot dense state. It does not say what, if anything, came before, or why the expansion began. At the very earliest moment the known laws of physics break down. The nature of the dark matter and dark energy that dominate the universe's contents is also unknown. The Big Bang is a well-tested account of the history of the universe from a fraction of a second onward, and it is silent about the instant of origin.

=== world | How vaccines train the immune system | How it works
> A vaccine is a rehearsal. It shows the body a harmless preview of a germ so that the real thing meets a prepared defence.
# The immune system remembers
When a germ infects you for the first time, your immune system takes days to mount a full response. Specialized cells must find the ones that recognize the invader and multiply them into an army. During that delay, you are ill. Afterwards, some of those cells persist for years or decades as memory cells. If the same germ returns, the response is fast and strong, often defeating the infection before you notice. This is why many diseases are caught only once.
# The idea of a preview
A vaccine exploits that memory without the illness. It presents the immune system with something that looks like the germ but cannot cause the disease: a weakened version, a killed version, or just a recognizable piece. The body responds as it would to a real infection, producing antibodies and memory cells. Later, when the genuine germ arrives, the defences are already trained. The principle is the same across nearly every vaccine in use.
# Jenner and the cow
Smallpox killed a large share of those it infected and scarred many survivors. In eighteenth-century England it was noticed that milkmaids who had caught cowpox, a mild disease of cattle, seemed to be spared. In 1796 the doctor Edward Jenner took material from a cowpox sore and scratched it into the arm of a boy named James Phipps, and later showed that the boy did not develop smallpox when exposed. The word vaccine comes from vacca, the Latin for cow.
# The main types
Live attenuated vaccines contain a weakened germ that multiplies a little and gives strong, lasting protection. Measles is an example. Inactivated vaccines contain germs that have been killed. Subunit vaccines contain only a part, such as a surface protein. Toxoid vaccines, as for tetanus, contain a neutralized version of the poison a bacterium makes. The mRNA vaccines introduced in 2020 deliver instructions for the body's own cells to make one harmless protein of the virus for a short time.
# Why boosters exist
The first dose of many vaccines produces a modest response. A second dose weeks or months later meets an immune system that already recognizes the target and reacts far more strongly, leaving more memory cells. For some diseases, protection fades over the years and a booster restores it. For others, such as influenza, the virus itself changes its appearance, so the vaccine has to be updated to match the strains expected each season.
# Protecting the group
When enough people in a community are immune, a germ struggles to find its next host and outbreaks die out. This indirectly protects those who cannot be vaccinated, such as newborns and people with weakened immune systems. The share needed depends on how contagious the disease is. Measles spreads so easily that about 95 percent of a population needs immunity to stop it, which is why even small drops in vaccination are followed by outbreaks.
# What they have achieved
Smallpox, which is estimated to have killed hundreds of millions of people in the twentieth century alone, was declared eradicated in 1980 after a global vaccination campaign. The last natural case occurred in 1977. Cases of paralytic polio have fallen by more than 99 percent since a worldwide effort began in 1988. Diseases such as diphtheria and measles, once a routine part of childhood, became rare wherever vaccination was widespread.
# Safety in proportion
Like any medicine, vaccines have side effects. A sore arm, tiredness or a mild fever for a day or two are common and are signs of the immune system responding. Serious reactions are rare and are monitored by health agencies. A 1998 paper claiming a link between a childhood vaccine and autism was found to be flawed and was retracted, and large studies covering millions of children have found no such link. Questions about your own circumstances are best taken to a doctor or pharmacist.
