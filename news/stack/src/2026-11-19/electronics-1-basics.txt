=== electronics | Voltage, current, resistance: the three numbers | The basics
> Every circuit you will ever meet is a story about three quantities. Learn what each one is and the rest of electronics stops being magic.
# Start with a water tank
Picture a tank on a tower with a pipe running down to a tap. The height of the tank sets how hard the water pushes. The width of the pipe sets how easily it flows. The stream coming out of the tap is the result of those two things together. Electricity behaves closely enough like this that engineers still use the picture. Voltage is the push. Resistance is how narrow the pipe is. Current is the flow that results. Nearly every question you will ask about a circuit is one of these three, hiding behind a part number or a symptom.
# Voltage is a difference, not a place
Voltage, measured in volts, is the push between two points. It is always between two points, which is why a multimeter has two probes. A 9 volt battery does not have 9 volts in it. It has 9 volts across it, from one terminal to the other. A bird sits on a power line unharmed because both feet are at the same voltage, so nothing pushes current through the bird. When someone says a point in a circuit is at 5 volts, they mean 5 volts compared with ground, the point everyone agreed to call zero.
# Current is what actually moves
Current, measured in amperes or amps, is how much charge passes a point each second. It is the thing that does the work and the thing that does the damage. An LED glows at about 0.02 amps, written 20 milliamps. A phone charger delivers 1 to 3 amps. A car starter motor pulls several hundred for a few seconds. Current only flows around a complete loop, out of the source, through the parts and back again. Break the loop anywhere and it stops everywhere at once, which is all a switch really does.
# Resistance sets the price of flow
Resistance, measured in ohms, is how hard a material fights current. Copper wire fights very little, so a metre of hookup wire is a small fraction of an ohm. Rubber, glass and dry air fight so hard that almost nothing gets through. In between sit resistors, parts built to have a known value such as 220 or 10,000 ohms. Designers use them like valves, choosing how much current each branch of a circuit is allowed. Resistance turns electrical energy into heat, which is useful in a toaster and a nuisance nearly everywhere else.
# How the three lock together
The three quantities are tied by one rule. For a given resistance, more voltage pushes more current. For a given voltage, more resistance allows less current. Put 9 volts across 450 ohms and 0.02 amps flows. Double the voltage and the current doubles. Double the resistance and the current halves. You never get to pick all three. Choose two and the circuit decides the third for you. That is why a part rated for 5 volts dies on 12: the higher push forces more current through it than it was built to carry.
# Units and the prefixes you will meet
Electronics spans enormous ranges, so the units come with prefixes. Milli means a thousandth, micro a millionth. Kilo means a thousand, mega a million. A sensor might draw 50 microamps while the motor beside it draws 2 amps, forty thousand times more. A pull-up resistor might be 10 kilohms and a current sense resistor 10 milliohms. Getting the prefix wrong is the classic beginner error, and it is always wrong by a factor of a thousand. Say the unit out loud with its prefix every time and the mistake becomes much harder to make.
# What to carry out of this
When a circuit confuses you, ask the three questions in order. Where is the push coming from, and how many volts is it? What path can current take to get back to the source? What on that path limits the flow? A dead circuit usually has no push or a broken path. A hot or smoking one usually has too little resistance on the path. Beginners often chase exotic explanations. People who fix things for a living start with these three, because most faults are one of them wearing a disguise.

=== electronics | Ohm's law in your head | The basics
> One short formula does most of the arithmetic in electronics. Here is how to use it without a calculator and how to know when it stops applying.
# The rule itself
Ohm's law says voltage equals current times resistance. Write it V equals I times R, where I is the old symbol for current. Rearranged, current is voltage divided by resistance, and resistance is voltage divided by current. Cover the one you want and the other two show you what to do. Twelve volts across 6 ohms gives 2 amps. Five volts pushing 0.5 amps means 10 ohms. Georg Ohm published the relationship in 1827 and was mocked for it. Nearly two centuries later it is the first thing anyone checks.
# Think in milliamps and kilohms
Small circuits rarely deal in whole amps. They deal in milliamps, thousandths of an amp, and kilohms, thousands of ohms. Those two units cancel neatly. Volts divided by kilohms gives milliamps directly. Five volts across 10 kilohms is 0.5 milliamps. Three point three volts across 1 kilohm is 3.3 milliamps. Keep the units paired like that and you can do most bench arithmetic in your head. Mix amps with kilohms and you will be off by a factor of a thousand, the most common mistake a beginner makes.
# The classic example: an LED
A red LED wants about 20 milliamps and drops about 2 volts when lit. You have a 5 volt supply. The resistor in series must soak up the other 3 volts while passing 20 milliamps. Three volts divided by 20 milliamps is 0.15 kilohms, or 150 ohms. The nearest common value above that is 220 ohms, which gives a slightly dimmer and longer lived LED. That thirty second calculation is the one you will do most often in hobby electronics, and it is nothing but Ohm's law applied to the leftover voltage.
# Use it to find faults
Ohm's law also works backwards as a detective. If a resistor marked 100 ohms measures 4 volts across it, 40 milliamps must be flowing through it, whether or not the design intended that. Measuring voltage across a known resistance is how technicians find current without cutting a wire. If the number is far from what the design expects, something upstream is wrong. A voltage that should be there and is not, or a current that is much too high, points you toward the fault faster than staring at the board.
# Where the law runs out
Ohm's law describes parts whose resistance stays put, such as resistors and wires. Many parts do not behave that way. A diode passes almost nothing until about 0.6 volts and then conducts freely. A light bulb filament has ten times more resistance hot than cold. A transistor changes its resistance on command, which is the whole point of it. For those parts you still use the law, but on one operating point at a time, with numbers taken from the datasheet, not from one fixed resistance.
# The same law explains wire sizes
Wire has resistance too, small but real. A thin wire carrying a large current loses voltage along its length, and the loss is current times the wire's resistance. Ten amps through a cable with a tenth of an ohm of resistance drops a full volt before the power reaches the load, and the cable warms up doing it. That is why jumper cables are as thick as a thumb, why long runs of low voltage garden lighting dim at the far end, and why electricians choose wire gauge by the current it must carry.
# A habit worth building
Before you connect power to anything, estimate the current. Take the supply voltage, divide by the smallest resistance on the path, and ask whether the answer is reasonable. Five volts into 220 ohms is about 23 milliamps, fine. Five volts into a wire with nothing else on the path is whatever the supply can deliver, which means a short circuit. The estimate takes five seconds and saves parts, batteries and fingertips. People who do this every time rarely see smoke. People who skip it meet smoke early and often.

=== electronics | Power and heat: why parts burn | The basics
> Voltage and current together make power, and power that has nowhere to go becomes heat. This is the sum that decides whether a part survives.
# Power is volts times amps
Power, measured in watts, is how fast energy is being used. In a circuit it is voltage multiplied by current. A phone charger delivering 5 volts at 2 amps supplies 10 watts. A 12 volt car headlamp drawing 5 amps uses 60 watts. Substituting Ohm's law gives two more forms: current squared times resistance, and voltage squared divided by resistance. All three give the same answer. Pick whichever uses the two numbers you already know, and you can find the power in any part of a circuit.
# Every part has a limit
A resistor does not just have a resistance. It has a power rating, the heat it can shed without cooking. The common small through-hole resistor is rated a quarter of a watt. Put 5 volts across a 100 ohm one and it dissipates 25 divided by 100, a quarter of a watt, right at its limit and too hot to touch. Put 12 volts across it and it must shed 1.44 watts. It will brown, smell and fail. The value was right. The size was wrong.
# Why the square matters
Because power rises with the square of current, small increases hurt more than you expect. Double the current through a wire and it makes four times the heat. Triple it and you get nine times. This is why an extension cord that runs a lamp happily gets dangerously warm feeding a space heater, and why power companies send electricity long distances at very high voltage. For the same power, higher voltage means lower current, and lower current means far less energy wasted heating the wires along the way.
# Leave yourself a margin
Good practice is to run parts at no more than about half their rating. If a resistor will dissipate 0.2 watts, use a half watt part. The same thinking applies to capacitors and voltage, to transistors and current, and to power supplies. A part run at its limit works on the bench and fails in a closed box on a hot day, because ratings assume air at room temperature moving freely. Derating, as engineers call it, is the cheapest reliability you can buy.
# Getting the heat out
When a part must handle real power, the design job becomes moving heat away. That is what the finned metal heatsinks on amplifiers and voltage regulators do. They give the heat more surface to leave from. A fan helps further by replacing the warm air. Thermal paste fills the microscopic gaps between a chip and its heatsink, because air is a poor conductor of heat. A regulator that shuts down after ten minutes is usually not broken. It is hot, and it is protecting itself.
# Batteries are a power budget
Power thinking also tells you how long a battery lasts. A battery's capacity is given in amp hours or milliamp hours, the current it can supply multiplied by the time. A 2,000 milliamp hour cell feeding a circuit that draws 100 milliamps lasts roughly 20 hours. Multiply capacity by voltage and you get watt hours, the honest measure of stored energy and the number airlines use to limit what you can carry on. If a gadget dies early, measure what it draws while asleep. A forgotten 5 milliamps runs all day, every day.
# The check to run
For any part that feels warm, work out the power. Measure the voltage across it and the current through it, multiply, and compare with the rating on the datasheet. If you are above half, change something: a larger part, a higher resistance, a lower voltage or a heatsink. Smell is also a tool. Overheated resistors and circuit boards have a sharp, sweet, burnt odour that people who repair electronics learn to recognize from across a room. If you smell it, cut the power first and investigate second.

=== electronics | Series and parallel: two ways to connect anything | The basics
> There are only two basic ways to join parts. Knowing which one you are looking at tells you what the voltage and current must be doing.
# One path or several
Parts are in series when they sit end to end on a single path, so the same current has to pass through each in turn. Parts are in parallel when they connect across the same two points, so each gets the same voltage and the current splits between them. Old strings of holiday lights were wired in series, which is why one dead bulb darkened the lot. The sockets in your house are wired in parallel, which is why switching off a lamp does not switch off the refrigerator.
# Series: resistance adds, voltage divides
In series, resistances add. A 100 ohm and a 220 ohm resistor in series behave as 320 ohms. The same current flows through both, and the supply voltage is shared between them in proportion to their values, with the bigger resistor taking the bigger share. On a 3.2 volt supply, that pair would pass 10 milliamps, with 1 volt across the first and 2.2 across the second. The shares always add up to the supply. That bookkeeping rule is called Kirchhoff's voltage law.
# Parallel: more paths, less resistance
In parallel, adding a resistor always lowers the total resistance, because you have opened another path. Two equal resistors in parallel make half of one. Two 1 kilohm resistors give 500 ohms. For unequal values, multiply the two and divide by their sum. A 1 kilohm and a 3 kilohm pair gives 750 ohms. The total is always smaller than the smallest branch. Each branch draws its own current as if the others were not there, and the supply must provide the sum.
# Batteries follow the same logic
Cells in series add their voltages. Four 1.5 volt AA cells end to end make 6 volts, with the same capacity as one cell. Cells in parallel keep the voltage of one but add their capacities, so the pack lasts longer or delivers more current. Laptop and electric car packs combine both, with groups of parallel cells stacked in series. Mixing old and new cells is a bad idea in either arrangement, because the weaker cell gets driven by the stronger ones, heats up and can leak.
# Why overloaded outlets trip
Every appliance plugged into a household circuit is in parallel with the others. Each one adds its current to the total flowing through the wire in the wall. A kettle and a toaster together can draw more than the wire is rated to carry. The circuit breaker exists to notice that total and open the loop before the wire overheats. So a tripped breaker is not a fault in any one appliance. It is the sum of the parallel branches exceeding what the shared wire can safely handle.
# Capacitors do the opposite
Resistors add in series and shrink in parallel. Capacitors do the reverse. Two capacitors in parallel simply add, because you have made a bigger tank. Two in series give less than either one, using the same multiply and divide by the sum rule that parallel resistors use. That is why you often see several small capacitors side by side on a board: together they act as one larger part, and they respond faster than a single big one would. Knowing which rule belongs to which part saves a lot of confusion later.
# Reading a real circuit
Real circuits mix both arrangements, and the skill is spotting which is which. Follow a wire from one part. If the only place the current can go next is into a second part, those two are in series. If the two parts share both ends, they are in parallel. Collapse each simple group into a single equivalent value, redraw, and repeat. A tangle of a dozen resistors usually reduces to one number in a few steps. This is the first technique taught in every circuits course, and working engineers never stop using it.

=== electronics | The voltage divider: the most reused circuit there is | The basics
> Two resistors and one idea. It sets reference levels, reads sensors and shrinks signals, and it hides inside thousands of larger circuits.
# Two resistors in a row
Connect two resistors in series across a supply and tap the point where they meet. The voltage at that point is a fixed fraction of the supply. With equal resistors it is exactly half. In general, it is the supply voltage times the bottom resistor divided by the sum of both. A 10 kilohm resistor on top and a 20 kilohm on the bottom across 9 volts gives 6 volts at the middle. Nothing else is involved. The ratio of the two parts sets the output, whatever their absolute values.
# Turning resistance into voltage
Many sensors work by changing resistance. A thermistor changes with temperature, a photocell with light, a flex sensor with bending. A microcontroller cannot measure resistance directly. It measures voltage. So you pair the sensor with a fixed resistor to make a divider, and the midpoint voltage now moves as the sensor's resistance moves. The humble divider is the translator between the physical world and the input pin. If you ever wire a light sensor to an Arduino, this is the circuit you are building.
# A volume knob is a divider
A potentiometer is a strip of resistive material with a sliding contact. The two ends go across the signal and the slider picks off a fraction of it. Turning the knob changes the ratio of the resistance above the slider to the resistance below it. That is a voltage divider you adjust by hand. Volume controls, light dimmer inputs, joysticks and the position sensors in older throttle pedals all work this way. One part gives you every ratio from zero to one.
# Stepping signals down safely
Dividers also shrink voltages to levels a chip can accept. A 5 volt sensor output feeding a 3.3 volt input pin can pass through a 10 kilohm and 20 kilohm divider and arrive at 3.3 volts. A 12 volt battery can be measured by a chip that tolerates only 3.3 volts by dividing by four or more first. The chip reads the smaller number and the software multiplies it back up. This is how nearly every battery gauge senses its own pack voltage.
# The catch: loading
A divider only holds its ratio if almost no current is drawn from the midpoint. Whatever you connect there is effectively in parallel with the bottom resistor, which changes the ratio. Feed a motor from a divider and the voltage collapses. The rule of thumb is that the load should have at least ten times the resistance of the divider's resistors. That is why a divider is fine for signals and references, and why you never use one to power anything. Power needs a regulator, which adjusts itself as the load changes.
# Dividers you did not mean to build
Dividers also appear by accident. A corroded connector adds a few ohms in series with a load, and the supply voltage is now shared between the corrosion and the load. The load sees less than it should, and the bad connection gets warm. A weak battery behaves the same way: its internal resistance rises with age, so it measures fine with nothing attached and sags the moment a motor asks for current. Mechanics chase this fault constantly. Measuring the voltage at the load while it is running, not at the battery while idle, exposes it.
# Choosing the values
Small resistors make a stiff divider that tolerates loading but wastes current all day. Large ones save power but become sensitive to loading and electrical noise. For signals into a microcontroller, values between 1 kilohm and 100 kilohms are the usual range, with 10 kilohms the common default. In battery gadgets designers push toward hundreds of kilohms to save every microamp. When you see two resistors in series with a wire leaving from between them on a schematic, you now know what you are looking at.

=== electronics | AC and DC: why the wall and the battery disagree | The basics
> Batteries push one way. The wall socket reverses direction many times a second. The reasons are historical, practical and still shaping every gadget you own.
# Two kinds of flow
Direct current, DC, flows in one direction, like water down a river. Batteries, solar panels and USB ports supply DC. Alternating current, AC, flows back and forth, reversing direction on a regular beat. The power from a wall socket is AC. In North America it completes 60 full cycles a second and in most of the rest of the world it completes 50. Nearly all electronics run on DC inside, which is why almost everything you plug in begins by converting one into the other.
# Why the grid chose AC
In the 1880s, Thomas Edison's DC systems and the AC systems backed by George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla competed to electrify cities. AC won for one practical reason: the transformer. A transformer changes AC voltage up or down with nothing but two coils of wire around an iron core. That let power stations send electricity at high voltage, where little is lost in the wires, and step it down near homes. DC had no equally simple way to change voltage at the time, so DC stations had to sit within a mile or so of their customers.
# What the wall numbers mean
A North American socket is called 120 volts, but the voltage is a smooth wave that swings to about plus 170 volts and back to minus 170. The 120 figure is the RMS value, the steady DC voltage that would deliver the same heating power. Most of Europe, Asia and Africa use about 230 volts RMS. Higher voltage means less current for the same power, which is why a kettle in Britain boils noticeably faster than one in the United States on an ordinary socket.
# Turning AC into DC
A power adapter does the conversion in stages. A transformer or switching stage brings the voltage down. Diodes, which pass current in one direction only, flip the negative half of the wave so that it all points the same way. That step is called rectifying. A large capacitor then fills in the gaps between the bumps, and a regulator trims the result to a steady figure such as 5 volts. Old adapters were heavy because they used iron transformers. Modern ones are small because they switch at high frequency, which needs far less iron.
# DC is making a comeback
The grid is AC, but more and more of what connects to it is DC by nature. Solar panels make DC. Batteries store DC. LEDs, computers and electric car motors' controllers run on DC. Modern power electronics can now change DC voltages efficiently, the thing Edison lacked. That is why very long transmission links increasingly use high voltage DC, and why USB has quietly become a household DC standard, delivering up to 240 watts in its latest version, enough for a laptop or a monitor.
# Frequency and why plugs differ
The number of cycles a second is called frequency and is measured in hertz. Clocks and motors once relied on it for timing, which is one reason an appliance built for one country may misbehave in another even with a plug adapter. Voltage is the bigger hazard. A 120 volt hair dryer on a 230 volt socket draws about four times its rated power for a moment and then fails, sometimes dramatically. Most phone and laptop chargers are marked 100 to 240 volts and work anywhere. Read the small print on the label before you travel.
# What matters at the bench
Three practical points follow. First, polarity matters on DC: connect a part backwards and it may be destroyed, so check the plus and minus marks. Second, mains AC is dangerous in a way small DC supplies are not. Work on the low voltage side of an adapter and leave the wall side closed unless you are trained. Third, when a multimeter reads nonsense, check its dial. Measuring a battery on the AC setting or a socket on the DC setting produces numbers that look almost plausible and mean nothing.
