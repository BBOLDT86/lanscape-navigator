=== mind | The Dunning-Kruger effect: what the study actually found | Classic experiments
> It is quoted to mean that stupid people think they are clever. The original finding was narrower, more interesting and applies to everyone.
# The lemon juice robber
In 1995 a man named McArthur Wheeler robbed two banks in Pittsburgh in daylight without a mask. He was arrested within hours, after security footage was broadcast. He was astonished. He had rubbed lemon juice on his face, believing that since lemon juice works as invisible ink, it would hide him from cameras. The psychologist David Dunning read about the case and wondered whether a lack of ability might also remove the ability to recognize it.
# The experiments
Dunning and his student Justin Kruger tested Cornell undergraduates on logic, grammar and humour, then asked them to estimate how well they had done compared with their peers. The results were published in 1999. Students who scored in the bottom quarter, around the 12th percentile on average, estimated that they were at about the 62nd. Those in the top quarter slightly underestimated their standing. Nearly everyone thought they were at least somewhat above average.
# The double burden
The authors' explanation was that the skills needed to do a task well are often the same skills needed to judge whether it has been done well. Someone with a poor grasp of grammar cannot see their own grammatical errors, because seeing them requires the knowledge they lack. Incompetence, they argued, carries a double burden. People reach wrong conclusions, and their lack of skill also deprives them of the means to realize it.
# What it does not say
The popular version goes much further than the data. The study did not find that the least able were the most confident. The weakest students rated themselves lower than the strongest did. They simply did not rate themselves low enough. The widely shared graph with a peak labelled as a mountain of ignorance, followed by a valley of despair, appears nowhere in the research. It is an internet invention.
# The statistical objection
Some researchers argue that much of the pattern arises from arithmetic. If most people rate themselves as somewhat above average, and their estimates are only loosely related to their real scores, then the lowest scorers must overestimate and the highest must underestimate. Simulations with random data can produce a similar chart. The debate continues. Most agree that people are poor at assessing themselves. How much extra blindness the least skilled have is disputed.
# It is about domains, not people
Dunning has emphasized that the effect is not a description of a kind of person. Everyone is unskilled in most fields, and in each of those fields, everyone is vulnerable. An excellent surgeon may be confidently wrong about investing. A brilliant engineer may badly misjudge their understanding of nutrition. A little knowledge is the dangerous stage, since it supplies enough vocabulary to feel informed without enough depth to see what is missing.
# How to check yourself
Self-assessment improves with outside measurement. Seek feedback from people who know the field and will be frank. Test yourself against an objective standard: an examination, a timed task, a result that can be compared. Try to explain the subject to an expert and notice where you falter. Ask what a specialist would say is the hardest part of the subject. If you cannot name it, you are probably still at the stage of not knowing what you do not know.
# The useful lesson
In the original experiments, training the low scorers in the skill improved both their performance and the accuracy of their self-ratings. Learning more showed them how much they had missed. The practical moral is modesty about areas you have not studied seriously, combined with some caution in assuming that confidence in others signals knowledge. Loudly expressed certainty and actual expertise are only loosely connected.

=== mind | Hindsight bias: I knew it all along | Biases one at a time
> Once you know how something turned out, you cannot recover how uncertain it looked beforehand. That distortion makes us unfair judges of decisions.
# The effect
After an event has occurred, people believe that it was more predictable than it really was. The outcome seems to have been obvious, and they recall having expected it. This is hindsight bias. It appears after elections, football matches, medical diagnoses, business failures and market crashes. Knowing the ending reorganizes memory of what came before, so that the clues pointing to the actual result stand out and those pointing elsewhere fade.
# The experiment
In the early 1970s, the psychologist Baruch Fischhoff asked people to estimate the probability of various possible outcomes of President Nixon's forthcoming visits to China and the Soviet Union. After the trips, he asked the same people to recall what probabilities they had given. For events that had happened, they remembered having rated them as more likely than they actually had. For events that had not, they remembered lower figures. Their memories had moved toward the known result.
# Why it happens
The mind does not store past beliefs as a fixed record. It reconstructs them when asked, using everything it now knows. Once you have learned the outcome, you cannot think your way back to ignorance. You also build a causal story linking earlier events to the result, and a good story makes the result feel inevitable. The alternatives that seemed just as plausible at the time were never worked into a story, and so they are forgotten.
# Judging decisions by outcomes
The most damaging consequence is that we evaluate decisions by how they turned out. A surgeon who recommends a low-risk operation is blamed if the rare complication occurs. An investor who took a foolish gamble and won is praised for vision. Psychologists call this outcome bias. Good decisions sometimes have bad results, and bad decisions sometimes succeed. Judging by results alone punishes sound judgement and rewards luck.
# In courts and inquiries
The problem is serious wherever blame is assigned after the fact. In studies, people told that an accident occurred rate the precautions taken beforehand as less reasonable than people given identical facts without the outcome. Official inquiries into disasters read memos that warned of the danger and ask why they were ignored, forgetting the hundreds of other warnings about things that never happened. The signal is obvious only once you know which one mattered.
# It stops us learning
If everything that happened feels as though it was predictable, there is nothing to learn from being surprised. People who believe they saw the crash coming do not revise the methods that failed to warn them. Overconfidence in forecasting grows, because the record seems better than it was. Real learning requires an honest comparison between what you expected and what occurred, and hindsight quietly corrupts the first half of that comparison.
# The remedy is a written record
The only dependable defence is to write predictions down before the outcome is known. For an important decision, record what you decided, why, what you expected to happen and how confident you were. Date it. When the result is in, read the note before forming a view. People who keep such decision journals are regularly startled by the gap between what they now believe they thought and what they wrote at the time.
# A fairer way to judge
When assessing someone else's decision, including your own past choices, ask what was known at the time, what the reasonable options were and what the chances looked like then. Ask whether you would have chosen differently without knowing the result. Try listing the other ways it could plausibly have gone. This is more effort than condemning or praising by outcome, and it is the only way to tell skill from fortune.

=== mind | What is up to you: the Stoic test | Philosophy for daily life
> A teaching from a Roman slave turned philosopher sorts every worry into two piles. Most distress comes from mistaking one pile for the other.
# Epictetus
Epictetus was born into slavery around the year 50 and later gained his freedom and taught philosophy in Rome and then in Greece. He wrote nothing himself. A student recorded his teaching, and a short handbook drawn from those notes opens with the idea for which he is best known. Some things are within our power, and some are not. Peace of mind, he taught, depends on keeping the two apart and concerning ourselves only with the first.
# The two lists
What is within our power, according to the Stoics, is our own judgements, intentions, desires and actions: how we interpret events and what we choose to do. What is not is everything else. Our bodies, our possessions, our reputations, other people's behaviour, the weather, the past and the outcome of our efforts are all influenced by forces beyond us. We can affect some of these. We do not control them.
# Where suffering comes from
The Stoic claim is that distress arises when we treat things on the second list as though they were on the first. We demand that other people approve of us, that a plan succeed, that we never fall ill. Since none of these can be guaranteed, the demand ensures frustration and anxiety. If instead we attach our sense of success to what we actually control, our own effort and conduct, we can be disappointed but not undone.
# The archer
A favourite Stoic illustration is an archer. The archer can choose the bow, train diligently, aim carefully and release cleanly. Once the arrow has left the string, a gust of wind or a movement of the target may spoil the shot. A Stoic archer aims to shoot well, and regards hitting the target as preferred but not essential. The goal is redefined as doing one's own part excellently. The result is welcomed or accepted, whichever it turns out to be.
# It is not passivity
This is sometimes misread as resignation. The Stoics were not quietists. Marcus Aurelius, who wrote the private notes known as the Meditations, governed the Roman Empire and commanded its armies. The teaching is to act as vigorously as you can on what you can influence while not staking your peace on the outcome. Nor does it mean suppressing feeling. It concerns the judgements that produce needless suffering, not ordinary grief or affection.
# Events and opinions
A related teaching is that people are disturbed less by events than by their opinions about events. Two people stuck in the same delayed train have the same circumstances. One is furious and the other reads a book. The difference lies in what each tells themselves about the delay. The founders of modern cognitive therapy in the twentieth century acknowledged this idea as an ancestor of their approach, which treats distress by examining the thoughts behind it.
# Rehearsing difficulty
The Stoics also recommended imagining setbacks in advance: the loss of a job, an illness, the failure of a venture. The purpose was not gloom. It was to reduce the shock when misfortune arrives, to appreciate what one has while one has it, and to notice that most feared outcomes could be endured. A few minutes spent calmly picturing how you would cope with a feared event often shrinks it to a manageable size.
# Trying it
When something is troubling you, write it down and divide it. On one side, list the parts you control: what you will do, say and attend to. On the other, the parts you do not. Give your energy to the first list, and practise letting the second be what it is. The exercise takes a few minutes and clarifies more than hours of worry. It is very old advice, and it has lasted because it works for many people.

=== mind | How to give feedback people can use | Communication
> Most criticism fails because it is vague, late or aimed at the person. A simple structure makes it specific enough to act on and hard to take as an attack.
# Why it usually fails
Telling someone their presentation was weak, or that they need to be more professional, gives them nothing to work with. They know you are displeased. They do not know what to change. Because the remark is about them and not about anything in particular, it feels like a judgement of character, and people defend their character. The usual result is resentment and no improvement. Vague praise fails in a similar way: pleasant and uninformative.
# Describe the behaviour
Useful feedback names something the person did that an observer could have seen or heard. Not you were disorganized, which is a conclusion, but the report arrived two days after the date we agreed. Not you are careless, but there were four arithmetic errors in the summary table. A description of behaviour can be discussed and corrected. A label can only be accepted or rejected, and it will nearly always be rejected.
# Situation, behaviour, impact
One widely taught structure has three parts. First state the situation: when and where. Then the behaviour: what the person did. Then the impact: the effect it had on you, the team or the work. In Tuesday's client meeting, you interrupted the client twice while she was describing the problem, and she stopped offering details. Each part is a fact or a first-hand observation. The listener may not like it, and they cannot easily dispute it.
# Say it soon
Feedback loses value with every day that passes. The details fade for both of you, and the person may repeat the behaviour in the meantime. Saving up criticism for an annual review is nearly useless, and it arrives as an ambush. The best moment is as soon as you can speak privately and calmly. If you are angry, wait until you are not, since anger turns a description into an accusation, but do not wait a month.
# In private, and ask first
Criticism delivered in front of others adds humiliation, and the person will remember that and nothing else. Praise can be public. Correction should not be. It also helps to ask before beginning: may I give you some thoughts on the meeting? The question takes two seconds, gives the other person a moment to prepare and some sense of control, and makes it far more likely that they will listen instead of bracing for a blow.
# Look forward
Having described what happened, turn to what should happen next. What would you like them to do differently? Better still, ask what they think. People are more committed to solutions they have proposed themselves. A conversation that ends with a specific change agreed on both sides has succeeded. One that ends with the person merely feeling bad has achieved nothing, however accurate the criticism was.
# Praise needs the same precision
Specific positive feedback is as valuable as specific criticism and far rarer. Good job tells someone only that you are pleased. Saying that the way they summarized the three options on one page let the committee decide in ten minutes tells them exactly what to do again. People repeat what they understand to have worked. If you want more of a behaviour, describe it as carefully as you would describe a fault.
# Receiving it
The other half of the skill is taking feedback well. When someone criticizes your work, the instinct is to explain or defend. Resist it. Listen to the end. Ask a question to make sure you understand, and ask for an example if none was given. Thank them, since it took some effort to say. You can decide afterwards how much of it you agree with. People who react defensively soon stop being told anything.

=== mind | Listening: the half of conversation most people skip | Communication
> Most of us spend other people's sentences preparing our own. Real listening is a learnable set of behaviours, and it changes what people are willing to tell you.
# Hearing is not listening
In an ordinary conversation, a great deal of attention goes to things other than the speaker. You are planning your reply, judging what was said, recalling a similar story of your own or waiting for a gap. People speak at perhaps 150 words a minute, and the mind works much faster, so there is spare capacity that readily wanders. The speaker usually senses it. Being half heard is among the commonest complaints in marriages and workplaces.
# Listening to understand
The psychologist Carl Rogers, working in the 1940s and 50s, argued that the most helpful thing one person can do for another in conversation is to try to grasp the other's meaning from their point of view, without judging it. He and a colleague called this active listening. The listener's task is not to fix, advise or evaluate. It is to understand accurately and to show that they have. Simple as that sounds, few people do it.
# Reflecting back
The central technique is to restate what you heard in your own words and check it. So the deadline itself is not the problem, it is that nobody told you it had moved. If you are right, the speaker feels understood and goes further. If you are wrong, they correct you, and you have avoided a misunderstanding. It can feel artificial at first. Done sincerely and not mechanically, it is rarely noticed as a technique at all.
# Ask open questions
Questions that can be answered with yes or no shut a conversation down. Questions beginning with what or how open it up. What happened next? How did that affect you? What do you make of it? Be careful with why, which can sound like a demand for justification. A good question follows from what the person has just said, which also proves that you were paying attention. Questions that steer toward your own view are advice in disguise.
# Leave silence alone
Most people find a pause of three or four seconds uncomfortable and rush to fill it. Experienced interviewers, counsellors and negotiators do the opposite. When the speaker stops, they wait. Very often the speaker continues, and what comes after the pause is more candid than what came before. Silence signals that you are not in a hurry and that there is room for more. Learning to tolerate it is one of the simplest improvements a listener can make.
# Hold the advice
When someone describes a problem, the urge to offer a solution is strong. Frequently they have not asked for one. They may have thought of your suggestion already. They may want to think aloud, or simply to be heard. Jumping to advice can imply that the problem is easy and they should have solved it. If you are unsure what is wanted, ask: would it help to talk through options, or do you mainly want me to listen?
# Remove the distractions
Attention is visible. Turning to face someone, looking at them and putting away the phone all communicate that they matter. A phone lying on the table between two people, even untouched, is a reminder that the conversation can be interrupted at any moment. If you truly cannot give someone your attention now, it is better to say so and name a time when you can than to offer a distracted half of it.
# What it gets you
People tell good listeners more. They bring bad news earlier, admit mistakes sooner and explain what they actually want. In disputes, being accurately heard often lowers the temperature more than any concession does, since much anger is the frustration of not being understood. Try it once this week. In one conversation, do nothing but ask, reflect and wait. Notice how much longer the other person talks and how much you learn.

=== mind | Writing clearly: say the thing | Writing and thinking
> Unclear writing is usually unclear thinking with decoration. A handful of habits make almost anyone easier to read.
# Know what you want to say
Most muddled writing comes from starting before the writer knows the point. A useful test is to state your message aloud in two sentences to someone who knows nothing about it. If you cannot, the difficulty is not with the prose, and no amount of polishing will cure it. Work out the point first. Much of the labour of writing is discovering what you think, and a first draft is often the place where that happens.
# Put the point first
Readers are busy and many stop early. State the conclusion at the beginning, then support it. An email should give its request or its news in the first line. A report should open with what was found and what is recommended. School essays teach people to build toward a conclusion. For nearly every practical purpose the reverse order is better. A reader who stops after one paragraph should still leave with the main message.
# Short words, short sentences
Use the plain word when it will serve. Use, not utilize. Start, not commence. Help, not facilitate. Long words chosen to impress make a reader work harder and trust the writer less. Keep most sentences to one idea. If a sentence runs beyond two lines, look for the place where it can be split. Vary the lengths so the rhythm does not become monotonous, but let the short, direct sentence be your default.
# Prefer the active voice
In an active sentence, the subject acts: the committee rejected the proposal. In a passive one, the subject is acted upon: the proposal was rejected. The passive is longer and often hides who did what, which is why it is so common in official apologies. It has legitimate uses, when the actor is unknown or unimportant. As a habit, name who did the thing and what they did. Your sentences will be shorter and clearer.
# Cut
Nearly every draft is improved by removing a tenth of it. Look for words that add nothing: very, really, quite, in order to, the fact that, it should be noted that. Look for sentences that repeat the previous one in different words. Look for an opening paragraph that merely clears the throat. George Orwell, in a well-known essay on English prose, advised cutting any word that can be cut. Readers never complain that something was too easy to read.
# The curse of knowledge
Once you understand something, it is hard to remember not understanding it. In a much-cited experiment, people tapped out the rhythm of a familiar song and predicted that listeners would name it about half the time. Listeners managed it on roughly one attempt in forty. The tappers could hear the melody in their heads. Writers are in the same position. Terms and steps that seem obvious to you may be opaque to a reader.
# Be concrete
Abstract statements slide past the reader. Examples stay. Sales rose sharply is weaker than sales rose from 200 to 340 units a month. The policy caused problems is weaker than the policy left three branches without a manager for six weeks. Whenever you make a general claim, follow it with a particular case, a number or a name. If you cannot think of one, consider whether the general claim is true.
# Read it aloud
The best single editing technique is to read the draft aloud, or have software read it to you. The ear catches what the eye forgives: the sentence that runs out of breath, the word repeated three times, the phrase that sounds pompous, the step missing from an argument. Wherever you stumble, a reader will too. Leave a draft overnight if you can. In the morning you will read it more nearly as a stranger would.
