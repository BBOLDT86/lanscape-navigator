=== poker | The check-raise | After the flop
> Checking and then raising when your opponent bets is the out-of-position player's main weapon. It builds pots, punishes automatic bets and wins hands outright.
# What it is
A check-raise is exactly what the name says. You check, your opponent bets, and you raise. It was once considered so underhanded that some card rooms banned it. Today it is standard and essential. The player who acts first is at a disadvantage on every street. The check-raise is the tool that offsets it, because it allows the first player to trap bets that the second player makes simply because they were checked to.
# Why not just bet
When you are out of position against the player who raised before the flop, that player will bet a large share of flops whatever they hold. If you bet first with a strong hand, they fold their misses and you win a small pot. If you check, they bet their misses, and now your raise captures that extra money. Checking lets them put chips in with hands that would never have called a bet. The check-raise wins bigger pots with the same cards.
# For value
The foundation is strong hands: two pair, sets, and top pair with a good kicker on boards where opponents will continue with worse. On wet boards, raising also charges the many draws that would otherwise see the next card cheaply. Size the raise to about three times your opponent's bet, somewhat more if you will be out of position for the rest of the hand and the board is full of draws. A tiny raise gives every draw the right price.
# As a semi-bluff
If you only check-raise strong hands, attentive opponents will fold whenever you do it. So add draws. A flush draw, an open-ended straight draw, or a gutshot with two overcards all make good candidates. These hands can win immediately when the opponent folds, and they can still win by improving when called. A draw played this way earns far more than a draw played by calling and hoping, because it has two ways to succeed.
# Choosing the board
The play works best on flops that favour the caller's range: low and middling connected boards such as seven, six, four or eight, five, three. The preflop raiser's big cards have mostly missed, and you can credibly hold two pair, sets and straights. On boards with an ace or king, the raiser's range is strong and yours is not, so check-raising as a bluff there is expensive. Match the play to boards where the strong hands are plausibly yours.
# Against the right player
Check-raising needs an opponent who bets when checked to. Against someone who makes a continuation bet on nearly every flop, it is very profitable, as their betting range is full of air. Against a passive player who only bets strong hands, checking a big hand risks giving a free card, so simply bet. And if a tight, passive player check-raises you, believe them. At low stakes, that line is a strong hand far more often than not.
# After you raise
Have a plan for the turn. If called, your opponent usually has a pair or a draw. With a strong made hand, keep betting, sized to the wetness of the board. With a semi-bluff, continue on cards that improve your hand or that are bad for your opponent's likely holdings, and be willing to give up on blanks. The mistake to avoid is automatic follow-through. A second and third bet with nothing must have a reason behind it.
# Defending against it
When you are the one check-raised, the answer depends on who did it and on what board. Against aggressive players on draw-heavy boards, continue with your good pairs and your own draws, since they are bluffing some of the time. Against passive players, fold all but your strongest hands. You can also reduce your exposure in advance by checking back some flops, which removes the weak bets that a check-raise feeds on.

=== poker | Bluffing: how often, with what, and against whom | After the flop
> A bluff is a bet with the worst hand that makes a better one fold. Good bluffs are chosen by arithmetic and by the cards you hold, not by nerve.
# Why bluff at all
If you only bet with strong hands, observant opponents stop paying you. They fold when you bet and take the pot when you check. Bluffing is what forces them to call your value bets. In theory, the right mix of bluffs and value hands leaves your opponent with no profitable response. In practice, at most tables, bluffing is a tool to use selectively against players who fold too much and to put away against players who do not.
# The ratio on the river
On the last street there is a clean relationship between bet size and how many bluffs you can include. When you bet the size of the pot, your opponent is getting 2 to 1 and needs to win a third of the time. So a balanced betting range has one bluff for every two value hands. With a bet of half the pot, the opponent gets 3 to 1, and the mix is one bluff for every three value hands. Bigger bets permit more bluffs.
# Pick hands that cannot win otherwise
The best hands to bluff with are those with no chance at showdown. A missed draw that has become nine high cannot win by checking, so it loses nothing by bluffing. A hand such as third pair might win if checked, so turning it into a bluff wastes that value. This is the first filter. When choosing which of your weak hands to bet, start with the ones that are worthless if you check.
# Blockers
The second filter is which cards you hold. If the board shows three spades and you hold the ace of spades with no flush, your opponent cannot have the best possible flush. That makes strong hands less likely in their range and folds more likely. A card that removes combinations of the hands you fear is a blocker. Good river bluffs block the hands that would call. Equally, avoid holding cards that block the missed draws your opponent would fold.
# Tell a consistent story
A bluff must represent a specific strong hand, and your actions on earlier streets must fit it. If you called the flop and turn on a board with a flush draw and then bet big when the third suited card arrives, the story makes sense. If you checked twice and then bet big on a harmless river card, it does not. Before bluffing, ask what hand you are claiming to hold and whether you would have played that hand this way.
# Bluff on early streets with equity
On the flop and turn, the best bluffs are semi-bluffs: draws and hands with overcards that can improve. They win when the opponent folds and still have a chance when called. Pure bluffs with no possibility of improving are better saved for the river, when all the cards are out. A sensible pattern is to bet many draws on the flop, continue with the better ones on the turn, and on the river bluff with those that missed.
# Count the opponents
Each additional player in the pot makes a bluff less likely to work, since every one of them has to fold. A bluff that succeeds 50 percent of the time against one opponent might succeed 25 percent of the time against two with similar tendencies. Bluff mainly in pots with a single opponent. Against several, bet your real hands and your strong draws and let the rest go.
# Most games are under-bluffed
All of this theory assumes an opponent who can fold. Many recreational players call far too often, driven by curiosity and by the fear of being bluffed. Against them, the right number of bluffs is close to zero, and the right response is to bet your good hands larger. The reverse also holds. At lower stakes, big bets and raises on the river are strong hands far more often than theory would predict. When a quiet player suddenly bets big, folding is usually right.

=== poker | Think in ranges, not hands | After the flop
> Trying to guess the exact two cards an opponent holds is a losing habit. Strong players assign a whole set of possible hands and narrow it street by street.
# The mistake of putting someone on a hand
Beginners decide that an opponent has ace king and then play the rest of the hand as if that were known. Sometimes they are right, which reinforces the habit. More often the opponent turns over something else and the beginner is baffled. Nobody can deduce two exact cards from a few bets. What can be deduced is the collection of hands consistent with what the opponent has done. That collection is called a range.
# Start before the flop
A range begins with the first action. A tight player raising from early position might hold big pairs, ace king, ace queen and a few suited big cards, perhaps one hand in eight. A loose player raising on the button might hold four hands in ten. A player who just calls a raise rarely has aces or kings, since most players reraise those. Position, the action and what you know of the player set the starting list.
# Narrow with each action
Every later decision removes hands from the list. Suppose the flop is king, eight, three and the raiser bets. That keeps most of the range, since many players bet any two cards here. The turn is a two and they bet again, larger. Now the weakest hands drop out. On the river they bet a third time. By this point, a typical player's range is mostly a king with a good kicker or better, plus an occasional busted bluff.
# Weigh, do not eliminate
Ranges are not just in or out. Some hands are more likely than others within the range. A player might bet three streets with ace king every time and with a bluff one time in five. Good hand readers weight the possibilities instead of crossing them off. They also keep unlikely hands in mind for players who have shown they are capable of odd plays. The picture is a set of probabilities that shifts with each bet.
# Your own range
The idea applies to you as well. Your opponents, if they are thinking, are assigning you a range. So ask what your line looks like from across the table. If you would only play a hand this way with very strong holdings, your bets will get too many folds, or your checks will invite bluffs. Thinking about your whole range in a spot, not just the hand you happen to hold, is how good players stay hard to read.
# Capped and uncapped
A range is capped when it cannot contain the strongest hands. If a player just calls before the flop and then calls bets on a board of ace, king, queen, they are unlikely to hold a set or two pair of the top cards, having not raised at any point. Their range has a ceiling. A capped range is vulnerable to large bets, because its owner can never be confident. Recognizing when an opponent is capped is a signal to apply pressure.
# Compare equity against the range
Once you have a range in mind, the decision is arithmetic. How does your hand do against all of it? If the pot is offering 3 to 1 and your hand beats a third of the hands in their range, call. This is far more reliable than fearing the single worst possibility or hoping for the best one. Software lets you practise this away from the table by entering a range and seeing your equity against it.
# Building the habit
Start small. On every hand you watch, even those you have folded, guess the raiser's range before the flop and revise it as the action unfolds. When cards are shown, check whether the hand was inside your range. If it was not, ask what you missed. Over a few months, this turns hand reading from a mysterious talent into a routine, and it works equally well against players you have never seen before.

=== poker | The river: the last decision | After the flop
> No more cards are coming. Every hand is now exactly one of three things, and knowing which one yours is settles most river decisions.
# What changes on the river
On earlier streets, hands have potential. Draws can arrive and leads can change. On the river all of that is finished. Your hand's value is fixed, and it will either win at showdown or it will not. There is nothing left to protect against and no equity to deny. The only purposes of a river bet are to get called by a worse hand or to make a better hand fold. A bet that does neither is a mistake.
# Three categories
Sort your hand into one of three groups. A value hand is strong enough that worse hands will call a bet. A bluff is a hand that cannot win at showdown and can only take the pot by betting. In between are hands with showdown value: good enough to win sometimes when checked, not good enough to be called by worse. These medium hands should usually check. Betting them drives out everything they beat.
# In position
When you act last and are checked to, the decision is simple. With a value hand, bet an amount that worse hands will pay. With a hand that has showdown value, check behind and see who wins. With nothing, decide whether your opponent will fold often enough to justify a bluff, given the size you would need. Acting last means you never face a raise after checking, which is one more reason position is worth so much.
# Out of position
First to act is harder. If you check a strong hand hoping to raise, the opponent may check behind and you win nothing extra. If you bet a medium hand, you may be raised off it. A common approach is to bet your strong hands and your chosen bluffs, and to check your medium hands planning to call some bets and fold to others. Against aggressive opponents, checking a few strong hands as well keeps them from attacking every check.
# Facing a bet
When your opponent bets the river, work out the price. A bet of half the pot means you need to hold the best hand 25 percent of the time. A pot-sized bet means 33 percent. Then estimate, from the way the hand was played, how often this player is bluffing or betting a worse hand for value. If that is more often than the price requires, call. If less, fold. The strength of your own hand matters only relative to their range.
# Bluff catchers
A bluff catcher is a hand that beats every bluff and loses to every value bet. Top pair on a board where a flush and a straight have both arrived is a typical example. With such a hand, raising is pointless, and the choice is call or fold. The useful question is whether there are enough missed draws in the opponent's range. If every obvious draw completed on the river, they have few natural bluffs and you should fold more.
# Raises mean strength
A river raise is the strongest action in poker. In most games, particularly at low and middle stakes, players almost never raise the river as a bluff. They raise with the nuts or close to it. Folding a good hand to a river raise feels terrible and is usually correct. Over time, the players who pay off river raises with one pair or two pair, just to see it, give back a large part of what they win elsewhere.
# Take your time
The river is where pots are biggest and mistakes cost the most. There is no penalty for pausing. Replay the hand from the start: what the opponent did before the flop, how they reacted to each card, what sizes they used. Ask which hands play this way. Then decide. Many expensive errors on the river are made in two seconds by players who would have got it right with twenty.

=== poker | Tilt: the leak that is not about cards | The long game
> Every player knows how to play better than they do when upset. Managing that gap is worth more than most strategy study.
# What tilt is
Tilt is the deterioration of your play caused by emotion. The term comes from pinball, where shaking the machine in frustration causes it to lock up. In poker it usually means anger after a bad beat leading to reckless aggression, but it takes many forms. A player on tilt knows the right play and makes the wrong one. Nearly all experienced players agree that it costs them more than any gap in their technical knowledge.
# The kinds
Tilt is not only fury. There is the urge to win back losses quickly by playing bigger pots with weaker hands. There is entitlement, the feeling that after so many lost coin flips you are owed a win. There is fear, playing too tightly after a big loss to avoid another. There is boredom, entering pots because you have folded for an hour. And there is overconfidence after a hot streak. Each one pushes your play away from what you know is correct.
# Why it happens
The brain's response to a painful loss is the same system that handles physical threat. Heart rate rises, attention narrows, and the urge to act immediately becomes strong. Careful reasoning is exactly what that state suppresses. Poker also delivers punishment for correct play on a regular basis, which is unusual. Getting your money in as a four to one favourite and losing happens one time in five, and each time it feels like an injustice.
# Know your triggers
Players tilt in their own characteristic ways. For one it is a particular opponent who talks too much. For another it is being bluffed and shown the cards. For another, losing three hands in a row, or being card-dead for an hour, or making one mistake that they then cannot stop replaying. Write yours down. A trigger you have named in advance is much easier to notice in the moment than one that takes you by surprise.
# Catch it early
Tilt is far easier to stop at the first sign than at full strength. Early signs are physical and behavioural: a hot face, a tight jaw, faster breathing, acting quickly, saying something sharp, thinking about a previous hand during the current one. When you notice one, take the simplest step available. Sit out a round. Stand up. Take ten slow breaths. The aim is to interrupt the reaction before it reaches your decisions.
# Set limits before you play
Rules made in advance work because they do not depend on judgement in the moment. A stop-loss is a commitment to quit after losing a set number of buy-ins, commonly two or three. A time limit serves the same purpose, since tired players tilt more easily. Some players also set a rule to quit after any hand that makes them visibly angry. The right moment to leave is always earlier than it feels.
# Quitting is a skill
The session ends when you say it does, and choosing that moment well is as much a part of the game as any bet. Leaving while behind feels like accepting a loss. But the loss has already occurred, and the only question is whether you play your best from here. If the answer is no, further hands cost money on average. There will always be another game tomorrow, and you will arrive at it with your bankroll and judgement intact.
# Outside the game
What you bring to the table affects how you play. Sleep, food, alcohol, stress from work or home, and playing with money you cannot afford to lose all make tilt more likely. Players who treat poker seriously look after the ordinary things for that reason. If you find yourself chasing losses repeatedly, or playing to escape other problems, that goes beyond tilt. Gambling support services exist for exactly that, and using one is a sound decision.

=== poker | Table selection: where you sit matters more than how you play | The long game
> Your profit comes from the gap between your skill and your opponents'. Choosing whom you play against is the easiest way to widen it.
# The skill gap is everything
Poker has no house edge between players. Money moves from those who make more mistakes to those who make fewer, minus the fee the room takes. The tenth best player in the world loses money at a table with the top nine. An average player wins steadily at a table of beginners. Improving your own game is slow. Choosing a softer game is immediate, and yet most players sit in the first open seat and never look around.
# Signs of a good table
A profitable table has recognizable features. Many players see each flop. Players call the big blind instead of raising. Pots are contested by four or five people. Hands are shown down that should have been folded long before. The mood is lively, with conversation, laughter and drinks. A quiet table of silent players wearing headphones, with most pots won by a single raise before the flop, is a table of regulars and should be avoided.
# What to count
You can estimate a table's quality in a few minutes of watching. Count how many players see the flop on average. Three or more suggests a loose, profitable game. Note the size of the average pot relative to the blinds. Watch for anyone who plays almost every hand, since one such player can make a table worth staying at. Online, lobbies often display the percentage of players seeing the flop and the average pot size for each table.
# Seat selection
Within a table, some seats are better than others. Because betting moves clockwise, you act after the player on your right in most hands. You want the loose, free-spending players on your right, so that you see what they do before you decide and can isolate them with raises. You want tight, predictable players on your left, since they will seldom interfere. An aggressive, skilled player directly on your left is the worst neighbour, as they will act after you all night.
# Ask to move
In most card rooms you can request a table change or a seat change, and nobody will mind. Tell the floor staff you would like to move and they will put you on a list. If a seat opens at your own table that is better placed, you can usually take it by telling the dealer. Few players bother. Those who do are making a decision worth more per hour than nearly any refinement of strategy.
# Games change
A table is not fixed in quality. The loose player who made it good may go broke or go home. Two strong regulars may sit down. Check every half hour or so whether the reasons you chose the game still apply. If the table has turned tough, leave, even if you are winning and comfortable. Loyalty to a seat is not rewarded. Good players are restless in this one respect.
# Stakes and times
Game quality varies with the clock and the stake. Evenings and weekends bring recreational players. Weekday mornings are dominated by regulars. Holiday periods and the hours around major sporting events are often soft. Moving up in stakes usually means tougher opposition, though not always, and a lively game one level below your usual one can easily be more profitable than a tight game at your normal stake.
# Ego is the obstacle
The main reason players ignore all this is pride. Seeking out weak opponents feels less admirable than beating strong ones. But poker pays for results, not for the difficulty of the opposition. Playing against better players has value as a deliberate lesson, paid for knowingly. As a routine, it is a slow way to lose. The most consistently profitable players are rarely the most gifted. They are the ones who are most careful about where they sit.
