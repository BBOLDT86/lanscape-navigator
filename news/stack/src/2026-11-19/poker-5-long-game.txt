=== poker | Live tells: what bodies give away, and what they do not | The long game
> Physical tells are real but far less reliable than films suggest. Betting patterns tell you more, and the best use of tell-reading is to break close decisions.
# Keep them in proportion
The image of poker as a contest of stares is misleading. The most trustworthy information about an opponent's hand is how they have bet: what they did before the flop, how they sized each bet and how that compares with their usual behaviour. Physical tells are a supplement. Good players use them to tip a decision that is otherwise close, not to overrule the logic of the hand. A player who relies mainly on reading faces will lose to one who reads betting.
# Establish a baseline
A behaviour means something only as a departure from what is normal for that person. Some players always talk. Some always sit rigidly still. Some always shuffle their chips. None of that is information until it changes. Spend your first half hour at a table watching how each opponent acts in hands where the cards are eventually shown. Once you know how someone looks and sounds when relaxed, you can notice when they differ.
# Weak means strong
A long-standing observation, set out most famously by the writer Mike Caro, is that recreational players act the opposite of what they hold. With a big hand, they sigh, shrug, hesitate and bet as if reluctantly. With a bluff, they bet forcefully, stare the opponent down and try to look confident. The acting is instinctive and often unconscious. Against inexperienced opponents, a display of strength is slightly more likely to mean weakness, and the reverse.
# Hands and breathing
Involuntary signs are harder to fake. Trembling hands when placing a large bet usually indicate a very strong hand, not a bluff. It is the release of tension from someone who expects to win a big pot. Bluffers tend to become still, as if trying not to be noticed, and sometimes hold their breath. A player who was chatting and suddenly falls silent after betting, or who freezes when you look at them, is more often bluffing.
# Timing
How long someone takes can be as revealing as how they look. An instant call on the flop usually means a draw or a medium hand, since a very strong hand would at least consider raising and a weak one folding. A long pause followed by a bet often means strength dressed up as uncertainty. An instant bet on a card that changes the board often means the player had already decided to bet, which leans toward a bluff. Timing tells also work online.
# Glances and chips
Watch opponents as the flop is dealt, not the cards themselves, which will still be there a second later. A player who looks at the flop and then immediately glances at their own chips often liked what they saw and is thinking about betting. A player who stares at the board for a long time has frequently missed. Someone who reaches for chips before you have acted, as if ready to call, is commonly trying to discourage your bet.
# Reverse tells
Experienced opponents know all of the above and may act it out deliberately to mislead you. The more skilled the player, the less weight their mannerisms deserve. Against strong opposition, physical reads are close to worthless, and you should return to ranges and bet sizes. The classic tells are most dependable against recreational players who are not thinking about how they appear, which fortunately describes the players from whom most profit comes.
# Hiding your own
The simplest defence is uniformity. Take about the same time over every significant decision. Place your chips the same way each time. Look at the same spot on the table after you bet. Say nothing during a hand. You do not need a blank face, sunglasses or a hood. You need a routine that is identical whether you hold the nuts or nothing, so that there is no difference for anyone to observe.

=== poker | Tournaments and cash games are different sports | Tournaments
> The cards and the rules are identical. The incentives are not, and a strategy that wins in one format can lose steadily in the other.
# What your chips are
In a cash game, chips are money. A 5 dollar chip is worth 5 dollars, and you may leave with your stack whenever you wish. In a tournament, you pay an entry fee and receive chips that have no cash value. You play until you lose them all or win everyone else's. Prizes are paid according to the order in which players are eliminated. This one difference changes the correct play in a great many situations.
# The blinds rise
In a cash game, the blinds never change, and stacks are typically deep, a hundred big blinds or more. In a tournament, the blinds increase at set intervals. A stack that was comfortable an hour ago becomes short without your losing a hand. Much tournament play therefore happens with 20 to 40 big blinds, and a good deal with fewer than 15. Deep-stacked skills matter early. Short-stacked skills decide who wins.
# Survival has value
In a cash game, if you lose your stack you can buy another, so only the expected value in chips matters. In a standard tournament, once your chips are gone, you are out. Being alive has a value of its own, because it keeps open the chance of reaching the prizes. This makes some gambles that would be correct in a cash game incorrect in a tournament, particularly near the point where the prizes begin.
# The prize structure
A typical tournament pays about the top 10 to 15 percent of the field. Most of the prize money is concentrated in the last few places, with first often receiving a fifth or more of the pool. Scraping into the lowest prizes returns little more than the entry fee. The real money is at the final table. Good tournament players do not aim merely to survive into the prizes. They accumulate chips so that they can contend for the top spots.
# Variance
The swings in tournaments are far larger than in cash games. Even a very good tournament player reaches the prizes in only a minority of events, perhaps one in six or seven, and wins outright very rarely. Long runs without a meaningful result are normal. This is why the bankroll recommended for tournaments, counted in buy-ins, is several times that for cash games, and why results over a few dozen events tell you almost nothing about your skill.
# Stack size in big blinds
Tournament players think of their stack as a number of big blinds, not as a count of chips. With more than 40, you can play a full range of moves. Between 20 and 40, calling raises with speculative hands becomes costly, and reraising all in becomes a major weapon. Below about 12, the game simplifies to moving all in or folding. Recognizing which zone you and your opponents are in is the foundation of tournament strategy.
# Antes
Most tournaments add an ante after the early levels: a small forced payment from every player, or one larger payment from the big blind, on each hand. Antes make the starting pot bigger, which makes stealing it more rewarding and defending it more justified. Correct play becomes noticeably looser and more aggressive once they begin. Players who keep to the tight style suited to the early levels are slowly drained.
# Which to choose
Cash games suit people who want to play for a fixed time, leave when they like and see steadier results. Tournaments offer a large potential prize for a small fixed risk, a clear beginning and end, and long stretches of disappointment. They can last many hours with no option to stop early. Many players enjoy both. It helps to remember which one you are in, since instincts trained in one are often wrong in the other.

=== poker | The bubble and why chips are not worth what they say | Tournaments
> Near the prize money, losing chips hurts more than winning the same number helps. That imbalance has a name and a calculation, and it changes correct play.
# The independent chip model
In a tournament, the value of your stack is your share of the prize pool on average, given all the stacks. The standard way to estimate it is the independent chip model, known as ICM. It assumes each player's chance of finishing first equals their share of the chips, then works out second place among the rest in the same way, and so on. The result converts a chip stack into an expected prize in money.
# A three-player example
Three players remain with 1,000 chips each. The prizes are 50, 30 and 20 dollars. By symmetry, each stack is worth 33.33 dollars. Now two of them go all in against each other and one is eliminated. The loser collects third prize, 20 dollars. The winner has 2,000 chips against the remaining player's 1,000 and, by the model, an expected prize of 43.33 dollars. The player who sat out now has an expectation of 36.67.
# What the example shows
Look at the changes. The winner doubled their chips and their prize expectation rose by 10 dollars, from 33.33 to 43.33. The loser's fell by 13.33. The player who folded and watched gained 3.33 for doing nothing. Doubling your stack did not double its value. This is the central fact of tournament endgames. Chips you might lose are worth more than the same number of chips you might win.
# The risk premium
In the example, calling an all-in bet risks 13.33 dollars of value to gain 10. To break even you must win about 57 percent of the time, though in chips alone 50 percent would do. That extra requirement is sometimes called the risk premium. It means you should fold some hands that would be clear calls in a cash game. The more unequal the stacks and the steeper the payouts, the larger the premium becomes.
# The bubble
The effect is strongest on the bubble, the stage when one more elimination puts everyone left into the prizes. The player eliminated then has played for hours for nothing. Medium stacks have the most to lose and should play tightly, avoiding confrontations with stacks that could knock them out. A very short stack has less to protect and must take chances. The biggest stack can exploit all of this ruthlessly.
# The big stack's licence
A chip leader on the bubble can raise very frequently, because the players behind cannot call without risking their tournament. Opponents with medium stacks are forced to fold good hands. Big stacks often add substantially to their chips during this stage without showing a single hand. If you are that big stack, raise relentlessly into the medium stacks. If you are a medium stack, accept that you are being robbed and wait, unless you hold a hand strong enough to justify the risk.
# Fold when others are about to bust
The model produces advice that feels unnatural. If you have a medium stack and a much shorter one is about to be forced all in by the blinds, folding almost everything is correct, since their elimination raises your prize expectation for free. Calling an all-in with a hand that is a small favourite can be a mistake. In extreme cases, late in satellite tournaments where every remaining prize is identical, folding a pair of aces before the flop can be right.
# Do not overdo it
ICM is about the late stages. Early in a tournament, with the prizes far away, the difference between chips and money is small, and you should play to accumulate. Players who learn about ICM sometimes become too cautious everywhere and fold their way into small prizes without ever contending for large ones. Free calculators let you enter stacks and payouts to see the figures. An hour with one builds a sound feel for when survival should outweigh chips.

=== poker | Short stacks: when to move all in | Tournaments
> With ten big blinds, poker reduces to a single decision before the flop. It is the most thoroughly solved part of the game and one of the easiest to learn.
# Why the choice narrows
Suppose you have ten big blinds and raise to two and a half. You have put a quarter of your stack in. If someone reraises, you are getting such a good price that you can hardly fold. If you are called, the pot after the flop is more than half your remaining chips, and one bet commits you. Since you will rarely be able to fold later, the sound course is to put everything in at once or put in nothing.
# The power of the shove
Moving all in before the flop has two ways to win. Everyone may fold, in which case you collect the blinds and antes, typically adding a quarter or more to a ten big blind stack with no risk. Or you may be called, in which case you still have a chance, usually between 30 and 45 percent even with a modest hand. The combination makes shoving profitable with a much wider range of hands than most players expect.
# Calling is harder than pushing
A long-established principle is that you need a stronger hand to call an all-in than to make one. The player who shoves can win by a fold. The player who calls can only win at showdown and is up against a range that was chosen to be shoved. So the ranges are lopsided. From the small blind with ten big blinds, pushing more than half of all hands is correct. The big blind should call with considerably fewer.
# Position and stack size
The earlier your seat, the more players remain who could wake up with a strong hand, so the tighter your shoving range must be. Under the gun at a full table with ten big blinds, you might shove only pairs, big aces and the best suited broadway cards. On the button, with two players left to act, roughly a third or more of all hands qualify. As your stack shrinks from twelve big blinds toward six, the ranges widen further from every seat.
# Which hands hold up
When you are called, you will usually be behind, so the hands worth shoving are those that still do reasonably well against a calling range. Any ace is valuable because it is seldom far behind. Pairs are strong, since they are at least a coin flip against two higher cards. Suited connected cards do better than they look, because they are rarely dominated. The hands to avoid are weak offsuit kings and queens, which callers so often have beaten.
# Do not wait too long
The commonest error is waiting for a premium hand while the stack dwindles. With ten big blinds, a shove makes most opponents fold. With four, calling costs them so little that almost anyone will call with almost anything, and you must then win a showdown. The fold equity has gone. Each round of blinds also costs a large part of a short stack. It is better to shove a mediocre hand with eight big blinds than to wait for a good one with three.
# Being first in
Nearly all of this applies when everyone before you has folded. If someone has already raised, you are no longer stealing. You are calling their raise with your stack, and you need a far better hand, since their range is strong and they are unlikely to fold. Shoving over a raise is sound with good pairs and big aces, or against a player who raises very loosely. Being the first player to enter the pot is what gives the short stack its power.
# Learn the charts
Mathematicians have worked out the ranges at which neither the shover nor the caller can do better by changing strategy, and they are published as push or fold charts. A chart shows, for each seat and stack size, exactly which hands to shove. Memorizing the broad pattern takes an evening. Because so many tournament hands are played at these stack sizes, few areas of study give a faster return.

=== poker | Game theory or exploitation: two ways to win | Game theory in plain words
> One approach aims to be unbeatable. The other aims to punish specific mistakes. Strong players understand the first well enough to practise the second.
# An unbeatable strategy
Game theory shows that poker has an equilibrium: a strategy which, if you follow it, cannot be beaten in the long run by any counter-strategy. Players call it GTO, for game theory optimal. It mixes value bets and bluffs, calls and folds, in proportions such that an opponent gains nothing by adjusting. If they deviate from their own equilibrium play, they lose money to you automatically, without your needing to notice what they are doing wrong.
# How it is found
No human can calculate equilibrium play at the table. Computer programs called solvers approximate it by playing a simplified version of a situation against themselves many millions of times, adjusting until neither side can improve. They became widely available to players around the middle of the 2010s and transformed the study of the game. In 2017 a program beat a team of leading professionals at two-player no-limit hold'em, and in 2019 another beat professionals at six-player tables.
# What solvers taught
Some lessons surprised even experts. Solvers bet small with a wide range on many flops, where humans had bet large with a narrow one. They check strong hands more than people did. They use very large bets in spots where the opponent cannot hold the best hands. They defend their blinds with a wider range than had been considered correct. Much of what is now standard advice came from watching what the machines did and working out why.
# The limits of balance
Equilibrium play guarantees you will not lose to anyone. It does not guarantee you will win as much as possible. Against an opponent who calls far too often, the equilibrium strategy still bluffs at its usual rate and gives money away. It profits from their errors only passively. A strategy designed for a perfect adversary leaves a great deal on the table when the adversary is a tourist who has not folded a pair all evening.
# Exploitative play
The alternative is to depart from balance deliberately in order to attack a known weakness. If a player folds too much, bluff them relentlessly. If they call too much, never bluff and bet your good hands bigger. If they only raise with the nuts, fold to their raises without regret. This is exploitative play. Against weak or predictable opponents it earns far more than equilibrium play does, which is why it is the main source of profit in most real games.
# The risk
Every exploit opens you to a counter. If you bluff constantly because someone folds too often, and they notice and begin calling, you lose heavily until you adjust back. Against unobservant opponents, this hardly matters. Against good ones, it does. The larger your departure from balance, the more it earns while it works and the more it costs when it is detected. You are betting that you understand your opponent better than they understand you.
# How good players combine them
The practical approach is to use equilibrium play as a baseline and exploitation as an adjustment. Against unknown or strong opponents, stay near the baseline, where you cannot be hurt much. As you gather evidence of specific mistakes, move away from it in the direction that punishes them, by an amount proportional to your confidence. Knowing roughly what balanced play looks like is what allows you to recognize how an opponent is deviating from it.
# What to study
For most players, the best return comes from learning the broad shapes of sound play: which hands to open from each seat, how to size bets on different boards, how often to defend. Then spend your attention at the table on observing how actual opponents differ from that. Memorizing precise solver frequencies is a matter for professionals facing other professionals. Below that level, the largest edge lies in noticing mistakes and adjusting to them.

=== poker | Reviewing your play: where improvement happens | The long game
> Nobody gets better during the session. Skill is built afterwards, by looking honestly at decisions with the result set aside.
# Why review matters
At the table, decisions are made in seconds under pressure. There is no time to work out whether a call was correct, and the result of a single hand tells you almost nothing. A good play loses a third of the time. A bad play wins often enough to seem clever. Without review, you repeat the same errors for years and blame the cards. The players who improve fastest are consistently those who study their own hands away from the game.
# Mark hands as you go
During a session, note any hand where you were unsure. Online, software records every hand and lets you tag them. Live, write a few lines on your phone immediately afterwards: positions, stack sizes, the action on each street, the board. Memory rewrites hands within hours, usually in your favour. A dozen accurate records from a session are worth more than a hundred vague recollections.
# Review winners too
Most people only examine the hands they lost, hoping to find that they were unlucky. That is the wrong sample. Some of your worst plays won the pot. Calling with too little equity and hitting, bluffing into a player who rarely folds and happening to succeed. Those mistakes are the most dangerous, because they were rewarded. Choose hands for review by how uncertain you were, not by how they turned out.
# Judge the decision
For each hand, go street by street and ask what range your opponent had at that point and how your hand fared against it. Then ask whether your action was the best available given what you knew then. Cover the final result if it helps. The question is never whether you won. It is whether you would make the same play again against the same range. A correct fold that would have won the pot is still a correct fold.
# Use the tools
Free equity calculators answer most questions that come up. Enter your hand, the board and a realistic range for your opponent, and see what share of the pot you had. Compare that with the price you were offered. Many players discover that calls they agonized over were clearly right or clearly wrong by a wide margin. Solvers go further and are worth learning later. For most purposes, the arithmetic of equity against price is enough.
# Look for patterns
Single hands teach less than recurring themes. After reviewing for a few weeks, sort your notes. Do your big losses come mostly from calling large river bets? From playing too many hands in early position? From late in long sessions? Tracking software can show where you win and lose by seat and by situation. Most players find that a small number of repeated situations account for most of their losses.
# Talk hands through
Explaining a hand to another player exposes gaps in your reasoning. A study partner or a small group, ideally including someone better than you, speeds improvement considerably. Describe the hand without revealing the result, give your thinking at each point and ask what they would have done. Be prepared to be told you played it badly. If everyone always agrees with you, find a tougher group.
# Fix one thing at a time
Do not try to overhaul your whole game after one review. Choose the single most expensive pattern you have found and work on it for the next few sessions. Write a short rule for yourself, such as fold one pair to a river raise from a passive player, and check afterwards whether you followed it. Once it has become habit, take the next item. A steady ratio of perhaps one hour of study for every four or five of play is enough to keep improving.
