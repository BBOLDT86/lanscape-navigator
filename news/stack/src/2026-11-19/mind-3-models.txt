=== mind | Flow: when hard work stops feeling like work | Habits and attention
> There is a state in which attention is total, time distorts and difficult things feel effortless. It has known conditions, and you can arrange them.
# What flow is
Flow is a state of complete absorption in an activity. Attention narrows to the task. Self-consciousness fades. Time seems to speed up or slow down. Action and awareness merge, so that you are doing the thing without watching yourself do it. The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, who studied it for decades beginning in the 1970s, found the same description from rock climbers, surgeons, chess players, musicians and factory workers. He chose the word because so many of them said it felt like being carried by a current.
# The balance of challenge and skill
The central condition is a match between how hard the task is and how capable you are. If the challenge is far above your skill, you feel anxious. If it is far below, you feel bored. Flow occurs in the channel between, where the task stretches you without overwhelming you. Because skill grows with practice, the challenge must grow as well. This is why a game that was gripping at first becomes dull once you have mastered it.
# Clear goals
The second condition is knowing, moment to moment, what you are trying to do. A climber's goal is the next hold. A musician's is the next phrase. Vague tasks such as work on the report resist flow, because part of your mind is occupied with deciding what to do next. Breaking a large project into a concrete next action, such as draft the three paragraphs of the findings section, supplies the clarity that absorption needs.
# Immediate feedback
The third condition is that you can tell at once how you are doing. A tennis player sees where the ball lands. A programmer runs the code and it works or fails. Activities with slow or ambiguous feedback, such as management or long-term research, rarely produce flow unaided. You can sometimes build feedback in: a word count that rises, a checklist that shortens, a test that passes. The loop between action and result needs to be short.
# No interruptions
Flow takes time to enter, commonly ten to twenty minutes of sustained attention, and a single interruption ends it. A notification, a question from a colleague or a glance at a phone returns you to the start. This is the main reason it is so rare in modern office work. People who rely on it protect blocks of time fiercely. They close the door, silence devices and tell others when they will be available again.
# Where people find it
Surveys in which people were paged at random moments and asked what they were doing and how they felt produced a surprising result. Flow was reported more often at work than during leisure. Passive leisure, particularly watching television, almost never produced it. Work, for all the complaints about it, has goals, feedback and challenge built in. Leisure produces flow when it is active and skilled, as in sport, music, craft, gardening or games.
# What it is not
Flow is not happiness in the ordinary sense. During it, people report feeling very little, because attention is fully occupied. The satisfaction comes afterwards. Nor is it always good for you. Gambling machines and some video games are engineered to produce a flow-like absorption that serves the maker more than the player. And flow does not mean relaxing. It is a state of high effort that happens not to feel like strain.
# Arranging it
To make flow more likely, choose a task slightly beyond your comfortable level. Define exactly what done looks like for the next hour. Set up some way to see your progress. Remove every source of interruption you control. Then begin, and tolerate the first quarter of an hour, which usually feels like ordinary effort. You cannot force the state. You can only set the conditions and stay with the work long enough for it to arrive.

=== mind | Pressure and performance: the arousal curve | Emotions and stress
> A little stress sharpens you and a lot of it wrecks you. Where the turning point lies depends on what you are trying to do.
# An old experiment
In 1908 two psychologists, Robert Yerkes and John Dodson, trained mice to choose between two boxes, using mild electric shocks as the incentive. Stronger shocks made the mice learn faster, up to a point. Beyond that point, learning got worse. When the task was made harder, the best shock level was lower. Their finding, generalized to humans and to arousal of all kinds, became one of the most cited ideas in psychology, though the original evidence was thin.
# The inverted U
The usual picture is a curve shaped like a hill. On the left, at low arousal, you are sleepy, bored and unfocused, and performance is poor. As arousal rises, attention sharpens and energy increases. At the top is a zone of best performance. Beyond it, on the right, lie anxiety, tunnel vision and mistakes. The same racing heart that helped you at a moderate level now gets in the way of thinking clearly.
# Simple and complex tasks
The peak sits in a different place for different tasks. Simple, well-practised or physical tasks tolerate high arousal and may even benefit from it. A sprinter or a weightlifter wants to be highly activated. Tasks that need fine judgement, working memory or creativity are disrupted much sooner. A surgeon, a chess player or someone taking an examination does best when alert but calm. Getting psyched up helps the first kind of task and harms the second.
# Why too much hurts
Under strong stress, the body prepares for physical action. Stress hormones rise, attention narrows to the perceived threat and the brain regions that support planning and flexible thinking become less effective. That is useful when escaping danger and useless when solving a problem. People under heavy pressure fall back on habit, miss information at the edges and find that their minds go blank. Skills that are deeply practised survive best, which is why emergency crews train to the point of automatism.
# Choking
Experts sometimes fail under pressure at things they can do perfectly well in practice. One explanation is that anxiety makes them attend consciously to movements that normally run automatically, and conscious control is slower and clumsier. A golfer who thinks about the mechanics of the putting stroke putts worse. Routines help, because they occupy attention with something harmless. So does practising under realistic pressure, so that the feeling becomes familiar.
# How you read the feeling
The physical signs of anxiety and excitement are nearly identical: fast pulse, quick breathing, butterflies. Some experiments have found that people who tell themselves they are excited before a performance do better than those who try to calm down, since moving from anxious to excited is a smaller step than moving to calm. Results in this area are not all consistent, but the technique costs nothing. Treating the symptoms as readiness, not as a warning, is worth trying.
# Moving along the curve
You can shift your position deliberately. If you are too far to the right, slow your breathing with long exhalations, which engages the body's calming response within a minute or two. Narrow your focus to the next small step. If you are too far to the left, on a dull afternoon, raise arousal: move about, play music, add a deadline or a small stake, or work near other people. The skill is noticing which side of the hill you are on.
# Short stress and long stress
The curve describes pressure in the moment. Stress that continues for weeks or months is a different matter. It impairs sleep, memory and mood, and offers no benefit to performance. Brief demands followed by recovery make people more capable, much as exercise does. Unrelieved demand without recovery wears them down. A period of intense effort is sustainable only if there is a real break at the end of it.

=== mind | Deliberate practice: how skill is actually built | Learning and memory
> Experience alone does not make people better. A particular kind of effortful practice does, and it is less pleasant and more specific than simply putting in hours.
# Years are not enough
Many people do a job for twenty years without improving after the first two. Studies have found that some doctors, for example, are no more accurate at certain diagnoses late in their careers than early on. Once a skill is good enough to get by, it becomes automatic, and automatic performance stops developing. Repetition maintains a level. It does not raise it. The question that interested the psychologist Anders Ericsson was what the people who keep improving do differently.
# The violinists
In a well-known study published in 1993, Ericsson and colleagues examined violin students at a music academy in Berlin. The students judged most likely to become international soloists had accumulated considerably more hours of solitary practice by age twenty than the others, on average around ten thousand. All of the groups spent similar time on music overall. The difference lay in time spent alone, working on specific weaknesses, an activity the students themselves rated as demanding and not much fun.
# What makes practice deliberate
Ericsson called this deliberate practice and described its features. It targets a specific aspect of performance just beyond your present ability. It requires full concentration. It provides immediate feedback on what went wrong. It involves repeating the difficult part, with corrections, until it improves. And it is usually designed by a teacher or coach who knows how the skill is built. Playing a whole piece through for pleasure meets none of these conditions.
# The edge of ability
The essential ingredient is working at the boundary of what you can do. Below that boundary, practice is comfortable and teaches little. Far above it, you flounder. At the edge, you fail often, and each failure carries information. This is why deliberate practice is tiring. Even elite performers in Ericsson's studies could manage only about four hours a day of it, usually in sessions of an hour or so, and many took naps.
# Feedback
Without knowing what you did wrong, repetition merely fixes your mistakes in place. Good practice builds in a fast and honest signal. A coach watching. A recording you listen back to. A chess engine that shows the better move. A written answer checked against a model solution. Fields with rapid, clear feedback, such as chess and music, produce reliable expertise. Fields where feedback is slow or ambiguous, such as long-range forecasting, often do not.
# The ten thousand hour myth
The figure became famous through popular books, in a form Ericsson himself objected to. Ten thousand hours was an average for one group of violinists at age twenty, not a threshold, and individuals varied widely. Hours of the wrong kind count for little. And practice is not the whole story. A 2014 analysis combining many studies found that deliberate practice accounted for roughly a quarter of the differences in skill in games and a fifth in music, and much less in other fields.
# What else matters
The remaining differences reflect things such as starting age, physical and mental aptitudes, the quality of teaching and opportunity. Height matters in basketball however hard one trains. This does not make practice unimportant. It remains the largest factor that an individual controls, and nobody reaches a high level without a great deal of it. It simply means that effort is necessary and does not guarantee the same result for everyone.
# Applying it
For any skill you want to improve, identify the specific component that is holding you back. Design an exercise that isolates it. Find a source of immediate feedback. Work on it in short, concentrated sessions, and stop when concentration fades. Then repeat on another day. A cook might practise only knife cuts for twenty minutes. A writer might rewrite one paragraph five ways. An hour of this is worth many hours of comfortable repetition.

=== mind | Second-order thinking: and then what | Mental models
> Every action has consequences, and the consequences have consequences. Asking one more question than everyone else is a cheap way to avoid expensive mistakes.
# First order and beyond
First-order thinking looks at the immediate result of an action. I am hungry, so I eat the cake. Sales are slow, so we cut the price. Second-order thinking asks what happens next, and after that. If we cut the price, competitors will match it, customers will come to expect discounts and the brand will be cheapened. The first-order effect is usually obvious and the same for everyone. Advantage tends to lie in the later effects that most people do not trace.
# The rat bounty
In 1902 the French colonial government in Hanoi tried to control a plague of rats by paying a bounty for each rat tail handed in. Tails arrived by the thousand. Then officials noticed rats running about the city without tails. Catchers were cutting off the tails and releasing the animals to breed. Others had begun farming rats. A policy meant to reduce the rat population had created a reason to increase it. A similar story is told about cobras in colonial Delhi.
# Incentives change behaviour
The Hanoi episode shows the commonest second-order effect: people respond to the rule, not to its intention. Pay employees per line of code and you get long programs. Judge hospitals on waiting times and patients are kept in ambulances outside. Reward teachers for test scores and they teach to the test. Whenever you set a target or a reward, ask how a clever, self-interested person would meet it with the least effort. Some will do exactly that.
# More roads, more traffic
Building a wider road to relieve congestion seems certain to work. Traffic engineers have repeatedly observed otherwise. A faster road makes driving more attractive, so people take trips they would have skipped, travel at peak times instead of off-peak and move further from work. Within a few years the new lanes are as congested as the old. The effect is called induced demand. The first-order reasoning treated the number of drivers as fixed. It was not.
# Time and feedback
Later effects are easy to miss because they arrive late and are hard to connect to their cause. An extra hour of work each evening raises output this month. Its effects on health and family appear over years. Antibiotics given freely cure today's infections and breed resistant bacteria over decades. A useful exercise is to ask what the results will be in ten minutes, in ten months and in ten years, and to notice when the answers differ.
# How to practise it
The method is a question asked repeatedly: and then what? Write down the decision. Write its immediate effect. For each effect, ask what it will cause and who will react to it. Go two or three steps and stop, since beyond that the chain becomes guesswork. Pay particular attention to how other people will change their behaviour, since ignoring other people's responses is where most first-order reasoning goes wrong.
# Good second-order effects
The habit is not only for finding dangers. Many of the best choices are unappealing at first order and excellent afterwards. Exercise is tiring today and strengthening over months. Saving reduces what you can spend now and compounds for decades. Telling a client an unwelcome truth is uncomfortable and builds trust. Learning a difficult skill is slow and then pays for years. Options that look worse at first glance are less crowded, which is part of why they pay.
# Humility about prediction
There is a limit. Complex systems cannot be predicted in detail, and confident chains of reasoning about distant effects are frequently wrong. Second-order thinking is not a licence to argue that an obviously beneficial action will secretly backfire. Its sensible use is modest: look a step or two ahead, consider how people will adapt, prefer actions that can be reversed and watch what actually happens so that you can correct course.

=== mind | Inversion: solve it backwards | Mental models
> Many problems that resist a direct attack give way when turned around. Instead of asking how to succeed, ask what would guarantee failure, and avoid that.
# Turn the question around
The nineteenth-century German mathematician Carl Jacobi is said to have advised his students to invert, always invert, when stuck on a hard problem. Mathematicians do this routinely, proving a statement by showing that its opposite leads to a contradiction. The investor Charlie Munger made the idea widely known outside mathematics. His version was that it is often easier to see what will make a venture fail than what will make it succeed, and avoiding the first gets you a long way.
# Why backwards is easier
Success usually has many requirements, all of which must be met, and they are hard to list. Failure often needs only one thing to go wrong, and the candidates are easy to name. Asked what makes a good marriage, people offer vague generalities. Asked what would reliably ruin one, they answer at once and specifically: contempt, dishonesty, neglect. The inverted question produces a concrete list of things not to do, and a list of that kind can be acted on.
# The bombers
During the Second World War, the American military studied bombers returning from missions to decide where to add armour. The damage was concentrated on the wings and fuselage, so those areas seemed the obvious places. The statistician Abraham Wald pointed out the flaw. They were looking only at aircraft that had come back. The planes hit in the engines had not returned. Armour belonged where the surviving planes showed no holes. He had asked not where planes get hit, but where hits are fatal.
# Avoiding stupidity
A great deal of good performance consists of not making large errors. In amateur tennis, most points are lost through mistakes, not won by brilliant shots, so the amateur who simply keeps the ball in play beats the one going for winners. Investing is similar. Avoiding ruinous losses does more for long-term results than finding spectacular gains. Trying to be consistently not foolish is a more achievable aim than trying to be brilliant, and it often yields better results.
# Applying it to a goal
Take any goal and ask how you would guarantee the opposite. To ruin your health: sleep little, never move, eat badly, ignore symptoms. To fail at a job: miss deadlines, hide problems, blame others, stop learning. To lose money: borrow to speculate, put everything in one place, panic and sell at the bottom. Then check your current habits against the list. People are often surprised to find that they are already doing two or three of the items.
# For products and plans
Designers and engineers use the same move. Instead of asking what would make the product delightful, ask what would make a customer give up and leave. Instead of asking how the project will succeed, ask what would kill it. Safety engineering is built on this principle. Engineers list every way a system could fail and design against each one. Aviation's safety record comes less from making flights go well than from systematically removing the ways they go badly.
# What inversion cannot do
Avoiding failure is not the same as achieving excellence. A life spent only on eliminating mistakes can become timid. Inversion will not give you the idea for a great product or a piece of music. Its role is to clear the ground. It removes the obvious errors and the avoidable disasters so that your effort is not wasted. The positive work of creating something still has to be done by other means.
# A five-minute exercise
Choose one goal that matters to you this year. Write at the top of a page: how could I make sure this fails? List everything you can think of for five minutes. Circle the three items you are most at risk of actually doing. Decide on one specific safeguard for each. The page takes less time than most planning meetings and is often more useful, because guarding against known failures is something you can start on today.

=== mind | The famous findings that did not hold up | Classic experiments
> Several of psychology's best-known results have failed when other researchers repeated the experiments. Knowing which ones makes you a better reader of everything else.
# The replication crisis
A scientific finding is supposed to be repeatable. If another team runs the same experiment, they should get the same result. In 2015 a large collaboration of researchers repeated 100 studies that had been published in leading psychology journals. Only a little over a third produced a clear result the second time, and the effects were on average about half the original size. Similar problems have since been found in parts of medicine and economics.
# Why it happened
Most of this was not fraud. Journals preferred surprising positive results, so studies that found nothing went unpublished. Samples were often small, which makes flukes more likely. Researchers had many choices about which data to include and which comparisons to run, and could try several until one produced a publishable result, frequently without realizing what they were doing. A field can generate a large body of false findings this way while everyone acts in good faith.
# Willpower as a fuel tank
One influential idea, called ego depletion, held that self-control draws on a limited resource that runs down with use. In the original 1998 experiment, people who resisted eating chocolate gave up sooner on a later puzzle. Hundreds of studies followed. In 2016, a coordinated attempt across 23 laboratories and more than two thousand participants found an effect close to zero. The idea that willpower is depleted like a battery is now regarded as doubtful at best.
# Power posing
A 2010 study reported that standing in an expansive posture for two minutes raised testosterone, lowered a stress hormone and made people take more risks. The idea reached an enormous audience. A larger repetition in 2015 found no hormonal or behavioural effects, and one of the original authors publicly stated that she no longer believed the effects were real. People may report feeling more confident after such poses. The physiological claims have not held up.
# The marshmallow test
In studies begun in the 1960s, young children were offered one treat now or two if they could wait. Later follow-ups reported that those who waited did better in school and in life, and the test became a symbol of the importance of self-control. A 2018 study with a much larger and more varied sample found the link was far weaker and mostly disappeared once family background was taken into account. A child's ability to wait reflected their circumstances as much as their character.
# Others under question
The list goes on. Claims that reading words related to old age makes people walk more slowly have failed to repeat. The effect of holding a pen in the mouth to force a smile turned out to be much smaller than first reported. The Stanford prison experiment of 1971 has been heavily criticized after evidence emerged that the guards were coached. Not every challenged finding is false, but each of these deserves much less confidence than its fame suggests.
# What did hold up
Many classic findings have survived repeated testing. Anchoring is robust. So is the tendency to recall material better after testing than after rereading, and the benefit of spacing study over time. The Stroop effect, in which naming the ink colour of a colour word is slowed when the two conflict, is among the most reliable in the field. The crisis did not show that psychology is worthless. It showed which parts rest on firm ground.
# How to read a headline
The field has responded with larger samples, plans registered before data are collected and routine replication. For a reader, a few questions help. Was the study large? Has anyone repeated it? Is the claimed effect surprisingly big for such a small intervention? Is one study being presented as settled fact? A single exciting result is a suggestion. A finding repeated by independent teams is knowledge. Popular science books written before about 2015 should be read with that in mind.
