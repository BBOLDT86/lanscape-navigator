=== world | Thirteen days: the Cuban Missile Crisis | Turning points
> In October 1962 the United States and the Soviet Union came closer to nuclear war than at any other time. It ended through a deal that stayed secret for decades.
# The discovery
On October 14, 1962, an American spy plane photographed construction sites in western Cuba. Analysts identified them as launch positions for Soviet nuclear missiles capable of reaching Washington and much of the United States within minutes. President John F. Kennedy was told on the morning of October 16. The Soviet leader, Nikita Khrushchev, had sent the missiles secretly, to protect Cuba from invasion and to offset American missiles stationed near Soviet borders, in Turkey and Italy.
# The choices
Kennedy gathered a small group of advisers who met in secret for days. Military leaders urged air strikes on the missile sites followed by an invasion of Cuba. Others feared that an attack would kill Soviet troops and bring retaliation, perhaps against West Berlin, and then escalation. A middle course emerged: a naval blockade to stop further military shipments, combined with a demand that the missiles be removed. It left room for the next step to be Khrushchev's.
# The quarantine
On the evening of October 22, Kennedy announced the discovery on television and declared what he called a quarantine of Cuba, avoiding the word blockade, which is an act of war in law. American forces worldwide went to a heightened state of alert. Soviet ships were at sea, heading for the line. On October 24, the ships carrying military cargo stopped or turned back. Work on the missile sites already in Cuba, however, continued at speed.
# The most dangerous day
Saturday, October 27, was the worst. An American spy plane was shot down over Cuba and its pilot killed. Another strayed by accident into Soviet airspace. Kennedy's military advisers pressed for an attack within days. Unknown to Washington, Soviet forces in Cuba already had short-range nuclear weapons for use against an invasion. Leaders on both sides were making decisions with incomplete and partly wrong information about what the other had and intended.
# The submarine
That same day, American warships were dropping small practice depth charges to force a Soviet submarine to the surface near the quarantine line. The submarine carried a nuclear torpedo, which the Americans did not know. Its crew, out of contact with Moscow and suffering in extreme heat, believed war might have begun. The captain reportedly wanted to fire. Launch required the agreement of three senior officers. One of them, Vasili Arkhipov, refused, and the submarine surfaced.
# The deal
That evening the president's brother, Robert Kennedy, met the Soviet ambassador privately. The public terms were that the Soviet Union would remove its missiles from Cuba and the United States would pledge not to invade the island. There was a secret addition. The American missiles in Turkey would be withdrawn within a few months, provided this was never revealed. On October 28, Khrushchev announced that the missiles would be dismantled.
# What was hidden
For years the crisis was presented as a triumph of firmness, in which the Soviets backed down. The missile trade was publicly confirmed only decades later. The fuller record shows two leaders who had both been frightened by how nearly events had escaped them, and who each gave something up. Much of the danger had come from subordinates acting on their own and from accidents, not from decisions taken at the top.
# What changed
Both governments drew the conclusion that they needed better ways to communicate. In 1963 a direct teleprinter link, known as the hotline, was set up between Washington and Moscow. A treaty banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water was signed the same year. The rivalry continued for nearly three more decades, yet neither side again took a gamble of that kind. The crisis remains the standard case study in how wars can begin by miscalculation.

=== world | The computer that landed on the Moon | Engineering feats
> Apollo's guidance computer had less processing power than a cheap calculator. In the last minutes of the first landing, its design saved the mission.
# The goal
In May 1961, President Kennedy asked Congress to commit the United States to landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely before the end of the decade. At that moment the country had fifteen minutes of human spaceflight experience. The programme that followed, Apollo, employed around 400,000 people at its peak and cost roughly 25 billion dollars at the time. On July 20, 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed while Michael Collins orbited above.
# Why a computer was needed
Navigating to the Moon and landing on it required continuous calculation that no crew could do by hand. The spacecraft had to know its position and speed, fire its engines at exact moments for exact durations, and during landing adjust the thrust many times a second. Ground control could help with navigation in flight. The descent to the surface, with a radio delay of over a second each way, had to be controlled on board.
# The machine
The Apollo Guidance Computer was designed at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. It weighed about 32 kilograms and drew about as much power as a light bulb. It had roughly 2,000 words of erasable memory and 36,000 words of permanent memory. The astronauts communicated with it through a numeric keypad, entering two-digit codes for verbs and nouns. There was one in the command module and one in the lunar lander.
# Woven memory
The permanent memory was literally woven. Each bit was stored by passing a wire either through or around a tiny magnetic ring: through for a one, around for a zero. Workers at a factory, many of them women with experience in textiles, threaded the wires by hand, following the program. Once made, the memory could not be altered. The software therefore had to be finished and thoroughly tested months before each flight.
# Chips
The computer was among the first to be built from integrated circuits, the newly invented chips that put several transistors on one piece of silicon. In the early 1960s, the Apollo programme was buying a large share of all the chips made in the United States. That demand helped manufacturers improve reliability and bring down costs, which in turn hastened the growth of the industry that later produced personal computers.
# The software
The programs were written by a team led by Margaret Hamilton, who promoted the term software engineering to give the work the standing of other engineering disciplines. Her team designed the system to assign a priority to every task. If the computer was asked to do more than it could, it would abandon the least important jobs, restart and resume the vital ones. Few computers of the day had anything like this.
# The alarms
During the descent of Apollo 11, the computer began flashing alarm codes the astronauts had never seen in training. A radar left switched on in an unneeded mode was flooding it with useless data, and it was overloaded. Exactly as designed, it dropped low-priority work and kept flying the landing. An engineer in mission control recognized the code and judged it safe to continue. The decision to go on was made within seconds.
# The landing
The automatic guidance was bringing the lander down toward a crater strewn with boulders. Armstrong took partial manual control and flew onward to find level ground while the fuel ran low. He set down with well under a minute of fuel remaining before a mandatory abort. Twelve people walked on the Moon between 1969 and 1972. Each of those landings depended on a computer far weaker than the one in a modern washing machine.

=== world | The transistor: the invention inside everything | Inventions that changed things
> A small device demonstrated in a New Jersey laboratory in 1947 replaced the vacuum tube and became the most manufactured object in human history.
# The problem with tubes
Before the transistor, electronic equipment relied on vacuum tubes: glass bulbs in which a heated filament released electrons that a small voltage could control. Tubes could amplify signals and act as switches, which made radio, long-distance telephone calls and the first computers possible. They were also bulky, fragile, hot and hungry for power, and they burned out like light bulbs. An early American computer of 1945 used about 18,000 of them and consumed as much electricity as a small town.
# Bell Labs
The telephone company's research arm, Bell Laboratories, wanted something better for its vast network of amplifiers and switches. After the Second World War it set up a group to study semiconductors, materials such as germanium and silicon that conduct electricity better than insulators and worse than metals. The group was led by William Shockley and included the theorist John Bardeen and the experimenter Walter Brattain.
# December 1947
Bardeen and Brattain found that two closely spaced gold contacts pressed onto a piece of germanium could make a small current control a larger one. In December 1947 they demonstrated the device to their managers by amplifying a voice. It was named the transistor. Shockley, displeased at having been left out of the breakthrough, worked intensely over the following weeks and devised a more robust and practical design. The three shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1956.
# Out of the laboratory
The first widely sold product built on the new device was a pocket radio that went on sale in the United States in 1954. Japanese firms soon led the market, and the word transistor came to mean a small radio. Computers began to switch from tubes to transistors later in the decade. They became smaller, cooler and far more reliable. But every transistor still had to be made separately and wired to the others by hand.
# The integrated circuit
The answer was to make many transistors and their connections together on one piece of semiconductor. Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments built the first working example in 1958. Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor independently devised a version using flat layers of silicon that proved far easier to manufacture. The integrated circuit, or chip, removed the hand wiring and opened the way to putting more and more transistors on each one.
# Moore's observation
In 1965 Gordon Moore, a colleague of Noyce, noted that the number of components on a chip had been doubling every year and predicted the trend would continue. He later adjusted the pace to every two years. For more than half a century the industry kept roughly to that curve. A chip of the early 1970s held a few thousand transistors. A processor in a current phone holds well over ten billion.
# The type that won
Nearly all of those are a kind called the MOSFET, first built at Bell Labs in 1959. It is controlled by voltage, uses very little power and can be made extremely small. The dimensions of the smallest features on today's chips are measured in billionths of a metre. By some estimates, more MOSFETs have been manufactured than any other object, a number written with more than twenty digits.
# What it made possible
Each time transistors shrank, they became cheaper, faster and less power-hungry, and something new became practical: the pocket calculator, the personal computer, the mobile phone, the internet and the present generation of artificial intelligence. Few inventions have reshaped so much in so short a time. The same tiny switch, endlessly multiplied, is the basis of almost every electronic device. It began as a patch of germanium, a scrap of gold foil and a plastic wedge.

=== world | How the internet moves your data | How it works
> A message crossing the internet is chopped into pieces that travel separately and are reassembled at the far end. No single authority directs any of it.
# Circuits and packets
The old telephone network connected two callers by reserving a continuous path between them for the whole call. That is wasteful for computers, which send data in short bursts. In the 1960s, researchers in the United States and Britain independently proposed another approach. Break each message into small blocks, label each with its destination and let them find their own way across a shared network. The British researcher Donald Davies called the blocks packets.
# The first network
The idea was first tried on a large scale in a network funded by the American defence research agency and called ARPANET. On October 29, 1969, a computer at the University of California in Los Angeles sent the first message to another at a research institute several hundred kilometres north. The operator was typing the word login. The system crashed after two letters. Within a few years, the network linked dozens of universities and laboratories.
# A common language
Other networks appeared, each with its own rules, and they could not talk to one another. In the 1970s, Vinton Cerf and Robert Kahn designed a set of rules, or protocols, that allowed separate networks to be joined into one. ARPANET adopted them on January 1, 1983, a date often given as the birth of the internet. The name itself comes from the idea of a network of networks.
# Addresses and routers
Every device connected to the internet has a numerical address. Every packet carries the address it came from and the address it is going to. Between networks sit specialized computers called routers. A router reads the destination on each arriving packet and forwards it one step closer, using a table of routes it keeps updated by exchanging information with its neighbours. No router knows the whole path. Each knows only the next hop.
# Different routes, one message
Packets belonging to the same message may take different paths and arrive out of order, and some may be lost. A second layer of rules deals with that. The receiving computer checks the packets, puts them back in sequence and asks for any that are missing to be sent again. The sender also slows down when the network is congested. A photograph or a web page is thus rebuilt exactly from fragments that crossed the world separately.
# Names
People prefer names to numbers. A worldwide directory called the domain name system translates a name typed into a browser into the numerical address of the computer that holds the site. Your device asks a nearby directory server, which asks others if it does not know, and the answer comes back in a fraction of a second. The system was introduced in the 1980s and is consulted billions of times a day.
# The physical network
For all the talk of clouds, the internet is a physical thing. Nearly all traffic between continents travels as pulses of light through fibre-optic cables laid on the sea bed, each about as thick as a garden hose. Satellites carry a small share. On land, the fibres run alongside railways, roads and pipelines to buildings where networks interconnect. A ship's anchor dragging across a cable can slow the connections of an entire country.
# Nobody is in charge
There is no central control room. The internet is tens of thousands of independently owned networks that have agreed to use the same protocols and to carry one another's traffic. Technical standards are set by open bodies of engineers. The World Wide Web, invented by Tim Berners-Lee around 1990, is one application that runs on top of this foundation, as are email and video calls. The design's lack of a centre is the source of both its resilience and its disorder.

=== world | Longitude: the clock that found a ship's position | Inventions that changed things
> For centuries sailors could tell how far north or south they were and had no reliable way to know east from west. A carpenter solved it with a watch.
# Half a position
A ship's latitude, its distance north or south of the equator, can be found by measuring the height of the Sun at noon or of certain stars at night. Sailors had done this for centuries. Longitude, the position east or west, was a different matter. With no way to measure it, navigators estimated it from speed, heading and time elapsed, a method called dead reckoning. Over weeks at sea, the errors grew to hundreds of kilometres.
# The cost
The consequences were lethal. Ships missed islands where they needed to take on water, and crews died of scurvy on voyages that ran long. Ships struck coasts their captains believed were far away. In 1707 a British naval squadron returning from the Mediterranean ran onto rocks off the Scilly Isles in fog, having misjudged its position. Four ships sank, and well over a thousand men drowned. It was one of the worst disasters in British naval history.
# The prize
In 1714 the British Parliament offered a reward of up to 20,000 pounds, a fortune at the time, for a practical method of finding longitude at sea to within half a degree. A board of scientists and naval officers was set up to judge proposals. Most learned opinion expected the answer to come from astronomy, by using the Moon's motion against the stars as a kind of celestial clock.
# Why time is the answer
The Earth turns through 15 degrees of longitude every hour. So longitude is really a difference in time. If you know that it is noon where you are, by the Sun, and you know that at the same moment it is 2 in the afternoon at your home port, you are 30 degrees west of home. The entire difficulty was in knowing the time at home. That required a clock that would keep accurate time for months on a pitching, damp ship.
# Harrison
No such clock existed. Pendulum clocks were useless at sea, and changes in temperature made springs run fast or slow. John Harrison, a carpenter and self-taught clockmaker from the north of England, devoted most of his life to the problem. Between the 1730s and 1750s he built three large and ingenious sea clocks. Then he changed course entirely and produced a fourth timekeeper that looked like an oversized pocket watch, about 13 centimetres across.
# The trial
In 1761 Harrison's son carried the watch on a voyage from England to Jamaica. After 81 days at sea, it was found to be only about five seconds slow, a small fraction of the allowed error. The board was unconvinced and suspected luck. Its leading astronomers favoured the rival lunar method and demanded more trials and full disclosure of the mechanism. Harrison received his money in instalments over many years.
# Vindication
Harrison was by then an old man. He appealed to King George III, who tested one of the watches himself and took up the case. In 1773 Parliament awarded Harrison most of the remaining sum. He died three years later. Meanwhile, Captain James Cook had taken a copy of the watch on his second voyage around the world and praised it as a trusty guide. Within a few decades, marine chronometers were standard equipment.
# What remains
The lunar method was also made to work and was used alongside chronometers for a century, as it needed no costly instrument. In 1884 an international conference chose the line through the observatory at Greenwich, near London, as the world's zero of longitude. Satellite navigation has since replaced both methods. The principle is unchanged. A satellite receiver, like Harrison's watch, finds position by measuring time with great precision.

=== world | Why the calendar has leap years | How it works
> The Earth does not circle the Sun in a whole number of days. Twenty centuries of adjustments to cope with that are why February is odd and why 1900 had no leap day.
# The awkward number
The seasons repeat every 365.2422 days, the time the Earth takes to return to the same point in its orbit relative to the Sun. A calendar of 365 days falls behind the seasons by almost a quarter of a day each year. After a century, it is out by more than three weeks. For societies that needed to know when to plant and when to hold festivals, keeping the calendar aligned with the seasons was a practical necessity.
# Caesar's reform
The early Roman calendar was a muddle, adjusted by officials who sometimes lengthened or shortened years for political advantage. By the time of Julius Caesar it was about three months out of step with the seasons. In 46 before the common era, advised by an astronomer from Alexandria, Caesar imposed a new system: a year of 365 days with one extra day every fourth year. The year of the changeover had to be extended to 445 days to catch up.
# A small error
The Julian calendar assumes a year of exactly 365.25 days. The true figure is about 11 minutes shorter. That seems trivial, and it accumulates. The calendar gained a full day on the seasons roughly every 128 years. By the sixteenth century, the spring equinox was arriving about ten days earlier in the calendar than it had when the rules for dating Easter were fixed. The Church, which set its festivals by the equinox, decided to act.
# The Gregorian fix
In 1582 Pope Gregory XIII introduced the calendar most of the world now uses. It made two changes. Ten days were dropped at once: in the countries that adopted it immediately, Thursday the 4th of October was followed by Friday the 15th. And the leap year rule was refined. Years ending in two zeros are leap years only if they can be divided by 400. So 1600 and 2000 had a leap day. 1700, 1800 and 1900 did not.
# How good it is
The refinement removes three leap days every four centuries, giving an average year of 365.2425 days. That is within about half a minute of the true value. The Gregorian calendar drifts by one day in roughly three thousand years, which nobody has yet felt the need to correct. A sixteenth-century committee, working from naked-eye astronomy, produced a rule that will serve without amendment for longer than recorded history has so far lasted.
# A slow adoption
Catholic countries switched in 1582. Protestant and Orthodox countries, wary of a papal decree, held out. Britain and its colonies changed in 1752, by which time eleven days had to be dropped: the 2nd of September was followed by the 14th. Russia changed in 1918, which is why its October Revolution took place in November by the new reckoning. Greece followed in 1923. For centuries, travellers crossing Europe had to change the date as well as their money.
# Traces in the months
The names of the months preserve older arrangements. September, October, November and December come from the Latin for seven, eight, nine and ten, though they are now the ninth to twelfth months. The Roman year once began in March. July was renamed for Julius Caesar and August for his successor, Augustus. February, the last month of the old year, was where spare days were added or removed, which is why it is still the short one.
# Seconds, too
The adjustments have not stopped. The Earth's rotation is very slightly irregular and gradually slowing, so the length of a day measured by atomic clocks drifts away from the day measured by the Sun. Since 1972, timekeepers have occasionally inserted an extra second to keep the two together. These leap seconds cause trouble for computer systems, and the international bodies responsible have agreed to phase them out within the coming years.
