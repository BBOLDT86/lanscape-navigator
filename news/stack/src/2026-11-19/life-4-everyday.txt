=== life | First aid: the first five minutes | Preparedness
> In an emergency, a bystander who knows a few simple actions matters more than equipment. These are the ones worth learning, in outline.
# Call first, and stay safe
The most important act in a serious emergency is to call the emergency number, or to tell a specific person to do it. Do not assume someone else has. Before you approach, check that you will not become a second casualty: traffic, fire, electricity, deep water. The dispatcher on the phone will guide you through what to do until help arrives, so put the phone on speaker and keep it beside you. Everything below is a summary. A proper first aid course is far better.
# Severe bleeding
Heavy bleeding can kill in minutes, faster than an ambulance can arrive. The remedy is firm, direct pressure. Put a clean cloth, or a bare hand if nothing else is available, on the wound and press hard without letting up. If blood soaks through, add more cloth on top and keep pressing. Do not lift it to look. For life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg that pressure will not stop, a tourniquet applied above the wound is recommended by current guidance.
# When someone collapses
If a person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, they need chest compressions at once. Kneel beside them, place the heel of one hand on the centre of the chest with the other hand on top, and push hard and fast, about two compressions a second, letting the chest rise fully between pushes. Untrained bystanders are advised to do compressions only, without rescue breaths. Continue until help takes over. Doing something imperfectly is far better than doing nothing.
# The defibrillator
Many public buildings have an automated external defibrillator in a wall cabinet. These devices are designed for people with no training. Switch it on and it gives spoken instructions. It analyses the heart rhythm and will only deliver a shock if one is needed, so it cannot be used wrongly on someone who does not need it. Send someone to fetch one while compressions continue. Early use of a defibrillator greatly improves the chance of survival.
# Choking
A person who is truly choking cannot speak, cough or breathe, and may clutch at their throat. If they can cough forcefully, encourage them to keep coughing. If they cannot, first aid organizations teach a combination of firm blows between the shoulder blades and abdominal thrusts, in which you stand behind the person and pull sharply inward and upward above the navel. The techniques differ for infants and are best learned hands-on in a class.
# Burns
For a burn, cool the area under cool running water as soon as possible, for around twenty minutes. This limits the damage and eases pain, and it helps even if started some time after the injury. Remove rings and tight items before swelling begins. Do not use ice, butter, oil or toothpaste, all of which can make matters worse. Cover the burn loosely with cling film or a clean dressing. Large or deep burns, and burns to the face, hands or genitals, need medical care.
# Unconscious but breathing
A person who is unconscious and breathing normally should be rolled onto their side with the head tilted slightly back, so that the tongue and any fluid do not block the airway. This is the recovery position. Stay with them and watch their breathing until help arrives. If a neck or back injury is likely, move them as little as possible. Do not give an unconscious or drowsy person anything to eat or drink.
# Prepare before you need it
Take a certified first aid and resuscitation course and refresh it every couple of years, since guidance changes and skills fade. Keep a basic kit at home and in the car: dressings, bandages, tape, gloves, scissors and a foil blanket. Know where the nearest defibrillator is at your workplace and gym. Save the emergency number for any country you travel to. These few preparations take an afternoon, and one of them may one day save someone you know.

=== life | Home fire safety: the plan to make before you need it | Preparedness
> Modern furnishings burn fast, and the time to get out of a burning house can be only a couple of minutes. Preparation decides what happens in those minutes.
# Why time is so short
Fire services warn that a household may have as little as two minutes to escape once a smoke alarm sounds. Furniture and fittings made with synthetic materials burn hotter and faster than the wood, cotton and wool of older homes, and they produce dense toxic smoke. Most people who die in house fires are overcome by smoke and gases, often in their sleep, before flames reach them. The purpose of every precaution is to gain time and to use it.
# Smoke alarms
Working smoke alarms are the single most effective protection. Fit one on every level of the home, inside each bedroom and outside each sleeping area. Interconnected alarms, which all sound when one detects smoke, are best. Press the test button once a month. Replace batteries when the unit chirps, unless it has a sealed long-life battery. Replace the whole alarm every ten years, since the sensors degrade. The date of manufacture is printed on the back.
# The kitchen
Cooking is the leading cause of house fires, and the usual story is a pan left unattended. Stay in the kitchen while frying or grilling. If a pan of oil catches fire, do not move it and never throw water on it, which makes burning oil erupt. Turn off the heat if you can reach the control safely, and slide a lid or a baking sheet over the pan to smother the flames. Leave it covered until it is completely cool.
# Other common causes
Portable heaters should stand at least a metre from anything that can burn, and should be switched off when you leave the room or go to sleep. Do not overload sockets or run cables under rugs. Clean the lint filter of a tumble dryer after every load. Charge phones, scooters and other battery devices on hard surfaces, with the correct charger, and not overnight on a bed. Candles and cigarettes account for a large share of fatal fires.
# Close the doors
A closed door is a remarkably effective barrier. It holds back heat and smoke and limits the oxygen feeding the fire. Tests by fire researchers have found dramatic differences in temperature and air quality between rooms with open doors and rooms with closed ones during the same fire. Sleeping with bedroom doors shut can keep a room survivable for precious extra minutes. Closing doors behind you as you leave slows the spread.
# The escape plan
Every household should agree on a plan and walk through it. Identify two ways out of every room where possible, usually a door and a window. Choose a meeting place outside, such as a particular tree or a neighbour's gate, so that you can tell at once whether everyone is out. Practise it twice a year, including at night. Children who have rehearsed are far more likely to act correctly than to hide.
# Getting out
If the alarm sounds, leave at once. Do not stop for belongings. Smoke and heat rise, so stay low where the air is clearer. Before opening a door, feel it with the back of your hand. If it is hot, use your other way out. Once outside, stay outside and call the fire service from there. Never go back in for possessions or pets. If your clothes catch fire, stop, drop to the ground and roll.
# Extinguishers and carbon monoxide
A household extinguisher can put out a small, contained fire if you have a clear exit behind you. The method is to pull the pin, aim at the base of the flames, squeeze the handle and sweep from side to side. If the fire is not out within seconds, leave. Separately, any home with fuel-burning appliances or an attached garage needs carbon monoxide alarms. That gas cannot be seen or smelled and kills people in their sleep every winter.

=== life | Your back and your desk | The body
> Most back pain is not caused by damage and gets better with movement. The best posture is the one you are about to change.
# How common it is
Low back pain is among the most widespread health complaints in the world, and most adults experience it at some point. In the great majority of cases, no specific structural cause can be found, and doctors describe it as non-specific. That is reassuring. It means the pain usually does not signal serious damage. Most episodes improve substantially within a few weeks, whatever is done, though recurrences are common.
# Keep moving
For decades, the standard advice for a sore back was bed rest. Research overturned it. Trials comparing rest with staying active found that people who continued with ordinary activity, within the limits of pain, recovered faster and had fewer long-term problems. Current guidelines in many countries recommend remaining active, returning to normal activities as soon as possible and using exercise as the main treatment. Hurting does not necessarily mean harming.
# The myth of perfect posture
Many people believe that slouching damages the spine and that sitting bolt upright protects it. The evidence for this is weak. Studies have not found a consistent link between any particular sitting posture and back pain. The spine is a strong, adaptable structure built to bend. What seems to matter more is staying in any single position for a long time. Researchers and physiotherapists increasingly say that the best posture is the next one.
# Break up sitting
The practical consequence is to change position often. Stand up at least every half hour, even for a minute. Walk to speak to a colleague. Take phone calls on your feet. Shift in your chair. A desk that adjusts between sitting and standing height helps mainly because it makes changing position easy, not because standing is better. Standing still all day brings problems of its own.
# Setting up a desk
A comfortable arrangement reduces strain on the neck, shoulders and wrists. Place the top of the screen at about eye level and an arm's length away. Keep the keyboard and mouse close enough that your elbows stay near your sides, bent at roughly a right angle. Your feet should rest flat on the floor or on a support. A laptop used for hours needs a separate keyboard and a stand, since otherwise either the screen is too low or the keys are too high.
# Lifting
For heavy objects, the familiar advice holds. Get close to the load, bend at the hips and knees, hold it near your body and avoid twisting while carrying. More important than technique is not lifting more than you are conditioned for. Backs become robust through gradual, regular loading, which is why strength training is protective. People who are generally strong and active are less likely to be troubled by back pain.
# What helps
Exercise is the best-supported treatment for persistent back pain, and no one type has proved clearly superior. Walking, strengthening, stretching, yoga and swimming have all shown benefit. The best choice is whichever one you will keep doing. Heat can ease a sore back in the short term. Good sleep, lower stress and a healthy weight all help. Scans are seldom useful for ordinary back pain and often show harmless changes that cause needless worry.
# When to seek help urgently
A few symptoms need prompt medical attention. Numbness around the groin or inner thighs, new difficulty passing urine or controlling the bowels, and weakness in the legs can indicate pressure on the nerves at the base of the spine and are an emergency. Pain after a serious fall or accident, pain with fever or unexplained weight loss, and severe pain that worsens at night should be assessed by a doctor.

=== life | Laundry: the chemistry in the drum | Around the house
> Washing clothes is applied chemistry. Knowing what detergent does and what the symbols on the label mean keeps clothes cleaner and lasting longer.
# What detergent does
Laundry detergent has several working ingredients. Surfactants loosen oily dirt and hold it in the water so that it rinses away. Builders soften the water, which lets the surfactants work properly. Many detergents contain enzymes, biological catalysts that break down particular kinds of stain: one kind for proteins such as blood and egg, another for starches, another for fats. Optical brighteners are added to make whites appear brighter under daylight.
# Cold water works
Heating water accounts for most of the energy a washing machine uses, about nine tenths by the estimate of the American energy efficiency programme. Modern detergents and their enzymes are formulated to work in cool water, and most everyday loads come out just as clean. Cold washing is also gentler, with less fading and shrinking. Hot water is still worth using for towels, bedding and heavily soiled items, and for anything used by someone who has been ill.
# Less detergent than you think
Using more detergent does not produce cleaner clothes. Excess is not fully rinsed out. It leaves a residue that stiffens fabric, traps dirt and odours and builds up inside the machine. Modern machines use little water, and concentrated detergents need only a small measure. Follow the dosing lines on the cap, adjusted for load size, soil and water hardness. If towels feel stiff or the machine smells musty, too much detergent is a likely cause.
# Sorting
Sort by colour and by fabric. New dark and brightly dyed items release dye for their first several washes and will tint anything pale. Wash whites, lights and darks separately. Heavy items such as jeans and towels abrade delicate fabrics and should not share a load with them. Close zips so they do not snag other garments, and turn printed shirts and dark jeans inside out to reduce fading.
# Stains
Treat a stain as soon as you can, before it dries. Blot, do not rub. Rinse from the back of the fabric with cold water to push the stain out the way it came in. Protein stains such as blood, egg and milk must be treated cold, since hot water sets them permanently. Oily stains respond to a drop of washing-up liquid worked in before washing. Check that the stain has gone before using a dryer, because heat fixes whatever remains.
# The care label
The symbols on a garment's label form a small language. A tub shape gives the washing instructions, with a number for the maximum temperature in Celsius or dots for heat level, and a hand in the tub meaning hand wash. A triangle concerns bleach. A square concerns drying, with a circle inside it for tumble drying. An iron shape gives ironing heat by its dots. A plain circle means professional cleaning. A cross through any symbol means do not.
# Drying
Tumble drying is hard on clothes. The lint in the filter is fibre that has been worn off the fabric. High heat shrinks cotton and damages the elastic in underwear and sportswear. Use the lowest heat that does the job and take clothes out while slightly damp. Line or rack drying costs nothing and extends the life of garments. Wool shrinks through a combination of heat, moisture and agitation, which is why it is washed cool and laid flat.
# Looking after the machine
A washing machine needs occasional attention. Leave the door and detergent drawer open between washes so that the interior can dry, which discourages mould on the door seal. Run an empty hot cycle every month or so to clear residue. Clean the drain filter a few times a year. Empty the dryer's lint filter after every load, both for efficiency and because accumulated lint is a recognized cause of house fires.

=== life | Hanging things on walls so they stay there | Repairs a homeowner can do
> A shelf or a television that falls off the wall was fixed to the wrong thing. Knowing what is behind the plasterboard is most of the job.
# What a wall is made of
Most modern interior walls are sheets of plasterboard, also called drywall, fixed to a frame of vertical timbers called studs. The board itself is a soft, chalky material faced with paper. It will hold a nail well enough for a small picture and almost nothing heavier. Anything with real weight has to be fixed either into a stud or with a fixing designed to spread the load across the back of the board.
# Finding the studs
In North American houses, studs are usually spaced 16 inches apart, measured centre to centre, and each is about an inch and a half wide. Electrical boxes for sockets and switches are normally fixed to the side of a stud, which gives you a starting point to measure from. Knocking on the wall produces a hollow sound between studs and a duller one over them. An electronic stud finder, or a small strong magnet that locates the screws holding the board, is more reliable.
# Light loads
For a framed picture of a few kilograms, a picture hook with a nail driven in at a downward angle is enough, even into plasterboard alone. Galleries commonly hang work so that its centre is about 145 centimetres, or 57 inches, from the floor, roughly eye level for an average adult. Measure, mark lightly in pencil and use a spirit level for anything wide. Two hooks set apart keep a frame from tilting.
# Medium loads
For mirrors, small shelves and curtain rails, where no stud is conveniently placed, use a wall anchor. Simple plastic plugs that expand as a screw is driven in are suitable only for light items. Self-drilling anchors with coarse threads hold more. Toggle bolts, which pass through a hole and open out behind the board like wings, spread the load widely and hold considerably more. The packaging states a rated load, which should comfortably exceed the weight to be hung.
# Heavy loads
Televisions, large shelves loaded with books, wall cabinets and anything a person might pull on should be screwed into studs with long, substantial screws that pass through the board and well into the timber. A wall bracket for a television is designed to span two studs. If the studs are not where you need them, fix a horizontal timber or metal rail across two studs first and mount to that.
# Masonry is different
Solid walls of brick, block or concrete require a different approach. A hammer drill with a masonry bit makes the hole. A plastic or nylon plug of the correct size is tapped in, and the screw expands it against the sides of the hole. Drill into the brick or block itself, not into the softer mortar between. The plug, the drill bit and the screw must match, and the sizes are printed on the packet.
# What not to drill into
Cables and pipes run inside walls. Electrical cables generally travel vertically above and below sockets and switches, and horizontally at the level of sockets, so avoid drilling directly in line with them. Pipes are likely near sinks, baths and radiators. Many stud finders include a setting that detects live cables and metal. Drill only as deep as you need. A piece of tape wrapped around the bit makes a simple depth marker.
# A few good habits
Hold an envelope or a folded sticky note beneath the drill to catch the dust. Drill a narrow pilot hole before driving a large screw into timber, to prevent splitting. Fit the screws loosely, check with the level, then tighten. Test the fixing by pulling down on it firmly before trusting it with something that matters. If you rent your home, check what your agreement says about making holes before you begin.

=== life | The basic toolkit: twelve tools that cover most jobs | Tools
> Most household repairs need the same few tools. Buying them once, at reasonable quality, costs less than a single call-out fee.
# Why own tools
A great many household tasks are simple: tightening a loose hinge, assembling furniture, hanging a shelf, replacing a tap washer, bleeding a radiator. They become difficult only without the right tool. A basic kit turns these from problems into ten-minute jobs. It does not need to be large or expensive. A dozen well-chosen tools kept together in one box will handle the great majority of what an ordinary home requires.
# Screwdrivers
Start with screwdrivers, since screws hold most things together. You need at least two cross-head drivers and two flat ones in different sizes, or one handle with interchangeable bits. The driver must fit the screw snugly. One that is too small will slip and chew up the head, after which the screw is very hard to remove. Press firmly as you turn. Clockwise tightens and anticlockwise loosens, with rare exceptions.
# Hammer and pliers
A claw hammer of medium weight drives nails and pulls them out. Hold it near the end of the handle and let its weight do the work. Combination pliers grip, bend and cut wire. Long-nosed pliers reach into tight places and hold small parts. An adjustable spanner fits nuts and bolts of many sizes and should be set tight on the nut so that it does not slip and round off the corners.
# Measuring and marking
A steel tape measure of five metres or more is essential. The hook on its end is deliberately loose. It slides by exactly its own thickness so that measurements are accurate whether you hook it over an edge or push it against a surface. A spirit level tells you whether something is truly horizontal or vertical. Add a pencil and a square for marking right angles. The old rule is to measure twice and cut once.
# Cutting
A utility knife with replaceable blades cuts cardboard, carpet, plasterboard and much else. Change the blade as soon as it dulls, since a blunt blade needs more force and is more likely to slip. Always cut away from your body and retract the blade when you put it down. A small hand saw handles occasional cuts in timber. A junior hacksaw cuts metal and plastic pipe.
# The cordless drill
The one power tool worth buying first is a cordless drill that also drives screws. It makes holes in wood, metal and, with the right bit, light masonry, and it saves a great deal of effort when assembling furniture or fixing things to walls. It has an adjustable clutch, a numbered ring behind the chuck, that stops turning at a set resistance so that you do not overdrive screws. Buy a mixed set of drill and driver bits with it.
# The small extras
A set of hex keys fits the bolts in flat-pack furniture and many bicycles. A torch, or better a head torch, leaves both hands free under a sink. A roll of strong tape, some cable ties, a selection of screws and wall plugs and a tube of general-purpose glue solve a surprising number of problems. Light machine oil eases stiff hinges and locks. Penetrating spray helps free seized bolts but is not a lasting lubricant.
# Safety and quality
Eye protection is cheap and should be worn whenever drilling, hammering or cutting, since fragments travel fast. Gloves help with rough materials. Turn off the power at the breaker before any electrical work and the water at the valve before any plumbing. As for quality, the cheapest tools often fit poorly and wear out quickly. Mid-range tools from an established maker, bought once, will usually last a lifetime of domestic use.

=== life | Walking: the most underrated exercise | The body
> It needs no equipment, skill or membership. The evidence that regular walking lengthens life and improves health is as solid as anything in exercise science.
# The evidence
Large studies that fitted people with step counters and followed them for years have found a clear pattern. Those who take more steps each day have a lower risk of dying during the study period, and of heart disease in particular. The greatest difference is between the least active people and those who do a moderate amount. Simply moving out of the lowest group, from very few steps to a few thousand more, is associated with a large improvement.
# Where ten thousand came from
The target of ten thousand steps did not come from research. It dates from the 1960s, when a Japanese company sold a pedometer whose name translates roughly as ten thousand steps meter. It was a marketing choice, and a memorable one. The figure is not wrong as an aim, but it is not a threshold. Benefits begin far below it, and people who cannot reach it should not conclude that fewer steps are pointless.
# How many is enough
A study of older American women found that those averaging about 4,400 steps a day had notably lower death rates than those averaging about 2,700, with benefits levelling off at around 7,500. A later analysis combining many studies found the curve flattening at roughly 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for people over sixty and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults. More than that does no harm. The extra gains simply become smaller.
# Pace
Speed adds something. Brisk walking, the pace at which you breathe harder but can still hold a conversation, counts as moderate exercise. For most adults that is around a hundred steps a minute. Half an hour of brisk walking on five days a week meets the standard guideline for physical activity. Studies generally find that faster habitual walkers fare better than slower ones, though total volume appears to matter more than speed.
# Beyond the heart
Walking is weight-bearing, which helps maintain bone strength. It improves the body's handling of blood sugar, and a short walk after a meal has been shown to blunt the rise in blood sugar that follows eating. Regular walkers report better sleep and mood. Walking outdoors, especially in green surroundings, appears to reduce stress. And many people find that walking helps them think, a link noticed by writers and scientists for centuries.
# Building it in
The advantage of walking is that it fits into a day without needing a block of spare time. Get off the bus a stop early. Take the stairs. Walk during phone calls. Park at the far end of the car park. Walk to nearby errands. Take ten minutes after lunch and ten after dinner. These small pieces add up to thousands of steps, and since they become part of the routine, they persist when gym resolutions lapse.
# Making it harder
When ordinary walking becomes easy, there are simple ways to increase the demand. Walk faster for a minute or two at a time. Choose a route with hills. Carry a loaded backpack, which strengthens the legs and trunk considerably. Walk on uneven trails, which challenge balance. Lengthen one walk a week into a proper hike. Walking can be scaled from gentle rehabilitation up to hard training.
# What it does not do
Walking is not a complete programme. It does little to build strength in the upper body, and it will not maintain muscle mass on its own as you age, so it should be paired with some form of resistance training twice a week. For very fit people, it may be too gentle to improve fitness further. For most people most of the time, it is the foundation: safe, free, sustainable and effective.
