=== money | How a mortgage really works | Your money
> The monthly payment never changes, but what it buys changes every month. Seeing the schedule explains why early extra payments are so powerful.
# One payment, two parts
A standard fixed-rate mortgage has a level monthly payment for its whole life. Each payment does two jobs. Part of it pays the interest charged on what you still owe. The rest reduces the amount you owe, called the principal. The split between those two is not fixed. It shifts a little every month, and the pattern of that shift, called amortization, is the most important thing to understand about a home loan.
# Why the early years are mostly interest
Interest is charged on the outstanding balance. At the beginning, the balance is at its largest, so the interest portion is at its largest too. Take a loan of 300,000 dollars over 30 years at 6.5 percent. The payment is about 1,896 dollars a month. In the first month, roughly 1,625 of that is interest and only about 271 reduces the debt. As the balance falls, the interest share shrinks and the principal share grows, slowly at first and then faster.
# The total bill
Over the full 30 years, that borrower makes 360 payments totalling about 683,000 dollars. The house cost them 300,000 in principal and roughly 383,000 in interest. Paying more in interest than the amount borrowed is normal on a long loan at such a rate. This is not a trick by the lender. It is the plain cost of using someone else's money for three decades, and seeing the figure clearly helps you decide how much house to buy.
# What a shorter term does
The same loan over 15 years has a payment of about 2,613 dollars, some 700 more each month. The total interest falls to roughly 170,000 dollars, less than half. Shorter loans also usually carry a slightly lower rate. The trade is between a higher required payment now and a far lower lifetime cost. Whether that is wise depends on how secure your income is, since a payment you are obliged to make is very different from one you choose to make.
# The power of small extra payments
You can capture much of the benefit of a shorter loan without the obligation. Any amount paid beyond the scheduled payment goes entirely to principal, which cuts every future interest charge. Adding 200 dollars a month to the 30-year example would pay it off about seven years early and save around 100,000 dollars in interest. Extra payments made in the early years do the most, because that principal would otherwise have accrued interest for decades. Check that your lender applies extra money to principal.
# Fixed and adjustable
With a fixed rate, the interest rate and payment are locked for the life of the loan. With an adjustable rate, the rate is fixed for an initial period and then moves with market rates. Adjustable loans start cheaper because the borrower is taking on the risk that rates will rise. They can suit someone certain to sell or refinance within a few years. For anyone staying long term, a payment that might jump is a risk that should be priced with care.
# What is not in the payment
The principal and interest figure is not your full housing cost. Property taxes and home insurance are often collected by the lender alongside it and held in an escrow account. Borrowers with a small down payment usually pay mortgage insurance as well. Then there is maintenance, which many planners estimate at around one percent of the home's value each year, averaged over time. A budget built on principal and interest alone will come up short.
# Questions before signing
Ask for the amortization schedule and read the first and last pages. Ask what the total interest will be if you make every payment as scheduled. Ask whether there is any penalty for paying early. Compare the annual percentage rate, which includes fees, not just the headline interest rate. And compare several lenders, because a difference of a quarter of a percentage point, small as it sounds, is worth many thousands of dollars over the life of a typical loan.

=== money | Credit scores: what the number measures | Your money
> A three-digit number decides what you pay to borrow. It follows a small set of rules, and most of them reward doing boring things for a long time.
# What it is
A credit score is a number that estimates how likely you are to repay borrowed money on time. In the United States the best-known version, the FICO score, runs from 300 to 850. Lenders use it to decide whether to approve a loan and at what interest rate. Landlords, insurers and some employers look at credit records too. The score is calculated from the information in your credit report, a record kept by credit bureaus of your accounts and payment history.
# The five ingredients
FICO publishes the rough weights. Payment history counts for about 35 percent: whether you have paid on time. Amounts owed counts for about 30 percent, mainly how much of your available credit you are using. Length of credit history is about 15 percent. New credit, meaning recent applications, is about 10 percent. The mix of account types, such as cards and instalment loans, is the last 10 percent. Income, savings and employment are not part of the score at all.
# Payment history
Nothing matters more than paying every bill on time. A single payment reported 30 days late can cut a good score substantially, and the mark stays on the report for seven years, though its effect fades. The simplest protection is to set every account to pay at least the minimum automatically. You can still pay more by hand. The automatic payment is a safety net against the forgotten bill, which is how most damage to otherwise good records happens.
# Utilization
The second factor is the share of your credit limits that you are using. Someone with a 10,000 dollar total limit and a 3,000 dollar balance has a utilization of 30 percent. Lower is better, and people with the highest scores typically use less than a tenth. The balance that counts is usually the one on your statement date, so even people who pay in full every month can show high utilization. Paying before the statement closes brings the reported figure down.
# Age and new accounts
A long record of well-managed accounts helps, which is why closing your oldest card can hurt. Each application for new credit generally triggers a hard inquiry, which lowers the score by a few points for a time. Several applications in quick succession look like distress. An exception is rate shopping: multiple inquiries for a mortgage or car loan within a short window are normally counted as one, so comparing lenders does not punish you.
# Myths
Checking your own score or report does not lower it. That is a soft inquiry and is invisible to lenders. Carrying a balance and paying interest does not help. The score rewards using credit and paying it off, not paying interest, so paying in full each month is ideal. And you do not have one score. There are several scoring models and three major bureaus whose data can differ, so the number varies depending on who is asking.
# Check the report
Errors on credit reports are common enough that checking is worthwhile. In the United States, each of the three major bureaus must provide a free copy of your report, available through the official site, annualcreditreport dot com. Look for accounts you do not recognize, late payments you believe were on time and wrong balances. Disputes are filed with the bureau, which must investigate. An unfamiliar account can also be the first sign of identity theft.
# Building or repairing
For someone starting out, a secured card, backed by a deposit, or being added as an authorized user on a relative's well-managed account can establish a record. For repair, the recipe is the same as the ingredients list: pay everything on time, bring balances down, avoid new applications and wait. There is no legitimate shortcut. Companies that promise to erase accurate negative information for a fee are selling something that time does for free.

=== money | Tax brackets: why a raise never costs you money | Your money
> A widespread fear holds that earning more can push you into a higher bracket and leave you with less. With a marginal system, that is not how it works.
# The misunderstanding
Many people believe that if a raise moves them into a higher tax bracket, all of their income is then taxed at the higher rate. Some have turned down overtime because of it. In a marginal tax system, which is what the United States and most other countries use for income tax, this is simply wrong. The higher rate applies only to the dollars above the threshold. Everything below it is taxed exactly as before.
# Buckets, not a single rate
Picture your income filling a row of buckets, one after another. The first bucket is taxed at the lowest rate. When it is full, income spills into the second, taxed at the next rate, and so on. Take an invented system to keep the numbers simple: 10 percent on the first 10,000 dollars, 20 percent on the next 30,000, and 30 percent on everything above 40,000. Each rate touches only the money inside its own bucket.
# A worked example
Someone earning 50,000 dollars under that invented system pays 10 percent of the first 10,000, which is 1,000. Then 20 percent of the next 30,000, which is 6,000. Then 30 percent of the final 10,000, which is 3,000. The total is 10,000 dollars. This person is in the 30 percent bracket, yet their tax is 20 percent of their income. Being in a bracket describes only the rate on your last dollars.
# Marginal and effective
Two rates describe every taxpayer. The marginal rate is the rate on the next dollar you earn, 30 percent in the example. The effective rate is total tax divided by total income, 20 percent in the example. They answer different questions. Use the marginal rate to judge a decision at the edge: the value of a deduction, or what you keep from overtime. Use the effective rate to understand what share of your income actually goes to tax.
# What happens with a raise
Suppose the same person gets a raise of 5,000 dollars. All of it lands in the top bucket and is taxed at 30 percent, so they pay 1,500 more in tax and keep 3,500. Their take-home pay has gone up. No amount of extra income, under a purely marginal system, can reduce what you keep. Had they been just below the 40,000 threshold, the dollars above it would be taxed at 30 percent and the dollars below would be untouched.
# Deductions and credits
The same logic explains two terms people often confuse. A deduction reduces the income that is taxed, so it saves you its amount multiplied by your marginal rate. A 1,000 dollar deduction is worth 300 dollars to someone in a 30 percent bracket and 100 dollars to someone in a 10 percent bracket. A credit reduces the tax itself, dollar for dollar. A 1,000 dollar credit is worth 1,000 dollars to everyone who owes at least that much.
# The real exceptions
There are cases where earning more does leave someone worse off, but they come from outside the bracket system. Certain benefits and subsidies are withdrawn once income passes a set level, sometimes all at once. A person just over such a line can lose more in support than they gained in pay. These are called benefit cliffs, and they are a genuine problem for households near them. The cause is the design of those programmes, not the tax brackets.
# Why this matters
Understanding marginal rates changes practical decisions. You should not refuse a raise, a bonus or extra hours on tax grounds. Contributions to tax-deferred retirement accounts are most valuable when your marginal rate is high. And when you hear a proposal to raise the top rate, you can work out what it means: it changes the tax on income above the top threshold only. Real brackets and thresholds change from year to year, so check the current figures for your own country.

=== money | Reading an income statement | How business works
> A company's income statement tells the story of a year in a dozen lines. Read from the top down, it shows where the money came from and where each slice went.
# What the statement is
An income statement, also called a profit and loss statement, reports what a business earned and spent over a period, usually a quarter or a year. It starts with sales and subtracts costs in a fixed order until it arrives at profit. The top line is revenue and the bottom line is net income, which is where the everyday phrases top line and bottom line come from. Reading it is a matter of following the subtraction.
# Revenue
Revenue is the total value of goods and services sold in the period. It is recorded when the sale is earned, not necessarily when cash arrives, so a company can report revenue for goods a customer has not yet paid for. Growth in revenue is the first thing analysts look at, but it says nothing about whether the sales were profitable. A business can grow its top line quickly while losing more money on every additional sale.
# Cost of goods sold and gross profit
The first subtraction is the direct cost of what was sold: materials, factory labour, or for a retailer the price paid to suppliers. Revenue minus this cost is gross profit. Expressed as a percentage of revenue, it is the gross margin. A software firm may keep 80 cents of each dollar of sales at this stage. A supermarket may keep 25. Gross margin shows how much room a business has to pay for everything else.
# Operating expenses
Next come the costs of running the company that are not tied to individual units: salaries of sales and office staff, marketing, rent, research and development. Subtract them from gross profit and you have operating income, the profit from the core business. Many analysts consider it the most informative line, because it leaves out financing and tax, which depend on how the company is funded and where it is based, not on how good the business is.
# Below the operating line
After operating income, the statement deducts interest paid on debt and adds any interest earned, then deducts income tax. One-off items also appear here, such as the gain from selling a division or the cost of a lawsuit. What remains is net income. Divide it by the number of shares and you get earnings per share, the figure most quoted in the financial press when a company reports its results.
# A tiny example
Imagine a bakery with sales of 500,000 dollars. Flour, butter and bakers' wages cost 200,000, leaving a gross profit of 300,000, a 60 percent gross margin. Rent, counter staff, marketing and equipment wear cost 230,000, leaving operating income of 70,000. Interest on the oven loan takes 10,000 and tax takes 15,000. Net income is 45,000 dollars, a net margin of 9 percent. Nine cents of every dollar a customer hands over ends up as profit.
# What to compare
A single statement means little alone. Compare each line with the same period a year earlier to see direction. Compare margins with those of competitors to see relative strength. Look at whether costs are growing faster than revenue. A company whose sales rise 10 percent while its operating expenses rise 20 is heading the wrong way, whatever the headline says. Trends across several years reveal far more than any one period.
# What it cannot tell you
The income statement has blind spots. Profit is an accounting figure built on estimates, such as how quickly equipment wears out, and it is not the same as cash. A profitable company can still run out of money. The statement also shows nothing about what the company owns or owes. For those you need its two companions, the cash flow statement and the balance sheet. The three are designed to be read together.

=== money | The balance sheet: what a company owns and owes | How business works
> If the income statement is a film of the year, the balance sheet is a photograph of one day. It rests on a single equation that always holds.
# The equation
Every balance sheet obeys one rule: assets equal liabilities plus equity. Assets are what the company owns or controls. Liabilities are what it owes to others. Equity is what is left for the owners. The two sides must be equal because everything the company has was paid for with either borrowed money or the owners' money. If the numbers do not balance, an error has been made somewhere, which is the origin of the name.
# A snapshot
Unlike the income statement, which covers a period, the balance sheet describes a single moment, usually the last day of a quarter or year. It can look quite different a week later. A retailer's balance sheet just before the holidays, heavy with stock bought on credit, differs from the one in January after the stock has been sold and the suppliers paid. Always note the date at the top before drawing conclusions.
# Assets
Assets are listed roughly in order of how quickly they can be turned into cash. Current assets come first: cash itself, money owed by customers, called receivables, and inventory. These are expected to convert to cash within a year. Then come long-term assets: buildings, machinery and equipment, shown at cost minus accumulated wear. Finally there are intangible assets such as patents, and goodwill, which arises when one company buys another for more than its recorded value.
# Liabilities
Liabilities are ordered the same way. Current liabilities are due within a year: bills from suppliers, wages owed, taxes due and the portion of loans repayable soon. Long-term liabilities include bonds, bank loans and lease obligations stretching further out. A liability is not a sign of trouble in itself. Buying stock from suppliers on 60 days' credit is a liability and also a cheap source of financing. The question is whether the debts fit the company's ability to pay.
# Equity
Equity is the residual: assets minus liabilities. It has two main sources. One is money that shareholders put in when shares were issued. The other is retained earnings, the accumulated profits that the company kept instead of paying out. Equity is also called book value. It is rarely close to what the company is worth on the stock market, since the market looks at future profits and the balance sheet records mostly historical costs.
# Can it pay its bills
The first test of health is short-term. Compare current assets with current liabilities. If a company has 2 million dollars of current assets and 1 million of current liabilities, the ratio is 2, a comfortable cushion. A ratio below 1 means that bills coming due exceed the resources on hand to pay them. Some businesses, such as supermarkets that collect cash before paying suppliers, run safely below 1. For most, it is a warning.
# How much debt
The second test is long-term. Compare total debt with equity. A company funded mostly by debt earns bigger returns for its owners in good times and is far more fragile in bad ones, because interest must be paid whether or not profits arrive. What counts as too much depends on the industry. Utilities with steady income carry heavy debt safely. A technology firm with unpredictable sales carrying the same load would be taking a serious risk.
# Reading it with the others
The three financial statements are linked. Net income from the income statement flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet. The change in the cash line between two balance sheets is explained, item by item, by the cash flow statement. Read alone, the balance sheet shows strength at a moment. Read alongside the others across several years, it shows whether that strength is being built up or drawn down, which is the question that matters.

=== money | Profit is not cash | How business works
> Businesses rarely die from lack of profit. They die when the cash runs out. The difference between the two is the most practical idea in accounting.
# Two different measurements
Profit is revenue minus expenses, measured by accounting rules that record a sale when it is earned and a cost when it is incurred. Cash is the money actually in the bank. The two can diverge widely and for long periods. A company can report a healthy profit and be weeks from being unable to pay its staff. Another can report losses for years while its bank balance grows. Understanding why is essential for anyone who runs or invests in a business.
# Sales that have not been collected
The first gap is timing of payment. A firm delivers 100,000 dollars of goods in March and sends an invoice due in 60 days. The sale counts as revenue in March. The cash arrives in May, if the customer pays on time. Meanwhile the firm has already paid for materials and wages. On paper, March was profitable. In the bank, March was a large outflow. Money owed by customers is called accounts receivable.
# Stock on the shelf
Inventory creates the second gap. Goods bought or made but not yet sold have cost cash, yet they do not appear as an expense until the sale happens. A shop that spends 50,000 dollars stocking up for the season has 50,000 less in the bank and no effect on profit. If the goods sell slowly, cash stays trapped on the shelves. Businesses that fail often have warehouses full of things nobody wanted in time.
# Depreciation
The third gap runs the other way. When a company buys a machine for 100,000 dollars that will last ten years, accounting spreads the cost, charging perhaps 10,000 a year as depreciation. The cash went out in full on the day of purchase. In later years, profit is reduced by a depreciation charge that involves no cash at all. This is why a company with heavy equipment can show modest profits while generating plenty of cash, at least until the machines need replacing.
# Growth eats cash
Put the first two gaps together and you reach a counterintuitive result: fast growth can kill a profitable company. Each new order requires materials and labour to be paid for now, while the customer pays later. The faster sales grow, the more cash is tied up in receivables and inventory. A firm doubling its sales every year may need outside funding precisely because it is succeeding. Many founders discover this at the worst possible moment.
# The cash flow statement
Accountants reconcile the two measures in a third financial statement. It has three sections. Cash from operations starts with net income and adjusts for the gaps described above. Cash from investing records spending on equipment and acquisitions. Cash from financing records borrowing, repayments, share issues and dividends. The three together explain exactly why the bank balance changed over the period. Experienced analysts often read this statement before the others.
# Free cash flow
One figure derived from it gets special attention. Free cash flow is cash from operations minus the spending on equipment needed to keep the business running. It is the money genuinely available to repay debt, pay dividends or expand. Because it is harder to flatter with accounting choices than net income is, many investors treat it as the truest measure of what a business produces. A company whose profits consistently exceed its free cash flow deserves a closer look.
# For a small business
The lesson for anyone running a small firm is to watch the bank balance as closely as the profit. Forecast cash week by week for the next three months. Invoice promptly and chase late payers. Negotiate longer terms with suppliers and shorter ones with customers. Keep a reserve or a line of credit arranged before you need it. A profitable order that would leave you unable to make payroll in the meantime is an order to restructure or refuse.
