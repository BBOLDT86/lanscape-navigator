=== mind | How a habit actually forms | Habits and attention
> A habit is a behaviour your brain has stopped deciding about. Understanding the loop behind it tells you where to push if you want to build one or break one.
# Decisions you no longer make
A large share of what you do each day is not decided. You do not weigh whether to brush your teeth or which way to turn out of your street. Researchers who asked people to log their actions hour by hour have found that around four in ten were things they did almost every day in the same setting. This is efficiency, not laziness. The brain hands repeated behaviour to circuits that run it automatically, which frees attention for things that are new or difficult.
# Cue, routine, reward
The standard description of a habit has three parts. A cue is the trigger: a time, a place, a feeling, a preceding action. The routine is the behaviour itself. The reward is whatever the brain gets out of it, which can be a taste, a sense of relief or simply the end of an itch. Repeat the sequence enough and the cue alone starts the routine with no deliberation. Experiments at MIT watching the brains of rats learning a maze showed the activity shrinking over time to a burst at the start and a burst at the end.
# How long it takes
The popular claim that a habit takes 21 days has no good evidence behind it. A study at University College London followed people adopting a new daily behaviour, such as eating fruit with lunch or doing sit-ups after coffee. The time until it felt automatic averaged about 66 days, and individuals ranged from 18 days to 254. Simple actions settled faster than demanding ones. Missing a single day did not noticeably set anyone back, which is worth remembering the first time you slip.
# Context does the work
Habits are tied to settings more than to intentions. People who move house or change jobs often find old habits loosening, because the cues have disappeared. The same fact can be used on purpose. If you want to practise guitar, leave it on a stand in the living room, not in its case in a cupboard. If you want to stop snacking, do not keep the snacks on the counter. Changing what is in front of you is usually more effective than promising yourself to try harder.
# Start smaller than seems reasonable
The reliable way to install a behaviour is to make the first version almost insultingly easy. Two minutes of stretching. One page. One push-up. The point of the early weeks is not the result. It is to make the link between the cue and the action automatic. Once showing up no longer takes a decision, extending the routine is easy. People who begin with an ambitious version usually manage it until the first bad day and then stop altogether.
# Replacing beats removing
A cue that has triggered a routine for years does not go quiet because you have decided it should. Breaking a habit by willpower alone leaves the cue and the craving in place with nothing to do. What tends to work better is keeping the cue and the reward and swapping in a different routine. If an afternoon slump sends you to the vending machine and what you really get from the trip is a break and a chat, a walk to a colleague's desk may serve the same need.
# What to try this week
Pick one small behaviour and attach it to something you already do every day without fail. After I pour my coffee, I will write one sentence in my notebook. After I park the car, I will take three slow breaths. Keep the new action under two minutes. Do not add a second habit until the first requires no thought. It is a slow method, and its advantage over the fast ones is that people are still doing it a year later.

=== mind | If-then plans: the smallest trick that works | Habits and attention
> Good intentions fail at a predictable point: the moment of action. One sentence, written in advance, moves the decision out of that moment.
# The gap between meaning to and doing
Most people who fail to exercise, save or study intended to do it. Researchers call this the intention to behaviour gap. Wanting something, even strongly, turns out to predict what people actually do far less well than you might expect. The trouble is rarely motivation. It is that when the moment arrives, you are tired or distracted, the opportunity passes unnoticed, or something more appealing is in front of you. Goals are set in calm conditions and carried out in messy ones.
# The format
The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer proposed a remedy in the 1990s and called it an implementation intention. Instead of a goal such as I will exercise more, you write a plan in the form: if situation X arises, then I will do Y. If it is Monday, Wednesday or Friday at 7 in the morning, then I will put on my running shoes and go out the door. The plan names a specific cue and a specific first action. Nothing more is needed.
# The evidence
The results have been repeated many times. In one British study, people were asked to exercise at least once in the following week. A group given only information did so about 38 percent of the time. A group given extra motivational material managed about 35 percent. A third group also wrote down when and where they would exercise, and 91 percent followed through. A later review covering 94 separate studies found a consistent and sizeable benefit across health, study and work goals.
# Why it works
Deciding in advance does two things. It makes the cue stand out, so that when 7 o'clock comes or you pass the gym, you notice it. And it links the cue to the action so that the response begins without a fresh debate. The decision has already been made by a calmer version of you. In the moment there is nothing to negotiate. The effect resembles a habit installed by a single act of planning instead of by months of repetition.
# Plans for obstacles
The format is even more useful for the moments that usually derail you. If I feel like skipping the session, then I will do just the first five minutes. If someone offers me a drink, then I will ask for sparkling water. If I open my phone during work, then I will close it and write down what I was avoiding. Identifying your most likely point of failure and writing a response to it in advance is sometimes called coping planning, and studies that combine it with an ordinary plan generally find the pair works better than either alone.
# Where it falls short
An if-then plan is not a spell. It works best when you genuinely hold the goal and when the cue is concrete: a time, a place or a preceding action. Vague cues such as when I have a free moment do little. Too many plans at once dilute each other. And a plan cannot overcome a goal you do not actually want, which is worth noticing. If you repeatedly ignore a well-made plan, the honest question may be whether the goal is really yours.
# Write one now
Choose one thing you have been meaning to do. Write a single sentence that states the day, the time, the place and the first physical action. Put it somewhere you will see it. Then write one more sentence for the obstacle most likely to stop you. That is the whole technique. It takes about a minute, costs nothing, and has better evidence behind it than most of what is sold as self-improvement.

=== mind | Attention is the scarce thing | Habits and attention
> You cannot do two demanding things at once, and switching between them costs more than it feels like. Protecting attention is mostly a matter of design.
# Multitasking is switching
When you believe you are doing two mentally demanding things at once, you are in fact alternating between them. Each switch has a cost. The brain must set aside one set of rules and load another. Laboratory tasks show people becoming both slower and more error-prone when they alternate, compared with finishing one task and then the other. The loss on each switch is a fraction of a second, but the fractions accumulate, and the errors rise with the complexity of the work.
# The residue
The business researcher Sophie Leroy gave the lingering cost a name: attention residue. When you leave a task unfinished and turn to another, part of your mind stays with the first. In her experiments, people who had been interrupted performed worse on the next task than those allowed to finish. The residue is thickest when the abandoned task was absorbing and incomplete. This is why a quick glance at email in the middle of hard thinking is never as quick as it looks.
# The price of an interruption
Field studies of office workers, including well-known work by Gloria Mark, found that people switched what they were doing every few minutes, and that after a real interruption it took on average over twenty minutes to get back to the original task. Often they passed through two other tasks on the way. About half of the interruptions were self-inflicted. Nobody had knocked or called. The worker had simply reached for something else.
# Why you reach for the phone
Self-interruption is not a failure of character. Difficult thinking is mildly unpleasant, and the brain looks for relief. A phone offers a reliable small reward within a second or two. Notifications make it worse by supplying a cue you did not ask for. One much-discussed study found that having your own phone visible on the desk, face down and silent, slightly reduced performance on demanding tasks, though attempts to repeat the result have been mixed. Either way, a phone within reach is a cue you have to keep refusing.
# Design beats discipline
Because the pull is constant, resisting it by willpower is exhausting and unreliable. Removing the cue works better. Put the phone in another room. Close the email program entirely instead of minimizing it. Turn off every notification that is not from a person who needs you now. Work in full-screen mode. These changes feel trivial and have large effects, because they remove dozens of small decisions an hour. You stop resisting temptation because the temptation is not there.
# Work in blocks
The other half is scheduling. Set aside a block of time, an hour or ninety minutes, for one demanding task and nothing else. Decide beforehand exactly what you will work on. Batch email and messages into a few set times instead of answering as they arrive. Many people find that two or three protected blocks produce more than an entire day of fragmented effort. Few people can sustain more than about four hours of truly concentrated work in a day, so guard those hours.
# Rest is part of it
Sustained focus needs recovery. Short breaks that actually rest the mind, such as a walk, looking out of a window or a conversation, restore concentration better than breaks spent scrolling, which keep demanding attention. Sleep matters more than any technique. A tired brain switches more, resists less and makes more mistakes. If you want to concentrate better tomorrow, the most effective step may be the unglamorous one of going to bed on time tonight.

=== mind | Spaced repetition: remembering on purpose | Learning and memory
> Forgetting follows a curve. Review something just as it is about to slip and you flatten the curve, with a fraction of the effort cramming takes.
# The forgetting curve
In the 1880s the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of meaningless syllables and tested himself at intervals. He found that forgetting is steep at first and then levels off. Much of the list was gone within an hour, more within a day, and what survived a few days tended to last. His curve has been reproduced many times since. It explains an experience everyone has had: studying something until it feels solid and finding it mostly gone a week later.
# Each review slows the loss
Ebbinghaus also found the remedy. When he relearned a list he had partly forgotten, it took less time than the first learning, and afterwards he forgot it more slowly. Every well-timed review makes the memory more durable, so the gap before the next review can be longer. A fact first reviewed after a day might next be reviewed after three days, then a week, then a month, then several months. A handful of brief reviews can hold something for years.
# Spacing beats massing
This is called the spacing effect, and it is among the most reliably demonstrated findings in the study of learning. For the same total study time, sessions spread out over days or weeks produce far better long-term retention than one concentrated session. Cramming does work for an exam the next morning. It simply produces knowledge that evaporates soon after. If you need to know something next year, studying it in one sitting is close to the least efficient method available.
# Why difficulty helps
The best moment to review is when recall has become slightly hard but is still possible. That effort of pulling the fact back is what strengthens it. Reviewing too soon feels pleasant and does little, because the answer is still sitting in short-term memory. This is counterintuitive, since easy review feels productive. Psychologists call such obstacles desirable difficulties. Learning that feels smooth is often shallow, and learning that feels like a struggle is often the kind that lasts.
# Flashcard systems
In the 1970s the German science writer Sebastian Leitner described a simple system using a row of boxes. New cards start in the first box and are reviewed daily. A card you answer correctly moves to the next box, which is reviewed less often. A card you miss returns to the first. Modern flashcard programs such as Anki automate the same idea, calculating a separate schedule for every card. Medical students and language learners use them to hold tens of thousands of facts.
# What it suits
Spaced repetition is built for discrete things you must simply know: vocabulary, anatomy, formulas, dates, legal definitions, the names of people you met. It is less suited to understanding an argument or acquiring a skill, though breaking a subject into good questions often forces understanding along the way. Write cards in your own words, keep each to a single fact, and prefer questions that require recall over ones you can answer by recognition. If you find yourself reciting a whole paragraph to answer a card, it is really several cards and should be split.
# A plan without software
You can use the principle with nothing but a calendar. After learning something, review it the next day, then three days later, then after a week, then after a month. Each review can be short, as long as you try to recall before you look. For a course, this means beginning revision in the first week and returning to old material regularly instead of leaving it all for the end. The total time is lower than cramming and the result is knowledge you keep. Language learners who review a little every day routinely outpace those who study for hours once a week.

=== mind | Test yourself: retrieval beats rereading | Learning and memory
> The most common study method, reading it again, is one of the weakest. Pulling information out of your head teaches you more than putting it back in.
# The illusion of knowing
Rereading a chapter feels effective. The words are familiar, the ideas flow and you finish with a sense of mastery. That feeling is produced by recognition, which is much easier than recall. Recognizing a sentence you have seen before tells you almost nothing about whether you could produce the idea unprompted. Students consistently predict that rereading will serve them best, and experiments consistently show they are wrong. Familiarity is being mistaken for knowledge. Highlighting has the same weakness. It feels like work and leaves you with a colourful page and very little that you could repeat without it.
# The key experiment
In a well-known study by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke, students read a short passage. Some then read it again. Others closed the text and wrote down everything they could remember. Tested after five minutes, the rereaders did slightly better. Tested a week later, the result reversed. Those who had practised recalling remembered substantially more, around 60 percent of the material against about 40 percent for repeated study in one version of the experiment. The rereaders had been more confident.
# Why recall strengthens memory
Retrieving a memory is not like reading a file. The act changes the memory, strengthening the path that leads to it and tying it to more cues. Each successful retrieval makes the next one easier. Reading the answer does not exercise that path at all. It also explains why a failed attempt still helps. Trying to recall something, failing, and then checking the answer produces better learning than being shown the answer at once, because the attempt prepared the ground.
# Feedback matters
Testing yourself works best when you find out whether you were right. Without feedback you may rehearse an error and make it stronger. The fix is easy: check your answer against the source after each attempt, and pay particular attention to anything you got wrong with confidence. Those are the most valuable items. Mistakes made confidently and then corrected tend to be remembered especially well, a finding researchers call the hypercorrection effect.
# Ways to do it
You do not need formal tests. Close the book and write a summary from memory. Explain the idea aloud to an imaginary listener. Turn headings into questions and answer them before reading the section. Use flashcards, and say the answer before turning the card. At the end of a lecture or meeting, spend two minutes writing the main points without looking at your notes. Each of these replaces passive exposure with an act of recall. None needs special materials, and each takes only a few minutes at the end of a study session or a working day.
# Explain it simply
A demanding form of retrieval is trying to teach the idea in plain language, without jargon, as if to a twelve-year-old. Wherever you find yourself falling back on a technical term or waving your hands, you have found the gap in your own understanding. Go back to the source for that part alone, then try again. The method is often associated with the physicist Richard Feynman, who had a reputation for being able to explain difficult things clearly.
# The cost and the payoff
Self-testing is less pleasant than rereading. It exposes what you do not know, and it is slower per page. This is why most people avoid it, and why it gives an advantage to those who do not. The practical rule is to spend more of your study time with the book closed than open. Read once, carefully. Then spend the remaining time trying to produce the material from memory, checking, and trying again a few days later. It will feel less comfortable and you will learn more.

=== mind | Procrastination is not about time | Habits and attention
> Putting things off looks like a planning problem. The research points somewhere else: it is a way of managing a bad feeling, and that changes the fix.
# A mood problem in disguise
People who procrastinate usually know exactly what they should be doing and how long they have. Better calendars rarely help. Psychologists who study the behaviour, among them Timothy Pychyl and Fuschia Sirois, describe it as a problem of emotion regulation. The task brings up something unpleasant, such as boredom, anxiety, self-doubt or resentment. Avoiding the task makes the feeling go away at once. The relief is the reward, and it teaches you to avoid again next time. Seen this way, procrastination is a habit like any other, with a cue, a routine and a payoff.
# The present self wins
The trade is lopsided because the relief is immediate and the cost is delayed. Humans weigh the present far more heavily than the future, a tendency economists call present bias. The person who will face the deadline next week feels like someone else, a stranger who can be handed the problem. Brain imaging studies have found that people thinking about their future selves show patterns closer to those seen when thinking about another person than about themselves.
# What makes a task avoidable
Certain features make a task more likely to be put off. It is vague, so you do not know where to start. It is large, with no visible end. It is boring, or it is difficult. It matters a great deal, which raises the stakes of doing it badly. Or it was imposed by someone else. Notice that importance makes procrastination worse, not better. This is why people clean the kitchen thoroughly on the day they are supposed to begin a report that could affect their career.
# Shrink the first step
The most dependable tactic is to reduce the task until starting takes no courage. Do not write the report. Open the document and write a bad first sentence. Do not do your taxes. Find last year's return and put it on the table. The unpleasant feeling is concentrated at the threshold. Once you are a few minutes in, the task is almost always less awful than you expected, and continuing is far easier than beginning was.
# Promise only five minutes
A related device is the five-minute bargain. Tell yourself you will work on the thing for five minutes and are then free to stop. Mean it. Most of the time you will carry on, because the hard part was starting. On the days you do stop, you have still done five minutes and kept the promise, which protects your trust in your own agreements. That trust is worth more than any single session.
# Be less hard on yourself
It seems obvious that harsh self-criticism should cure procrastination. Studies suggest the opposite. In research on students, those who forgave themselves for putting off studying before one exam procrastinated less before the next. Self-blame adds another bad feeling to the task, and bad feelings are what you were avoiding in the first place. Treating a lapse as information, not as a verdict on your character, makes it easier to return to the work.
# Change the surroundings
Finally, make avoiding harder and starting easier. Decide the night before what the first task will be and leave it open on your desk. Work somewhere the usual escapes are out of reach. Tell someone what you will finish by when, since an expectation from another person is a strong motivator. None of this removes the uncomfortable feeling. It only lowers the barrier enough that you start anyway, and starting is nearly always the cure. Expect to need the trick again tomorrow. That is normal.
