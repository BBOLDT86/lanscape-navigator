=== poker | Pot odds: the price of a call | The math you need
> Every call is a purchase. Pot odds tell you the price, and whether the hand you hold is worth paying it.
# A call is a bet at fixed odds
When an opponent bets, the pot is offering you a wager. You put in a known amount for the chance to win what is already out there. Pot odds are just that ratio: the money you can win compared with the money you must pay. Suppose the pot holds 100 dollars and your opponent bets 50. There is now 150 to win and it costs you 50 to try. You are getting 150 to 50, or 3 to 1. Nothing about your cards has entered the picture yet. This is purely the price.
# Turn the price into a percentage
Odds of 3 to 1 mean that out of four tries, you can lose three and win one and break even. So you need to win one time in four, or 25 percent. The quick formula is the size of your call divided by the total pot after you call. Here that is 50 divided by 200, which is 25 percent. If your hand will win more often than that, calling makes money over time. If it will win less often, calling loses money, no matter how the hand feels.
# Memorize four bet sizes
You do not need to calculate at the table if you learn the common cases. Facing a bet of one third of the pot, you need 20 percent. Facing half the pot, 25 percent. Facing two thirds, about 29 percent. Facing a bet the full size of the pot, 33 percent. A bet of twice the pot demands 40 percent. Notice how gently the requirement rises. Even a huge overbet never asks you to be a favourite. It only asks you to win often enough to pay for the times you lose.
# A worked example with a draw
You hold two hearts and by the turn the board shows two more. One card is left to come, and nine hearts remain among the 46 cards you cannot see. You will complete the flush about 19.6 percent of the time on that last card. Your opponent bets 50 into 100, so you need 25 percent. A flush draw alone is not enough at that price. Had the bet been 25 into 100, you would need only one sixth, about 16.7 percent, and the call would be correct.
# Why small bets are hard to fold to
Pot odds explain a feeling every player knows: small bets are annoying. A bet of a quarter of the pot offers 5 to 1, so you need to win barely 17 percent of the time. Many weak hands clear that bar. This is why good players do not fold everything to a tiny bet, and why betting small with a strong hand that wants a call is often correct. The size of the bet, as much as the cards, decides how wide the caller can profitably be.
# What pot odds leave out
The basic sum assumes the hand ends after your call. Often it does not. If more cards and more bets are coming, you may win extra money when you hit, which improves the deal, or face another bet when you miss, which worsens it. Those adjustments have their own names, implied odds and reverse implied odds. Pot odds are the starting point: the honest price right now. Skilled players start there and then adjust for what is likely to happen on later streets.
# Make it a habit
Before every call, say the price to yourself: what is in the pot, what does it cost, what percentage is that. Then ask how often your hand wins. Most losing players skip the first half and go straight to hoping. The habit takes a few sessions to become automatic, and it removes the most expensive leak in casual poker, which is calling too often with hands that cannot win enough of the time to justify the price.

=== poker | Outs and the rule of 2 and 4 | The math you need
> Count the cards that save you, multiply by two or by four, and you have your chance of improving, close enough to act on.
# What an out is
An out is any unseen card that turns your hand into the likely winner. If you hold four cards to a flush, there are thirteen cards of that suit in the deck, you can see four, so nine remain. Those nine are your outs. An open-ended straight draw, such as 8 and 9 on a board of 7, 10 and a king, can be completed by any six or any jack: eight outs. A gutshot, needing one specific rank in the middle, has four. Counting outs is the first step in knowing what a draw is worth.
# The shortcut
With one card to come, multiply your outs by two to get the approximate percentage chance of hitting. With two cards to come, on the flop, multiply by four. A flush draw with nine outs has roughly 18 percent on the next card and roughly 36 percent by the river. The true figures are 19.6 percent with one card left and 35 percent with two. The shortcut works because each unseen card is about 2 percent of the deck you cannot see.
# The numbers worth knowing cold
Four outs, a gutshot: about 9 percent on one card and 16.5 percent with two. Six outs, two overcards hoping to pair: about 13 and 24 percent. Eight outs, the open-ended straight draw: about 17 and 31.5 percent. Nine outs, the flush draw: about 19.6 and 35 percent. Fifteen outs, a straight and flush draw combined: about 33 percent on one card and 54 percent with two. That last hand is a favourite over a made pair, which surprises many players the first time they see it.
# Fix the shortcut for big draws
The rule of four overstates large draws. Fifteen outs times four is 60, but the real figure is 54 percent. The correction is simple: when you have more than eight outs, take the number of outs above eight and subtract it from your answer. Fifteen is seven more than eight, and 60 minus 7 is 53, within a point of the truth. For draws of eight outs or fewer the plain rule is accurate enough that no fix is needed.
# Not every out is clean
Some cards that complete your hand also complete a better one for someone else. If you hold a straight draw on a board with two spades, the two cards that make your straight in spades may hand an opponent a flush. If you hope to pair your ace against someone holding ace king, that pair leaves you second best. Experienced players discount tainted outs, counting a doubtful card as half an out or none. Overcounting outs is the most common way a draw gets overvalued.
# Use four only when you see both cards
The rule of four assumes you will see the turn and the river for the price you pay now. That is only true if you or your opponent is all in on the flop. If there is more betting to come, your opponent can charge you again on the turn, and you should compare the bet in front of you with your chance on the next card alone, the rule of two. Using four on the flop when another bet is coming is how players talk themselves into bad calls.
# Put it together with the price
The method is two numbers side by side. Count your clean outs and convert them to a percentage. Work out the percentage the pot is asking for. If the first is bigger, continue. If it is smaller, fold, unless you expect to win a lot more when you hit. With practice the whole comparison takes two or three seconds. It replaces the vague sense that a draw is promising with a figure, and the figure is right far more often than the feeling.

=== poker | Equity: your share of the pot | The math you need
> Before the last card is dealt, nobody owns the pot. Equity is the share that belongs to you on average, and thinking in shares changes how you play.
# A share, not a verdict
Equity is the percentage of the time your hand will win if all the remaining cards are dealt with no more betting. If you would win 60 times in 100, you have 60 percent equity, and 60 percent of the pot is yours in the long-run sense. On any single deal you win everything or nothing. Over thousands of deals the results converge on the share. Thinking this way separates the quality of a decision from the luck of one outcome.
# The match-ups to memorize
A few preflop confrontations come up constantly. A higher pair against a lower pair, such as aces against kings, is roughly an 80 to 20 favourite. A pair against two higher cards, queens against ace king, is close to even, about 55 to 45 for the pair. That is the classic coin flip. A hand that shares a card with a better kicker, ace king against ace queen, is about a 70 to 30 favourite. Two higher cards against two lower ones, ace king against 7 6, is only around 60 to 40.
# Nothing is ever dead before the flop
The striking thing about those numbers is how much the weaker hand keeps. The worst common match-up, an underpair against an overpair, still wins about one time in five. Seven two offsuit, the worst starting hand, wins roughly one time in nine against a pair of aces. This is why all-in pots before the flop swing so wildly and why a single lost coin flip tells you nothing about how you played. The cards to come carry a great deal of the result.
# Equity moves with every card
Your share is recalculated each time a card lands. Ace king against a pair of queens starts at about 45 percent. If the flop comes without an ace or a king, it falls to roughly 24 percent, because you now have six outs and two cards to come. If an ace lands, the positions reverse and the queens are left with two outs, around 8 percent. Strong players track this as the board develops. The hand you started with matters less on each street than the share you hold now.
# Betting is about denying or charging
Once you think in shares, the reason to bet with a good hand becomes clear. An opponent with a flush draw owns about a third of the pot on the flop. If you check and let them see cards for free, they collect that share at no cost. If you bet, they must either pay more than their share is worth or fold and surrender it. Giving free cards to hands with real equity is one of the quietest ways to lose money.
# Equity against a range
You never know your opponent's exact cards, so real equity is measured against the whole set of hands they could hold. Against someone who only raises with aces, kings, queens and ace king, a pair of jacks has only about 36 percent. Against someone raising a wide assortment of hands, the same jacks may have 65 percent. The cards have not changed. The opponent has. That is why the same hand can be a fold against one player and a confident raise against another.
# Practise away from the table
Free equity calculators let you enter two hands, or a hand against a range, and see the shares. Spending twenty minutes entering the situations you were unsure about is the fastest way to build intuition. After a few weeks you stop guessing. You simply know that a pair plus a flush draw is around 50 percent against top pair, that two overcards and a gutshot have about 40 percent, and that a bare gutshot is seldom worth chasing.

=== poker | Expected value: the only score that counts | The math you need
> Results tell you what happened. Expected value tells you whether the decision was right. Poker is won by people who learn to tell the two apart.
# The average result of a decision
Expected value, EV for short, is what a decision earns on average if you could repeat it thousands of times. To find it, multiply each possible outcome by its chance and add them up. Suppose you call 50 dollars to win a pot of 150, and you will win 30 percent of the time. You win 150 three times in ten and lose 50 seven times in ten. That is 45 minus 35, a profit of 10 dollars per call on average. The call is plus EV, even though you lose most of the time.
# Good decisions can lose
In the example above you lose the pot seven times out of ten and the call is still correct. A player who judges by results will decide after a few losses that the call was a mistake and stop making it. That player has just made their game worse. The reverse is equally common. Someone calls with a hopeless hand, hits a miracle card and concludes the play works. Short-term results in poker are loud and mostly noise. EV is the quiet signal beneath them.
# Every option has an EV
At each decision you are comparing options, not judging one. Folding has an EV of exactly zero from that point: you win nothing and lose nothing more. Money already in the pot is gone and does not count. Calling has some EV. Raising has another. The right play is whichever is highest, and sometimes the best available option is still negative, in which case folding at zero wins. Treating chips already invested as a reason to continue is the sunk cost mistake in its purest form.
# Small edges, many times
A single plus EV decision may be worth a dollar or two. That seems trivial until you count how many decisions a regular player makes. Several hundred hands in a live week, tens of thousands a month online. A consistent edge of a fraction of a bet per hand is the whole of a winning player's income. Casinos work the same way with an edge of a few percent on each wager. Nobody gets rich on one hand. They get rich on the same small advantage, repeated without boredom.
# The EV of a bluff
A bluff has two outcomes. Either the opponent folds and you win the pot, or they call and you usually lose your bet. Bet 50 into a pot of 100 with nothing. If they fold 40 percent of the time, you win 100 four times in ten and lose 50 six times in ten: 40 minus 30, a profit of 10 dollars. The break-even point for a half-pot bluff is one fold in three. Bluffing is not courage. It is a calculation about how often the other person lets go.
# Estimate, do not compute
Nobody runs exact sums at the table. What strong players do is estimate the two or three numbers that matter, then lean the right way. Is this call roughly break-even, clearly good or clearly bad? Will this opponent fold more than a third of the time? Rough answers to the right questions beat precise answers to the wrong ones. The precision comes later, in review, when you can check the hard spots with a calculator and correct your instincts for next time.
# Why it protects your mind
EV thinking is also armour. When you know a decision was correct, a bad result stings less, because you understand that you would make the same play again and profit in the long run. Players who lack this frame take every lost pot as evidence of bad luck or personal failure and start playing worse, a slide known as tilt. Keeping score by the quality of decisions, not by the size of tonight's stack, is how professionals stay level through the swings.

=== poker | Implied odds: the money that is not in the pot yet | The math you need
> Sometimes the price of a call is wrong on paper and right in practice, because of what you stand to win later. Here is when that is true and when it is wishful.
# Looking past the current bet
Pot odds measure the money in the middle now. Implied odds add the money you expect to win on later streets if you make your hand. Suppose you face a bet of 20 into 40 with a draw that hits about 18 percent of the time. You need 25 percent, so the call looks wrong. But if your opponent will pay you another 100 when you hit, you are really risking 20 to win 160. That needs only about 11 percent, and the call becomes clearly profitable.
# Set mining is the classic case
Small and medium pocket pairs are the textbook example. You will flop three of a kind or better about 12 percent of the time, roughly one flop in eight and a half. The immediate pot odds before the flop almost never justify a call on that basis. The call makes sense because a flopped set is well hidden and often wins a very large pot. A common guideline is that you and your opponent should each have at least 15 to 20 times the size of the call behind for the play to pay.
# Stack depth decides everything
Implied odds are limited by the smaller stack in the hand. If your opponent has only 60 dollars left and you must call 20, the most you can win later is 60, however perfectly you hit. This is why speculative hands such as small pairs and suited connectors gain value when stacks are deep and lose it when stacks are short. In a tournament with ten big blinds, those hands are nearly worthless. With 200 big blinds in a cash game, they are among the most profitable.
# Hidden hands get paid, obvious ones do not
The value of hitting depends on whether your opponent can see it coming. A set is disguised, since nobody can tell from the board that you hold a pair in your hand. A straight made with connected cards is often disguised too. A flush is the opposite. When the third card of a suit lands, everyone notices, and a careful opponent slows down. So flush draws have weaker implied odds than players assume, and a draw to anything but the highest flush can cost you money when a bigger one is out.
# The opponent matters most
Implied odds are a prediction about another person. Against a player who cannot fold top pair, they are enormous. Against a cautious player who gives up the moment the board turns frightening, they barely exist. Before making a call that depends on future winnings, ask what this particular opponent does with a good but second best hand. If the answer is that they keep betting or keep calling, call. If they shut down, you are paying full price for a prize you will not collect.
# Reverse implied odds
The idea also runs backwards. Some hands tend to win small pots and lose big ones. Hold king jack on a king high flop and you will often win a little when you are ahead and lose a lot when someone holds ace king or king queen. A weak flush draw has the same problem when it gets there against a bigger flush. These are reverse implied odds: the money you expect to lose later when your hand improves to second best. Dominated hands are the main culprits.
# An honest test
Implied odds are the most abused concept in poker, because they can excuse any call. To keep yourself honest, state the amount out loud: how much more must I win, on average, when I hit, for this call to break even? Then ask whether this opponent, with these stacks, on this kind of board, will really pay that. If you cannot say yes with a straight face, the call is not about implied odds. It is about wanting to see another card.

=== poker | Fold equity: winning without the best hand | The math you need
> A bet can win two ways: by being called and holding up, or by making the other player fold. The second way has a value you can estimate.
# Two ways to win
When you check or call, you can only win by showing down the best hand. When you bet or raise, you add a second path: your opponent may fold. Fold equity is the value of that second path, the share of the pot you pick up simply because the other player gives in. It is the reason aggressive poker beats passive poker over time. The passive player needs the best cards. The aggressive player needs either the best cards or an opponent who is not willing to continue.
# How often a bluff must work
The arithmetic for a pure bluff is short. Divide your bet by the total of the pot plus your bet. A bet of half the pot must succeed one time in three. A bet of two thirds of the pot must work 40 percent of the time. A pot-sized bet needs to work half the time. A bet of one third of the pot needs only one fold in four. Smaller bluffs need to work less often but are easier to call, so the two effects pull against each other.
# The semi-bluff adds a third way
A semi-bluff is a bet with a hand that is probably behind now but can improve, such as a flush draw. It wins if the opponent folds, and it can still win if they call and you hit. Take a pot of 100 and a bet of 100. If the opponent folds 40 percent of the time and you have 35 percent equity when called, the play earns about 43 dollars on average. With no equity at all, the same bet would lose 20. The draw turns a losing bluff into a strong play.
# Who you bet into matters
Fold equity is a property of the opponent, not of your cards. Some players call far too much and almost never fold a pair. Against them your fold equity is close to zero, and bluffing is setting money on fire. Others fold too readily when the bet is large or the board is frightening. Against them, hands with no showdown value at all become profitable bets. The first adjustment a thinking player makes at any table is deciding which kind of opponent is in each seat.
# Your image and your story
People fold when the bet is believable. A player who has been caught bluffing twice in the last hour has little fold equity for a while. One who has shown down only strong hands has plenty. The line you take in the hand matters too. A bluff is credible when your earlier actions fit the strong hand you are now representing. A sudden large bet on the river from someone who checked every street before it tells a story that does not add up, and observant opponents call it.
# Short stacks lose it
Fold equity shrinks as your stack does. A raise only frightens someone if it puts meaningful chips at risk. In a tournament, a player with fifteen big blinds who moves all in can still force folds from hands that do not want to gamble. A player with four big blinds cannot, because calling costs so little that almost any hand is priced in. This is why waiting too long to make a move with a dwindling stack is a mistake: you give up the weapon before you use it.
# The takeaway
Every time you consider betting, estimate two numbers. How often does this opponent fold to this bet? How much equity do I have when they do not? If the folds alone make the bet profitable, your cards hardly matter. If they do not, you need real equity to make up the difference. Players who only bet when they have a hand are easy to read and easy to beat. Players who understand fold equity win pots that their cards alone never would.
