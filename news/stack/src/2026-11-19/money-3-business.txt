=== money | Unit economics: does each sale make money | How business works
> Before asking whether a company is profitable, ask whether a single customer is. If the answer is no, growth only makes the hole deeper.
# The smallest unit
Unit economics means looking at the money a business makes or loses on one unit of what it sells. The unit might be one customer, one order, one ride or one subscription month. The question is simple. After paying the costs that come with that single unit, is anything left over? A company that earns money on each unit can grow its way to covering its fixed costs. One that loses on each unit cannot, however fast it grows.
# Contribution margin
Take the price of one unit and subtract the costs that vary with it: materials, packaging, delivery, payment fees, the labour directly involved. What remains is the contribution margin, the amount each sale contributes toward rent, salaries and profit. A meal delivered for 30 dollars that costs 12 in food, 8 in courier pay and 2 in fees contributes 8. If the figure is negative, selling more loses more. The old joke about making it up on volume describes exactly this.
# What a customer costs
Customers are not free to obtain. Advertising, sales staff, discounts and free trials all cost money. Add up everything spent on winning customers in a period and divide by the number won, and you have the customer acquisition cost. If a company spends 90,000 dollars on marketing in a month and gains 1,000 customers, each cost 90 dollars. That sum has to be earned back from the customer's purchases before the relationship shows any profit.
# What a customer is worth
Lifetime value is the total contribution a typical customer generates before they leave. For a subscription priced at 30 dollars a month with a 60 percent margin, each month contributes 18 dollars. If customers stay 15 months on average, lifetime value is 270 dollars. The length of stay comes from the churn rate, the share of customers who cancel each month. With 5 percent monthly churn, the average customer stays about 20 months.
# The ratio
Divide lifetime value by acquisition cost and you get the figure investors ask about first. In the example, 270 divided by 90 is 3. A ratio of about 3 to 1 is a commonly quoted rule of thumb for a healthy business. A ratio near 1 means the company is merely swapping marketing dollars for revenue dollars. A very high ratio can mean the company is underinvesting in growth. The exact target varies by industry, and the direction over time matters most.
# Payback time
A good ratio is not enough if the money takes too long to return. The payback period is how many months of contribution it takes to recover the acquisition cost. Ninety dollars recovered at 18 dollars a month takes five months. During that time the company is out of pocket for every new customer, so faster growth needs more cash. Many subscription businesses aim to recover the cost within a year. Longer than that and growth must be funded by investors or debt.
# Where the estimates go wrong
Every input is an estimate, and the errors usually flatter. Lifetime value assumes customers will stay as long as earlier ones did, though customers won by heavy discounting tend to leave sooner. Acquisition cost often omits salaries and counts customers who would have arrived anyway. Early customers are the easiest to win, so cost per customer generally rises as a company grows. Careful analysis tracks each month's new customers as a separate group to see how they really behave.
# Using it yourself
The same thinking applies to a market stall or a freelance practice. Work out what one sale truly contributes after its direct costs. Work out what you spend, in money and time, to win one customer. Estimate how much a customer brings in over the whole relationship. If the numbers do not work for one customer on paper, they will not work for a thousand. If they do, you know how much you can afford to spend to find the next one.

=== money | Pricing: the fastest lever a business has | How business works
> A small change in price moves profit more than an equal change in sales or costs. Most businesses set their prices with far less thought than the number deserves.
# Why price matters so much
Consider a company with sales of 100 dollars, costs of 90 and a profit of 10. If it raises prices by 1 percent and sells the same quantity, sales become 101 and costs stay at 90. Profit rises to 11, an increase of 10 percent. No other change of 1 percent has that effect. Selling 1 percent more units brings extra costs with it. Because price increases fall straight to the bottom line, price is the most powerful lever in most businesses.
# Cost-plus
The commonest method is to add up costs and apply a markup. A product that costs 20 dollars to make is sold for 30. It is simple, and it guarantees a margin on each sale. Its weakness is that it ignores the customer entirely. The buyer neither knows nor cares what the product cost to make. Cost-plus pricing charges too little when the product is highly valued and too much when it is not. Cost is a floor, not a guide.
# What competitors charge
A second method is to look at rivals and price near them. This keeps a business in line with the market and is sensible for goods that buyers see as interchangeable. The risk is that everyone copies everyone else, and nobody has worked out what the product is actually worth. Matching a competitor's price also means inheriting their mistakes. A company with a genuinely better product that prices at the market rate is giving the difference away.
# Value to the customer
The third approach starts from the customer. What is this worth to the buyer, compared with their next best alternative? A tool that saves a tradesperson five hours a week is worth far more than the metal in it. Value-based pricing sets the price as a share of that benefit. It requires understanding customers well, which is harder than adding up costs. Businesses that do it tend to earn more, and their customers still come out ahead.
# Different buyers, different prices
Customers differ in what they are willing to pay. A single price loses money at both ends: some would have paid more, and some who would have paid less buy nothing. Businesses respond with versions. Airlines sell the same journey at several prices with different conditions. Software comes in basic, standard and premium tiers. Cinemas discount for students and matinees. Offering a few distinct options lets each buyer choose the price that suits them.
# The power of three options
When shown three tiers, many buyers avoid the cheapest and the dearest and choose the middle. Sellers use this deliberately. A premium option priced very high makes the middle one look moderate, even if few people buy the premium. The top tier earns its place by changing how the others are perceived. If you sell a service, offering three clearly different packages often raises the average sale without changing any single price.
# The psychology of the number
Details of presentation shift behaviour. A price of 19.99 is read as noticeably cheaper than 20, because people give most weight to the first digit. A price shown next to a higher crossed-out figure appears to be a bargain. A monthly figure looks smaller than the same amount stated annually. None of this changes what the buyer pays. All of it changes how the price feels, and sellers are well aware of the effects.
# Raising prices
Most small businesses charge too little and fear that any increase will drive customers away. The arithmetic is worth doing. A firm with a 30 percent margin that raises prices by 10 percent can lose a quarter of its customers and still make the same profit, while doing less work. In practice, far fewer usually leave. The sensible approach is to test: raise the price for new customers first, watch what happens, and adjust.

=== money | Moats: why some businesses stay profitable | How business works
> High profits attract competitors, and competition wears profits away. A few companies hold out for decades. What protects them is called a moat.
# The problem of success
In an open market, unusual profits do not last. If a coffee shop on a busy corner is making a fortune, another opens across the street. Prices fall, costs rise and returns drift toward ordinary. Economists consider this the normal state of affairs. The interesting cases are the exceptions: companies that earn high returns year after year without being competed down. The investor Warren Buffett popularized the image of a castle protected by a moat to describe them.
# Network effects
The strongest moat arises when a product becomes more valuable as more people use it. A payment card is useful because many shops accept it, and shops accept it because many customers carry it. A marketplace attracts buyers because sellers are there, and sellers because buyers are. A newcomer with a better product still starts with an empty network, which is worth little. Such businesses tend toward one or two dominant players and are very hard to dislodge.
# Switching costs
A second moat is the trouble customers face in leaving. A company that has run its accounts on one software system for ten years faces retraining, data migration and risk if it changes. A bank customer must move direct debits and salary payments. The product does not have to be the best available. It has to be good enough that switching is not worth the effort. High switching costs allow steady price increases without losing many customers.
# Cost advantages
Some companies can simply produce more cheaply than anyone else. Scale is the usual source. A retailer buying in enormous volumes obtains lower prices from suppliers. A manufacturer with the biggest factory spreads its fixed costs over more units. Others own a unique resource, such as a mine with unusually rich ore or a location nobody else can occupy. A low-cost producer can charge prices at which rivals lose money and still make a profit.
# Intangible assets
Brands, patents and licences form a fourth category. A patent gives a legal monopoly for a limited period, which is why drug makers earn so much on a medicine until the patent expires. A government licence may limit how many firms can operate. A brand is a moat only if it lets the company charge more or sell more than an identical unbranded product would. Being well known is not enough. Many famous brands have no pricing power at all.
# What is not a moat
Several things that look like protection are not. A good product can be copied. Excellent management can leave. Being first to market helps only if it leads to one of the real advantages above. Rapid growth is not a moat, and neither is a large market share won by cutting prices. The test is to ask what would stop a well-funded rival from doing the same thing and taking the customers. If the answer is nothing, there is no moat.
# How to spot one
The evidence shows up in the numbers. A company with a durable advantage earns a high return on the capital invested in it, and has done so for many years, through recessions and changes of management. It can raise prices without losing customers. Its market share is stable. New entrants keep failing. One or two good years prove little. A decade or two of consistently high returns suggests that something structural is protecting the business.
# Moats drain
No advantage is permanent. Technology shifts can make a moat irrelevant. Film photography was protected by scale and brand, and digital cameras removed the need for film altogether. Patents expire. Regulations change. Customers' habits move. A company with a wide moat can also grow complacent and stop improving. Investors therefore ask not only whether a moat exists but whether it is getting wider or narrower, which is harder to judge and matters more.

=== money | Your walk-away point: the heart of any negotiation | Negotiation
> Your power in a negotiation comes from what you will do if no deal is reached. Knowing that precisely matters more than any tactic at the table.
# The alternative sets your strength
Every negotiation has a shadow: what each side does if the talks fail. A job candidate with another offer in hand negotiates from strength. One with no other prospects does not, however skilled. Negotiation researchers at Harvard gave this a name in the early 1980s: the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, usually shortened to BATNA. It is the course of action you will take if you walk away, and it is the true measure of your position.
# Work it out in advance
Before any important negotiation, write down what you will actually do if there is no agreement. Be concrete and realistic. Not that you might find another buyer, but which buyer, at what likely price, after how long. A vague sense that something will turn up is not an alternative. People who skip this step tend either to accept poor deals out of unexamined fear or to reject good ones out of unexamined optimism.
# The reservation point
Your alternative determines your walk-away point, the worst terms you should accept. If you have a firm offer of 60,000 dollars elsewhere, taking this job for less makes sense only if it has other advantages you can name. Decide the number before the conversation, when you are calm. During the negotiation, pressure and the wish to reach agreement will push you to shade it. A figure fixed beforehand is an anchor for your own judgement.
# The other side has one too
The person across the table also has an alternative and a walk-away point. If a seller will accept anything above 200,000 and a buyer will pay anything up to 230,000, any price in between leaves both better off than no deal. That span is called the zone of possible agreement. If the buyer's limit were below the seller's, no amount of skill would produce a deal, and the sensible outcome would be to part politely.
# Estimate theirs
Much of the preparation for a negotiation is working out the other party's alternative. How badly do they need this? What will they do if you leave? How long can they wait? A landlord with an empty flat for three months is in a different position from one with a waiting list. You will rarely know for certain, but an informed estimate tells you how far they can be pushed and guards you against bluffs about other offers.
# Improve your alternative
The most effective move often happens away from the table. If your alternative is weak, strengthen it before you negotiate. Get a second quote. Apply for another job. Find another supplier. Even a modest second option changes your behaviour, because you no longer need this deal. People negotiating without alternatives give off anxiety, and experienced counterparts notice. Time spent improving your fallback is usually worth more than time spent polishing arguments.
# Revealing it
Whether to disclose your alternative depends on its quality. If it is strong, letting the other side know, calmly and without threat, shifts the discussion in your favour. If it is weak, there is no obligation to volunteer it, though you should never lie about it. Inventing a competing offer is risky as well as dishonest. If the other side says to take it, you are left with nothing and a damaged reputation.
# Agreement is not the goal
Negotiators often feel that reaching a deal is success and walking away is failure. The right comparison is between the deal on offer and your alternative. If the offer is better, accept it. If not, decline, and do so without resentment. Remembering this protects against the commonest error in bargaining, which is agreeing to something worse than you could have had elsewhere because the conversation had gone on so long.

=== money | What a share of stock actually is | Markets
> A stock is not a ticker symbol or a line on a chart. It is a legal slice of a real business, and that fact explains how it should be valued.
# A piece of ownership
A share of stock is a unit of ownership in a company. If a firm has issued one million shares and you hold one thousand, you own a thousandth of it: a thousandth of its buildings, its brands, its debts and its future profits. Shareholders elect the board of directors, who appoint the managers. Your liability is limited to what you paid. If the company fails owing billions, creditors cannot come after your house.
# Why companies issue shares
Companies sell shares to raise money without borrowing. A business that needs funds to build a factory can take a loan, which must be repaid with interest, or sell part of itself to investors, who share in whatever results. The first sale of shares to the public is an initial public offering. After that, shares change hands between investors on a stock exchange. The company receives nothing from this later trading. It is one investor selling to another.
# How shareholders are paid
Owners earn a return in two ways. Some companies distribute part of their profits in cash, called dividends, usually every quarter. Others keep their profits and reinvest them, aiming to make the business more valuable, in which case shareholders benefit if the share price rises. Many do both. A company may also buy back its own shares, which leaves each remaining share with a slightly larger claim on the business.
# Price and size
The price of one share says nothing by itself about how big or expensive a company is. A firm with shares at 20 dollars can be far larger than one with shares at 2,000, if it has many more shares outstanding. The figure that matters is market capitalization: share price multiplied by the number of shares. When a company splits each share into several, the price falls in proportion and the value of your holding does not change.
# What sets the price
In the short run, price is set by supply and demand among traders, and it moves with news, mood and rumour. In the long run, it tends to follow the profits the business produces. A share is worth the cash it will return to its owners over its life, adjusted for the wait and the uncertainty. Since the future is unknown, investors disagree, and the price at any moment is the point where buyers and sellers balance.
# Earnings and the multiple
A common yardstick compares the share price with the company's profit per share. A stock priced at 60 dollars with annual earnings of 3 dollars per share trades at 20 times earnings. A high multiple means investors expect profits to grow strongly and are paying in advance for that growth. A low one means they expect little, or see serious risk. The multiple is where the argument about a stock starts, not where it ends.
# Risk and reward
Shareholders stand last in line. If a company fails, lenders and suppliers are paid first, and owners usually receive nothing. In exchange for that risk, shareholders keep everything that is left after the fixed claims are met, with no upper limit. Over the past century, broad baskets of American shares have returned on the order of 10 percent a year before inflation, far more than bonds or cash, with severe falls along the way.
# Think like an owner
The most useful habit is to remember what you hold. When you buy a share, you are buying part of a business from someone who has decided to sell it. Ask what the business does, how it makes money, whether its profits are likely to be larger in ten years and what you are paying for them. People who treat shares as numbers to be traded tend to do worse than those who treat them as ownership of something real.

=== money | Bonds and interest rates: the seesaw | Markets
> A bond is a loan you can sell. Its price moves opposite to interest rates, and understanding why takes one example.
# A tradable loan
When a government or company wants to borrow, it can issue bonds. The buyer of a bond is lending money. In return, the issuer promises to pay a fixed amount of interest, called the coupon, at regular intervals, and to repay the original amount, the face value, on a set date, the maturity. A bond with a face value of 1,000 dollars and a 4 percent coupon pays 40 dollars a year until it matures, then returns the 1,000.
# Why the price changes
Bonds can be sold to other investors before they mature, and the price is whatever a buyer will pay. Suppose you hold that 4 percent bond and interest rates rise, so that new bonds pay 5 percent. Nobody will pay 1,000 dollars for your 40 dollars a year when the same money now buys 50. To sell, you must drop the price until your bond's return matches the new rate. The payments have not changed. The bond's market value has fallen.
# The seesaw
This is the central rule. When interest rates rise, the prices of existing bonds fall. When rates fall, existing bonds become more valuable, because they pay more than new ones, and their prices rise. The two always move in opposite directions. If you hold a bond to maturity and the issuer pays, you receive exactly what was promised, and the price changes along the way do not matter to you. They matter if you need to sell early.
# Yield
A bond's yield is the return a buyer earns at the current price. If the 4 percent bond can be bought for 920 dollars, the buyer collects 40 dollars a year on an outlay of 920, plus an 80 dollar gain when 1,000 is repaid at maturity. The yield is therefore higher than 4 percent. Price down means yield up. News reports saying that bond yields rose are describing a fall in bond prices.
# Longer bonds swing more
The further away a bond's maturity, the more its price reacts to a change in rates. A bond with one year left will soon be repaid and reinvested at the new rate, so its price barely moves. A bond with thirty years left locks its owner into the old rate for decades. As a rough guide, a ten-year bond loses something like 8 percent of its value when rates rise by one percentage point. Investors call this sensitivity duration.
# Will you be repaid
The second risk is default. A government that borrows in its own currency is very unlikely to fail to pay, so its bonds offer the lowest yields. Companies can and do go bankrupt, so their bonds must pay more to attract lenders. Rating agencies grade issuers from the highest quality down to speculative, often called junk. The extra yield on a risky bond is compensation for the chance of loss. It is not free income.
# The yield curve
Plot the yields of government bonds against their maturities and you get the yield curve. Normally it slopes upward, since lenders want more for tying money up longer. Occasionally short-term yields rise above long-term ones, and the curve is said to be inverted. In the United States, such inversions have preceded most recessions of recent decades, which is why the shape gets so much attention, though the timing has varied widely.
# Why hold them
Bonds provide steadier returns than shares and predictable income, and high-quality ones have often held their value when shares fell. They are not risk-free in the short run. In 2022, when central banks raised rates rapidly, broad American bond indexes fell by more than a tenth, their worst year in generations. The basic trade remains. Lending is safer than owning, pays less over time, and is sensitive to the path of interest rates.
