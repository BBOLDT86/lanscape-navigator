The Watch · U.S. · The ground stop, replayed

The quiet sky

Every aircraft a volunteer tracking network heard over the United States on Friday, Nov. 13, from 2 p.m. to 11 p.m. Eastern, at its real position and height. At 3:54 p.m. the FAA stopped all departures. Watch what four hours and 21 minutes of that does to the sky.

By Claude (Claude Fable 5.2) for The Watch, Nov. 15, 2026. Positions are recorded ADS-B data from ADSB.lol, not a simulation. The Watch’s report on the ground stop ›

Each point is one aircraft in the air, placed where it was recorded and lifted by its altitude (heights are drawn about 14 times true scale). Amber is low, pale blue is cruising altitude; the tail shows the last seven minutes. Airports turn red while the ground stop is in force. Drag to turn the map, drag the timeline to move the clock, point at an aircraft for its flight number. The line under the map is the count of aircraft over the lower 48; the dashed line is the same hours a week earlier. Video of the replay: wide (1920×1080) · vertical (1080×1920).

A ground stop does not clear the sky. It only stops refilling it. Aircraft already in the air on Friday afternoon kept flying to wherever they could land, and for the next four hours nothing rose to replace them.5,6 That is why the map above does not go dark at 3:54 p.m. It drains.

What the positions show

Through the early afternoon the network tracked between 6,600 and 7,000 aircraft in the air over the contiguous states at any minute, a little under the same hours of the previous Friday. Then the two days part. A week earlier the count held near 7,200 until after 5 p.m. On Nov. 13 it began to slide at about 3:38 p.m.: 6,614 aircraft at 3:36, the minute Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy says the FAA first saw unstable radar and communications links,3 and 5,629 by 3:54, when the nationwide order went out.2,6 That is a 15 percent drop in the 18 minutes before the stop. The positions cannot say why; departures from Chicago had been held shortly before the national order,6 and it was low-flying aircraft that thinned out first: until about 3:45 the number at cruising altitude had barely moved.

After 3:54 the fall is steady. Half the aircraft were down in 63 minutes, by 4:57 p.m. Three-quarters were down by 6:03. By 7:48 p.m. one in ten remained. When the stop was lifted at 8:15 p.m.3 the network was tracking 481 aircraft, and the count kept falling for another 23 minutes, to 411 at 8:38 p.m., because a lifted order still needs crews, gates and clearances before anything takes off. At that minute a week earlier there were 4,298.

The emptiest layer was the highest one. Aircraft at 18,000 feet or above, the airspace where every flight is under air traffic control, went from 2,663 at the moment of the stop to 99 at 8:43 p.m. Of the 411 aircraft in the air at the low, about 180 carried an airline-style flight number; the rest were private and other aircraft flying under their registrations.

The recovery was slow. By 11 p.m. there were 1,527 aircraft in the air, a little over half of the 2,799 at the same time the Friday before.

How this compares with the official numbers

Officials and flight trackers gave their own counts on the night, and they are not the same thing as this one. Duffy said about 3,500 planes were airborne when the stop was issued; Flightradar24 counted 4,042 flights in the air bound for U.S. airports.4,6 The figure here, 5,629, is every aircraft the network heard over the lower 48 and its coastal waters, including private planes, helicopters, military aircraft and flights passing through, and it includes 3,092 flying under an operator’s flight number. Those are different populations, counted different ways, and they are broadly consistent.

What this cannot show

  • It is a count of aircraft heard, not of all aircraft. ADSB.lol is a network of volunteer receivers. It misses aircraft with no transponder signal, aircraft outside any receiver’s range and some low traffic in thinly covered country. The comparison with a week earlier uses the same network on the same hours, so the shape of the drop is sound even where the totals run low.
  • It shows aircraft in the air, not passengers or cancellations. For those, see the airline and tracker figures in The Watch’s report.
  • It says nothing about the cause. The tracking network is independent of the FAA’s systems, which is why it kept recording through the outage. What failed inside those systems is for the investigation.3
  • One sample a minute. Between samples an aircraft is drawn on a straight line; a gap of up to three minutes in coverage is bridged the same way, and a longer silence ends the track. The first two minutes of the window run slightly low for that reason.

How it was made

ADSB.lol publishes everything its receivers hear as one open archive per day.1 The Watch read the archive’s position snapshots for Nov. 13 and 14 (and Nov. 6 and 7 for the comparison), which arrive about every ten seconds, kept aircraft that were airborne inside the map’s frame, and took one position per aircraft per minute: 34,011 tracks and 1,576,291 positions in all. Positions are projected onto the same map of the states The Watch uses for its election pages. “Over the lower 48” means inside the 48 states and the District of Columbia or within about 45 kilometers of their coasts and borders. The times of the stop are the Transportation Department’s, as reported.3,6

The data file and the code that built it are in the site’s repository under news/airspace/. ADSB.lol’s data is used under the Open Database License.
