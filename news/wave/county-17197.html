In Will County, Illinois' closest collar county, Democrats pull far ahead

Kamala Harris carried this county by less than two points in 2024. Juliana Stratton won it by 19 in the Senate race, the third-biggest move in Illinois. Democrats also swept the countywide offices.

What the county did

Two years ago Will County was about as evenly split as a big American county gets. Kamala Harris carried it by 1.6 points out of 324,971 votes. On Nov. 3 Juliana Stratton, the Democratic lieutenant governor, beat Don Tracy, a former Republican state party chairman, by 19.3 points here. She led 134,797 to 90,564 with 95% of the expected vote counted. That is an 18-point move toward the Democrats.

Statewide, Stratton won by 24.3 points, 13.3 points better than Harris's 11.0-point margin. Will moved further than the state as a whole. By the size of its move it ranked third of Illinois' 102 counties, and 95 of the 102 moved left. Among the Chicago-area counties, Cook moved slightly more and Kane slightly less, both about 18 points. McHenry and DuPage moved 16 points and Lake 14. Nationally the typical county moved 14 points. Fort Bend County, Texas, which started from the same 1.6-point Democratic edge, moved 27. Palm Beach County, Florida, moved 10.

Will is a fast-growing collar county on Chicago's southwest edge. The Census Bureau estimated 712,2531 residents in 2025, and about 152,2412 of them live in Joliet, the county seat. The Associated Press, writing about Pope Leo XIV, called it "the more conservative suburban Will County"3. The pope is registered to vote in New Lenox, and the Will County Clerk's Office confirmed that his mail-in ballot had been counted4.

Who voted, and why

The Senate race has drawn 229,189 votes so far, 71% of the 2024 total. Stratton leads by about 44,000 votes. A county count can't tell voters who changed their minds from voters who didn't show up. Some of this 18-point move is Harris-to-Stratton switching, and some is Trump voters skipping a midterm. These numbers can't say how much is which. Counting isn't over either. Provisional ballots and the remaining mail ballots postmarked by Election Day will be counted on Nov. 175, and results will be official after canvassing no later than Nov. 245.

The Herald-News in Joliet headlined its account "As the nation went, so went Will County."6 Its clearest evidence was the county clerk's race. Republican Annette Parker had narrowly beaten Democrat Michelle Stiff in 2024. In Tuesday's rematch, with no major local issues separating them7, Stiff won easily. Stiff told Shaw Local that Trump's popularity in 2024, and the turn against him since, was "absolutely" a major factor in both elections7. County Executive Jennifer Bertino-Tarrant, a Democrat, said she expected Democrats to win the county races, but the margins exceeded expectations7.

There is no county-level poll on the issues. WTTW reported that Illinois voters express concerns about affordability, immigration enforcement and the Iran War at the polls8. NBC News' statewide exit poll found the economy was the issue voters named most often, with the state of democracy9 next. One local issue stood out. In Joliet, NBC Chicago reported, voters overwhelmingly rejected a question over whether the Joliet City Council should approve the construction of data centers10 for artificial intelligence. No reporting has tied that vote to the Senate result.

What it means for Will County

The Democratic gains reached down the ballot. Democrats won by wide margins in all four countywide races for sheriff, clerk, treasurer and regional superintendent of schools11. They also appeared to break an 11-11 tie on the County Board: Shaw Local reported that Alex Zapien, a Democrat, finished ahead of incumbent Racquel Mitchell, R-Bolingbrook, in District 97. Patrick Jones, a sheriff's sergeant, will replace Democrat Mike Kelley, who did not seek another term7.

Local leaders aren't reading it as a permanent change. "Will County is still a purple county," Bertino-Tarrant said6. She added that what happens nationally and in Springfield "does affect us locally."6 Bob Grogan, the state Republican chairman, told ABC7 that in "all the collars and the suburbs ... there has been a migration towards the Democratic Party."12 In a county this evenly split in 2024, both readings can be true. These are unofficial results, and the next test is two years away.
