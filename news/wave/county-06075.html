San Francisco moves left again, but less than the state around it

Xavier Becerra carried the City and County of San Francisco by 69 points, four more than Kamala Harris's 2024 margin. That shift was less than half of California's, and so far far fewer ballots have been counted than in 2024.

San Francisco is a county and a city at once, a single government at the tip of a peninsula. It covers 46.91 square miles1 and had about 18,600 people per square mile in 2020. The Census Bureau estimates the population at 826,0791 in 2025, down 6 percent from 2020. It counts 36.3 percent of residents as Asian alone and puts median household income at $140,970.

What the city did

In AP's count of the Nov. 3 governor's race, Democrat Xavier Becerra led Republican Steve Hilton 251,733 to 46,175 in San Francisco. That is a margin of 69.0 points, with 89.41 percent of the expected vote counted. In 2024, Harris carried the county over Donald Trump by 64.8 points, so the move is about 4 points toward the Democrats.

Across California the move was bigger. Becerra leads statewide by 30.4 points with about 71 percent of the expected vote counted, against Harris's 20.1-point margin, a move of 10.3 points. Fifty-four of the state's 58 counties moved left, and San Francisco ranks 35th by size of move. The largest moves came in the inland counties that were close in 2024: Riverside moved 17 points and San Bernardino 19. Los Angeles moved 12. Alameda, across the bay, moved 8. Orange and San Diego each moved 5, about the same as San Francisco.

Big counties elsewhere that started near San Francisco's 2024 margin went different ways. Manhattan matched it exactly in 2024 and barely changed. Multnomah County (Portland) moved 5 points, DeKalb County, Ga., 13, and Philadelphia 20. The typical county nationally moved 14.

The 297,908 votes counted so far equal 74 percent of San Francisco's 402,964 in 2024, and that share will rise as the count goes on. With a smaller electorate, a county's move mixes two things: who turned out and who changed sides. These counts cannot separate the two.

Why, according to local reporting

Statewide polling pointed to the president. Mark DiCamillo, who directs the Berkeley IGS poll, said Becerra has united the party's voters by "hammering away" against President Donald Trump2. According to The AP Voter Poll, as reported by NBC Bay Area, most voters who said the economy was the top issue facing the country supported Becerra3. Hilton, a former Fox News host who was endorsed by President Trump4, campaigned on $3 gas, your first $150,000 tax-free, capping vehicle registration5. He told Axios he expected independents and Democrats will split their tickets6. In his concession speech, he said, "Maybe this wasn't the best year to be running for office as a Republican"7.

Locally, the starting point matters. After 2024, the San Francisco Standard found that the seven neighborhoods that voted for Trump at the highest rates in 2024 have the city's largest Asian populations8. This year, Mission Local reported that Connie Chan's run for Congress may be driving higher-than-average turnout among the city's Chinese voters9. Its map of early ballots showed the highest returns in homeowner-heavy, wealthier neighborhoods like West of Twin Peaks, the Castro, Noe Valley and Bernal Heights9, with Bayview, the Tenderloin, SoMa and parts of the Mission9 trailing. The Watch found no published neighborhood-by-neighborhood analysis of the governor's race yet, so which parts of the city moved most is not yet known.

What it means

For most San Franciscans the governor's race was not the main event. State Sen. Scott Wiener won the open seat held by Nancy Pelosi. Chan conceded the race Wednesday.10 The Standard's maps showed Wiener had his strongest showing in the Castro and Pacific Heights, while Chan had the strongest margins in Chinatown and the Outer Sunset11. Axios reported that voters gave Mayor Daniel Lurie a sweeping victory Tuesday, overwhelmingly backing his efforts to reshape City Hall12. Mission Local described a rough night for the city's progressives. Even so, the Standard found that SF voted to tax billionaires, even though California didn't11.

So the city's local politics keep drifting toward the moderate wing of its Democratic majority, while its vote between the two parties moved a little further from Republicans. The Department of Elections is still counting, and all results are preliminary until certified.
