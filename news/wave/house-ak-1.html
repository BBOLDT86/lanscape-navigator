Begich leads Alaska's House count, but ranked choices still decide it

Republican Rep. Nick Begich holds a 7-point first-choice lead over independent Bill Hill with 88 percent of the expected vote counted. At 48.5 percent, he is short of a majority for now. Alaska tabulates ranked choices Nov. 18.

The AP has not called Alaska's only House seat. With 88 percent of the expected vote counted, Republican Rep. Nick Begich has 123,241 first-choice votes, or 48.5 percent. Independent Bill Hill has 104,460, or 41.2 percent. That is a lead of 18,781 votes, or 7.4 points. Eric Hafner, the only Democrat on the ballot, has 19,032 votes, or 7.5 percent. James McDermott has 6,726, or 2.6 percent. The map above this piece shows the seat as R+41.0 because it measures the Republican against the Democrat, and here the Democrat was Hafner, not the candidate the Democratic Party backed.

Because no one has a majority, the seat goes to Alaska's instant runoff. On Nov. 18, any race where no candidate has more than 50% of the vote will be decided by ranked choice tabulation.1 Results are set to be certified Nov. 25.2 The 25,758 ballots for Hafner and McDermott are what's left to move. Even if none of them went to Begich, Hill would need about 73 percent of them to close the gap. The Alaska Beacon's preliminary, partial ranked choice tabulations show Begich winning once McDermott and Hafner are eliminated, with Hill winning less than half of the second-choice votes from Hafner supporters.3 Hill's campaign manager told the Anchorage Daily News he expected early and absentee votes still to arrive, as well as ranked choice tabulation, to trend in Hill's favor.1

The race

Hill is a fisherman and retired educator from Southwest Alaska4 and a former superintendent of the Bristol Bay Borough School District. Since July, the Democratic Party has supported him5, and Democrat Matt Schultz suspended his campaign and endorsed him.6 In their Oct. 14 debate, Hill took particular issue with Begich's support for the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which ended enhanced subsidies from the Affordable Care Act7. The Beacon reported that he criticized Begich's votes in favor of the Iran war, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and President Donald Trump's tariffs.5 Begich, who had Trump's endorsement, said he had worked across party lines to get a record number of bills passed and tried to cast Hill as a Democrat-in-hiding.4 Hill continued to refuse to say which party he would caucus with.4

Begich had more money in his own campaign. The Daily News reported that he raised $6.4 million and spent $4.8 million8, while Hill raised $2.6 million and spent $2.5 million.8 Outside money narrowed that gap. Wheel Dog PAC spent $5.7 million supporting Hill and $2.7 million opposing Begich, according to OpenSecrets9. Club for Growth Action was the leading outside spender for Begich.10

Hafner's name on the ballot was a campaign issue in its own right. He is serving a 20-year federal prison sentence for threatening elected officials, judges and law enforcement officers11, and has never stepped foot in Alaska.12 In 2024, the Alaska Republican Party intervened in a court case to keep him on the ballot.13 Pro-Hill groups spent heavily on ads telling voters not to rank him.

The place

The whole state is one district, and Alaska has no counties. It is divided into boroughs and census areas, and this piece's data has no results below the state level. The wave map has no local moves to show for this seat.

The statewide numbers still give some context. Donald Trump carried Alaska by 13.8 points in 2024. This year Democrat Mary Peltola leads Sen. Dan Sullivan by 2.3 points, a 16-point move. Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins leads the governor's race by 21.4 points. Begich's 123,241 votes are about 3,900 more than Sullivan's 119,357. Peltola's 125,157 are more than Hill and Hafner have combined. That points to some ticket-splitting, but a total cannot show who split or why. And a move is not the same as persuasion. About 254,000 House ballots have been counted, out of roughly 288,000 that AP's estimate implies. These counts cannot separate voters who changed their minds from changes in who turned out.

Hill campaigned hardest on costs in rural Alaska, telling the Beacon that "the issues that are important to rural Alaska translate directly to the rest of Alaska."14

What it means

Begich flipped the seat from Democrats by beating Peltola in 20246. His grandfather, Democrat Nick Begich, once held it. In 2024, too, Begich was declared the winner after the ranked-choice votes were tabulated.15 No incumbent has lost here yet. The Daily News reported that the ballot measure to repeal ranked-choice voting has failed to pass16, so the instant runoff will decide this seat.

Nationally, AP has called 254 seats for Democrats, well past the 218 needed for a majority. Alaska's seat will not decide control of the House. It remains uncalled while the last ballots arrive and the rankings are tabulated.
