Begich leads Hill by six points as Alaska heads to ranked-choice count

Republican Rep. Nick Begich III holds 47.8% of first-choice votes to independent Bill Hill's 41.7%. That is short of a majority, so Alaska's only House seat goes to a ranked-choice tabulation while Democrats lead the state's Senate and governor races.

Eleven days after the election, Republican Rep. Nick Begich III leads Alaska's House race with 132,053 first-choice votes, or 47.8%. Bill Hill, an independent, has 115,220, or 41.7%. About 96% of the expected vote has been counted. Begich is short of a majority, so the Associated Press has not called the seat.

A note on the map: The Watch's House report measures the gap between the Republican and the Democrat, so this seat shows R+40.1. That is Begich's lead over Eric Hafner, the Democrat on the ballot, who has 21,187 votes (7.7%). The real contest is with Hill, and Begich leads him by 16,833 votes, or 6.1 points. James McDermott has 7,228 votes (2.6%).

The race

Hill is a Bristol Bay fisherman1 and a retired school superintendent running his first statewide race. The Alaska Beacon reported that since July, the Democratic Party has been supporting2 him. Democrat Matt Schultz recently suspended his campaign and threw his support behind the independent's bid1 after the August primary. Hill has declined to say which party he would caucus with if he is elected3.

Hill's campaign was about costs. In the Beacon's account, he argued that Begich had backed decisions by President Donald Trump that raised the prices Alaskans pay for essentials like food, gas and health care4. He also attacked Begich over cuts to Medicaid in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act5. Begich ran with Trump's endorsement and on resource development. On election night he told supporters, "Alaska's open, and it's going to stay that way."6 Fisheries did little to separate the two. At a debate in Kodiak, both men took very similar positions on issues including fisheries bycatch, declining fish stocks and representation in federal fisheries management7.

Begich raised more for his own campaign: $6.4 million in total receipts5 to Hill's $2.6 million. A super PAC closed much of that gap. The Anchorage Daily News reported that Wheel Dog PAC has also spent $5.7 million supporting Hill and $2.7 million opposing Begich, according to OpenSecrets3. On Begich's side, Club for Growth Action paid for canvassers and TV ads against Hill, as well as mailers calling him "just another Democrat"8.

Then there was Hafner. The Daily News describes him as a felon incarcerated in a New York prison who has never been to Alaska9, and the Beacon notes that the Constitution's residency rule would bar him from serving. In the campaign's final days, Alaska Democrats asked voters not to vote for him. Wheel Dog PAC has spent more than $500,000 on ads opposing Eric Hafner5. Even so, he drew more than 21,000 first-choice votes.

The state

Alaska has a single House seat, so the district is the whole state. The data block for this race has no borough or census-area figures, so The Watch can't show local moves here. The only comparison available is statewide.

That comparison is large. Trump carried Alaska by 13.8 points in 2024. Mary Peltola, the Democrat, now leads Sen. Dan Sullivan by 3.8 points, a 17.6-point move, in a race that is also uncalled. Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins won the governor's race by 22.7 points, a 36.5-point move. Begich has 4,225 more first-choice votes than Sullivan. The same totals cannot say how much of the move came from voters changing their minds and how much from who turned out. About 276,000 ballots have been counted in the House race and 277,000 in the Senate race, and the block has no 2024 turnout figure to compare them with.

As for where the votes came from: the Daily News reported early on that many of the precincts that haven't reported results yet are in rural communities expected to favor Hill6. As of Friday, it described Hill as narrowing the gap9 as late ballots were added.

What the result means

Begich took the seat from a Democrat when he beat former Rep. Mary Peltola in 20241. That race was also settled by ranked-choice tabulation. This year the Division of Elections will run the tabulation on Nov. 18: the candidate with the fewest votes gets eliminated and their support is redistributed to those voters' second preference10.

Hafner and McDermott have 28,415 first-choice votes between them, more than Begich's lead. Many ballots, though, rank only one candidate. The Beacon's partial ranked-choice tabulations so far show Begich winning once McDermott and Hafner are eliminated11, with Hill getting less than half of the second-choice votes from Hafner supporters11. The Daily News also reported that ballot data put Begich on track to win. These are early readings, not a result.

The voting system itself was on the ballot too. Begich was among the Republicans who backed repealing it, and the AP reported that voters rejected the latest attempt to repeal the state's system of open primaries and ranked choice voting12. Nationally, AP has called 256 House seats for Democrats and 169 for Republicans. Alaska is one of 10 still uncalled.
