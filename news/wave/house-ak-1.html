Alaska's Begich leads Hill, under 900 votes shy of a majority

Republican Rep. Nick Begich leads Bill Hill, the Democratic-endorsed independent, by nine points with about three-quarters of the expected vote counted, while Democrats atop Alaska's ticket ran even or ahead. A federal prisoner listed as the Democrat took 7.3%.

As of Saturday morning, Republican Rep. Nick Begich III had 105,647 first-choice votes for Alaska's only U.S. House seat. That is 49.6% of the 213,009 votes counted and about 860 short of a majority. Bill Hill, an independent, had 86,023, or 40.4%. Eric Hafner, the only candidate listed as a Democrat, had 15,550 (7.3%), and James McDermott had 5,789 (2.7%). About 74% of the expected vote is in, and AP has not called the race.

The race

Hill, of Naknek, is a former Bristol Bay Borough School District superintendent1 and a commercial fisherman. He was running for office for the first time. He told the Anchorage Daily News he had never been a registered Republican or Democrat and declined to say who he would caucus with.1 He had the Alaska Democratic Party's endorsement2, and Democrat Matt Schultz suspended his campaign and backed him3.

Hill backed more oil and gas development, attacked Begich over Medicaid cuts in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and talked about the economy and income inequality.4 The Alaska Beacon reported that his affordability message landed hardest in rural Alaska, where gas costs more than $10 a gallon in many communities5.

Begich is a freshman who generally sticks close to President Donald Trump and had his endorsement6. He campaigned on the seven bills he sponsored that became law.6 In Alaska Public Media's Debate for the State, he defended the Iran war and criticized the Affordable Care Act6, and more than once he called Hill an independent in name only, citing his support from major Democratic donors7. On election night he pointed to recent decisions favoring resource development on federal land6: "This is what delivering for Alaska looks like."6

More than $30 million has gone into the race, by OpenSecrets' count.4 Begich raised $6.4 million and Hill $2.6 million.8 But Wheel Dog PAC, a group created to back Hill4, spent $5.7 million for him and $2.7 million against Begich8. Club for Growth Action was Begich's biggest outside backer, paying canvassers and running TV ads against Hill.9

Some of that money was aimed at a candidate who never campaigned. Hafner is serving a 20-year federal sentence in New York and has never been to Alaska.10 He is on the ballot because the Alaska Supreme Court ruled in 2024 that the residency requirement applies only once a candidate is elected.10 Wheel Dog spent more than $500,000 on ads against him8, and Hill's campaign gave out yard signs saying Hafner was in prison in New York11. Alaska Public Media noted that Hafner spent nothing but was the only Democrat in the race, in a year when that label may have helped12.

The district

The district is the whole state.13 Alaska has no counties. It is divided into boroughs and census areas, and the results file behind this page has no local figures for this race, so there are no county moves to show.

The useful comparison is with the rest of the statewide ballot. In the Senate race, Democrat Mary Peltola led Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan 102,962 to 102,325. That 0.3-point edge is a 14.1-point move toward Democrats from Trump's 13.8-point win in 2024. Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins led Republican Bernadette Wilson by 19.9 points for governor. Hill got 16,939 fewer votes than Peltola, and Begich got 3,322 more than Sullivan.

Hafner's 15,550 votes nearly match Hill's shortfall against Peltola. But the totals can't show that these are the same voters. With about a quarter of the expected vote still out, the totals also can't tell voters changing their minds apart from differences in who turned out.

The map lists this seat at R+42.3, but that figure measures Begich against Hafner and does not describe the real contest. Against Hill, Begich leads by 9.2 points. That is narrower than Trump's 2024 margin but nowhere near the swing at the top of the ticket. The Beacon reported that Hill at first seemed to benefit from the national "blue tsunami" before Begich overtook him as more votes were counted.14

What the result means

Begich won the seat in 2024 by unseating Democrat Mary Peltola.1 His grandfather, a Democrat, held it before him. In 1972 the elder Begich's plane disappeared on a flight from Anchorage to Juneau, and he was reelected anyway.15 The Cook Political Report rated this year's race as leaning Republican.13

If Begich gets more than 50% of first choices, he wins outright. If not, the race goes to ranked-choice tabulation on Nov. 1812, the deadline for the last absentee ballots to arrive16. The Nov. 10 update is expected to include almost all of the remaining early vote and a large batch of absentee ballots, mostly from off the road system.17 Results are set to be certified Nov. 25.18

In a tabulation, the candidate with the fewest votes is dropped first.19 Right now that is McDermott. If his ballots pushed Begich past half, Hafner's ballots would never move. Even if every Hafner vote went to Hill, Hill would still trail by 4,074 in the current count. Before the election, pollster Ivan Moore warned in the Alaska Current that some Democrats might see Hafner's party label and not think to rank Hill second10.

Alaska's seat will not decide control of the House: Democrats have 252 called seats, past the 218 needed for a majority. Voters also weighed in on Ballot Measure 2, which would repeal Alaska's open primaries and ranked-choice general elections18, the system that produced this four-way race.
