The wave · the biggest counties · Multnomah County, Oregon

Multnomah County gave Merkley two-thirds of his Oregon margin

Portland's home county moved about five points toward the Democrats from 2024. That matched Oregon as a whole and fell well short of the swings in most other big Democratic counties. Voters also chose a new county chair.

What the county did

Multnomah County is home to Portland, Gresham and the east-county suburbs along the Columbia River. It is the most populous county in Oregon1, and it was again the base of the Democratic vote. With 92 percent of the expected vote counted, Sen. Jeff Merkley led state Sen. David Brock Smith, a Port Orford Republican2, by 295,646 votes to 53,733 in the county. That is a margin of 66.7 points. Kamala Harris carried the county by 62.1 points over Donald Trump in 2024, so the county moved about 5 points toward the Democrats.

That is almost exactly what Oregon did. Statewide, Merkley won by 19.4 points, 5.0 points better than Harris's 14.4. All 36 Oregon counties moved left, and Multnomah ranked 19th, right in the middle. The typical county moved 4.7 points. Washington, Clackamas and Lane counties each moved about 3 points. Marion County moved 7 and flipped from Republican to Democratic, and Jackson County ended up almost tied. Multnomah cast about one in five Oregon votes. Its net margin of 241,913 votes came to about two-thirds of Merkley's 361,855-vote lead statewide.

Against other big Democratic counties, Multnomah's move was modest. Philadelphia moved 20 points, DeKalb County, Georgia, 13, Montgomery County, Maryland, 10, and Alameda County, California, 8. Manhattan barely moved. Across the country the typical county moved 14 points. When a Democrat already wins about eight votes in ten, there is less room left to gain in points.

Why: turnout, Trump and a governor's race

So far the county has counted 89 percent as many votes as it cast in 2024, and about 8 percent of the expected vote is still out. County election officials said participation was on par with historical midterm trends, ahead of 2022 figures but behind 2018 levels.3 A county's move mixes two things: who turned out and who changed sides. A county-level count cannot separate them. Felisa Hagins of the Service Employees International Union, a top supporter of Gov. Tina Kotek, offered one reading at the Democrats' election-night party: "It's all about turnout," Hagins said. "And turnout drives the margin."4

Much of the local coverage pointed to President Trump. Trump had tried to send the National Guard to Portland, a federal judge permanently blocked the deployment, and on Dec. 31, 2025, President Trump announced the removal of federal troops from numerous cities, including Portland.5 OPB reported that Merkley temporarily considered retiring from Congress last year, but decided to run again due to concerns over what he sees as the Trump administration's threats to democracy2. He also vastly outraised Brock Smith2.

The governor's race went the other way. Kotek, a Democrat, beat Republican Christine Drazan statewide by 9.6 points, 4.8 points weaker than Harris did. The Watch's map follows the Senate race, so it has no county breakdown for governor. Before the election, an OPB poll found Kotek attracting 54% of voters from the tri-county region – less than the roughly 61% of voters she won there in the 2022 election.6 Pollster John Horvick told OPB that if she lost, it would be because she lost support from broadly moderate Democrats in the Portland metro area6. In the end OPB reported that she won as voters spooked by President Donald Trump's overreach opted for the certainty of an unpopular governor7. The Oregon Capital Chronicle reported that experts saw her win as more about national headwinds than approval of her first term. It also reported that Kotek built upon her 2022 performance in key strongholds such as Multnomah and Washington counties8, while Drazan did manage to build upon President Donald Trump's performance across the state in 2024.8

What it means at home

The race that mattered most inside the county was nonpartisan. Commissioner Shannon Singleton won the race for county chair, and her colleague Julia Brim-Edwards conceded Thursday, OPB reported. This was the county's first ranked-choice voting election. Former Commissioner Sharon Meieran received the fewest number of votes and was listed as defeated after the first round of results.9 Singleton used to run the homeless-services nonprofit JOIN. Brim-Edwards is a former Nike lobbyist and Portland Public Schools board member10. The chair is essentially the CEO of the state's most populous county, overseeing a billion-dollar budget, more than 6,000 staff10.

Willamette Week reported that the county employees' union, AFSCME, had withdrawn its endorsement of Brim-Edwards. Singleton said she would set up a dedicated labor table in her office to rebuild that relationship.11 In her concession, Brim-Edwards argued that "We can't make our community more affordable by making it more expensive for the people and businesses who call Multnomah County home."12 In Portland, Axios reported that the city's only measure, a participatory-budgeting initiative, was losing heavily in early returns.

None of this changes which party runs Multnomah County. What it shows is where the county's arguments now happen: among Democrats, over homelessness services, county management and taxes. The vote is still being counted, and final official election results will be certified and submitted to the state and districts by Nov. 30, 2026.3
