Air Force nurse ends Mike Turner's 23-year hold on Dayton's House seat

Kristina Knickerbocker, a first-time candidate from Yellow Springs, beat the 12-term Republican by 1.6 points. In the statewide races, all three counties in the district moved 12 to 16 points toward Democrats.

Kristina Knickerbocker is a nurse practitioner and Air Force veteran, and this was her first time on a ballot. She has unseated Republican Rep. Mike Turner in Ohio's 10th District. Turner had been the 12-term Republican lawmaker1 for the Dayton area. With 97.65% of the expected vote counted, AP's count gives Knickerbocker 149,024 votes (49.3%) and Turner 144,086 (47.7%). That is a gap of 4,938 votes, or 1.6 points. Thomas McMasters, a Libertarian2 according to the Journal-News, got 8,906 votes, more than the gap between the two leaders. The count can't tell us how his voters would otherwise have split. Knickerbocker declared victory on election night, but the Associated Press waited to call the close race3 until Thursday afternoon.

The race

Knickerbocker is 35 and defeated five candidates in the May primary4. She served a decade in the Air Force, including as a major in the 88th Air Base Wing at Wright-Patterson5, and later worked as a neuro-oncology nurse practitioner at Ohio State University's Wexner Center4. The Dayton Daily News noted that she never even voted in a partisan primary in Ohio before this year1.

She ran on prices, not on her opponent. Her launch video did not mention Turner and focused on high costs, especially health care.6 WDTN reported that affordability was a central issue, with Knickerbocker pressing to bring down prices on everything from gas to groceries7. Turner ran on his experience and on the Air Force base. He argued the moment called for strong and experienced leadership, given threats to the federal workforce and Wright-Patterson Air Force Base6. Signal Ohio reported that he said she was propped up by out-of-state PAC money and backed a "complete government takeover of health care,"8 a reference to her past support for a single-payer system8. Republicans also went after her veterans' disability claims5. She called that going after a disabled veteran5.

Most of the money came from outside the campaigns. A Dayton Daily News analysis counted about $9.1 million in outside spending across 19 committees.9 OpenSecrets figures showed about $4.7 million spent attacking Turner and about $637,000 supporting him.9 The Ohio Capital Journal reported that Knickerbocker outraised Turner from July through mid-October10. Turner himself put outside money at over $11 million7. In the closing weeks, the Cook Political Report moved the race from "likely R" to "lean R"4.

Lee Hannah, a political scientist at Wright State University, said Knickerbocker made a point of Turner's two-plus decades in office in a year when Republican incumbents looked weak11. He also blamed the top of the ticket. He called Vivek Ramaswamy, the Republican nominee for governor, a pretty bad candidate who ran a historically bad ad12. Hannah was referring to an ad featuring Donna Arthur, Amy Acton's estranged birth mother12.

The district

The map adopted in October 2025 replaced Clark County with Butler County13, or rather a slice of it reaching down to Middletown and Trenton2. The rest of the district is Montgomery County, home to Dayton, and Greene County. Hannah noted the district had been drawn to keep a Republican incumbent in office14.

The Watch's wave report compares the 2024 presidential vote with this year's statewide races for Senate and governor. On that measure, all three counties moved toward Democrats. Montgomery went from a half-point Harris edge to D+14.9, a move of about 15 points. Greene stayed Republican, but its margin shrank from R+19.8 to R+7.5, a 12-point move. Butler moved 16 points, from R+26.3 to R+10.3. That figure covers the whole county, though, and most of Butler lies outside the 10th. Taken together, the district's counties moved about 15 points. That is a bit less than the state as a whole: the Senate vote moved 16.1 points from 2024 and the governor's vote moved 19.1.

In the House race itself, Turner won Greene County and the corner of Butler County drawn into the district15, but he lost Montgomery County15, according to unofficial results reported by the Dayton Daily News.

These moves compare margins, not people. Montgomery cast 202,829 votes in the statewide races. Its elections director said the election set records for early voting16, and that overall turnout was on par with 2018 – the last time Ohio had both a governor's and U.S. Senate race17. County totals can't separate voters who switched sides from Republicans who stayed home or Democrats who turned out in bigger numbers.

What it means

This is Turner's first electoral defeat since 2001, when he lost a bid for a third term as Dayton mayor12. It also gives the Dayton area the first new representation since 200312. Turner said he wished Knickerbocker "every success"12.

He wasn't the only Ohio Republican incumbent to lose. Mike Carey was defeated by Democrat Don Leonard in a district that includes southern Clark and northern Miami counties18, and the two flips helped hand Democrats control of the U.S. House18. Nationally, 252 seats have been called for Democrats and 161 for Republicans, with 22 still uncalled.

The margin is narrow, but it is well outside the half-point threshold that triggers an automatic recount under Ohio law19. About 2% of the expected vote is still out. In Montgomery County, the provisional ballots will begin being counted next Thursday, and the election is set to be certified on November 2417. Knickerbocker is set to begin her two-year term on Jan. 320.
