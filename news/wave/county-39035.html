The wave · the biggest counties · Cuyahoga County, Ohio

Cuyahoga County gave Brown a bigger lead on far fewer votes

Ohio's second-biggest county moved 12 points toward Democrats between Kamala Harris's 2024 race and Sherrod Brown's Senate win. That was one of the smaller shifts in a state where all 88 counties moved left, and Cuyahoga started from a deep-blue base.

What Cuyahoga did

Cuyahoga County is Cleveland, its older inner-ring suburbs and the newer subdivisions to the south and west. On Nov. 3 it did what it has long done for Ohio Democrats. Sherrod Brown, the former senator who lost a re-election bid in 20241, took 333,139 votes in the county to 125,283 for Republican Sen. Jon Husted. That is about 70 percent to 26, a margin of D+43.7, with 97.76 percent of the expected vote counted. Kamala Harris carried the county by 31.5 points in 2024.

That is a 12-point move, but it is small by Ohio standards. All 88 of the state's counties moved left, and Cuyahoga ranks 81st by the size of its move. The typical county moved about 17 points, and the state as a whole moved 16 as Brown turned Donald Trump's 11.2-point win in 2024 into a 4.9-point victory. Among Ohio's big counties, only Hamilton (Cincinnati) moved less, at 10 points. Franklin (Columbus) moved 14 and Summit (Akron) 16. Elsewhere, among big counties that started with similar Democratic margins, Cuyahoga's move exactly matched Los Angeles County's. It trailed Wayne County, Mich. (14 points), Fairfax County, Va. (17) and Mecklenburg County, N.C. (21). Part of the gap may be arithmetic: a county that starts at D+31.5 has fewer Republican votes left to lose.

The county cast 475,488 votes, 83 percent of its 2024 total, or about 100,000 fewer. Even so, Brown's net lead of roughly 208,000 votes was larger than Harris's lead of roughly 181,000. A county count cannot separate voters who changed sides from voters who stayed home or showed up, and this move is a mix of both.

Why: turnout, prices and Trump

Turnout was unusually high for a midterm. Signal Cleveland reported that Cuyahoga County voters showed up at the polls at the highest rate in at least the last seven midterm elections2. Early in-person voting at the board of elections was more than double the 19,000 to 20,000 people who voted early in person in the prior two midterms2.

The electorate was not evenly spread across the county, though. Turnout tended to be higher in the suburbs than in Cleveland proper2. Bay Village, Brecksville and Independence were among the suburbs with more than 70% turnout. Cleveland Wards 5 and 14 notched the lowest turnout, around 27%.2 Turnout in the city still rose: Cleveland's 39.7% turnout was an 8.8-point improvement over its 30.9% turnout four years ago.2 Within Cleveland, turnout was eight percentage points higher on the city's West Side at 44% than on the East Side where it reached 36%.3

The statewide issues were the cost of living and Trump. CBS News exit polls found that Brown led with younger voters, those who rated the economy badly and independent voters4. They also found that those who opposed the construction of data centers in their community voted for Brown.4 NPR reported on a leaked National Republican Senatorial Committee memo that called data centers "the anchor hanging around Husted's neck"5. According to WCMH's reporting, Brown emphasized prescription drug and utility costs and criticized tariffs.6

In Cuyahoga's most Republican corner, the southwest suburbs, County Councilmember Mike Gallagher lost his seat. He told Ideastream Public Media: "Every time the president opened his mouth, my numbers went down"7. Ideastream noted that he outperformed most other Conservatives7 and still lost. Voters interviewed by The Land, a Cleveland nonprofit newsroom, gave a range of reasons. A Shaker Heights resident said he voted for Brown because he has a history of working with Republicans8.

What it means for the county

Locally, the change is clear. After Courtney Scheff beat Gallagher, Democrats will control all 11 council seats next year.9 Brian Poindexter, a Democrat and union ironworker, unseated Rep. Max Miller in the 7th District, which encompass[es] the southern suburbs of Cleveland10. That race became dominated by domestic abuse allegations against the incumbent11, which Miller denies.

Cuyahoga's weight in statewide Democratic wins is changing. Signal Cleveland found that about 15% of Acton's votes came from Franklin County2, compared with 14.3 percent from Cuyahoga. The figures in this report point the same way: Franklin cast more votes than Cuyahoga this year, about 505,000, and gave Brown a net lead of about 213,000 votes, slightly more than Cuyahoga's. Signal also noted the county still has political weight to throw around, even though the county's population is stagnant while other parts of Ohio grow.2

The county's suburbs had already started moving before this year. Semafor reported before the election that while losing to Bernie Moreno two years ago, Brown ran stronger in the suburbs of Cleveland and Cincinnati than he had in elections he actually won.12

The count is not finished. The results are unofficial, and a county elections official told WKYC that turnout could increase to nearly 56% once about 10,000 provisional ballots are counted.13
