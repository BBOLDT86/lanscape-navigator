The wave · state by state · Alabama

Every Alabama county moved left, and Barry Moore still won by nine

Republican Barry Moore beat Everett Wess by 9.4 points, compared with Trump's 30.6 in 2024. Madison, Mobile and Tuscaloosa counties flipped. The Black Belt moved least, and turnout fell furthest in the northern hill country.

Barry Moore, the Republican congressman from Enterprise, will be Alabama's next U.S. senator. With 98.97 percent of the expected vote counted, AP's count gives him 917,347 votes to 759,336 for Democrat Everett Wess. That is a margin of 9.4 points in a state Donald Trump carried by 30.6 points two years ago. Moore will succeed Sen. Tommy Tuberville, a Republican who ran for governor rather than seek a second Senate term.1 The results are still unofficial.

The 21.2-point move toward the Democrats reached every part of the state. All 67 counties moved left and none moved right. The typical county moved 15 points, and weighted by votes the shift was 18, more than the national typical move of 14. Six counties Trump carried in 2024 voted for Wess: Madison, Mobile, Tuscaloosa, Clarke, Marengo and Barbour. The governor's race was closer still. Tuberville beat Doug Jones by 4.0 points, and one day after the election, Jones conceded.2

A quiet race under a loud one

Most of the Senate fight happened before November. The general election campaign season for the Senate seat has been sleepy. In a deep-red state, advertising in the race largely halted after the GOP primary. Much of the race's heated action occurred during the Republican primary and subsequent runoff as renewed accusations about Moore's military service record gained traction3, Alabama Daily News reported. Moore denied the accusations. Trump had endorsed him, and he beat former Navy SEAL Jared Hudson in the June runoff. Wess is a Birmingham attorney and Midfield municipal judge4 who campaigned on affordable health care, economic opportunity, voting rights and equal justice4. He told ABC 33/40 that rising grocery and gas prices were among the concerns he heard from Alabama families.4

Above him on the ballot, Jones ran a full campaign on affordability, Medicaid expansion, worker wages, housing and government accountability5. Tuberville ran on a mix of social conservative policies, promising to expand the CHOOSE Act, the state's voucher program6, and to cut income and grocery taxes. The gap between the two races had shown up in polling. A late Blueprint Polling survey released by the Democratic firm Chism Strategies found Jones about 5 points ahead of both Democratic benchmarks7, including the Senate race. Party loyalty still dominated: NBC 15 reported that, according to unofficial figures from the secretary of state, a large majority of ballots were cast straight-ticket.

Redistricting also shaped the year. The Supreme Court allowed Alabama to use a new congressional map in November's elections that a lower federal court had found discriminatory to Black voters.8 Rep. Shomari Figures held his redrawn 2nd District anyway. Alabama Daily News said his win highlights voters' dissatisfaction with the economy and anger over the gutting of the Voting Rights Act.9

Where the map moved

The biggest move was in Talladega County. It went from Trump by 33.8 to Moore by 9.4, a 24-point shift that matched the statewide margin exactly. Before the polls closed, Yellowhammer News had named Talladega the state's bellwether and noted that whatever Tuberville gets in Talladega tonight is close to what he gets in Alabama.10

Tuscaloosa County moved almost as far, from Trump by 20.4 to Wess by 3.8. AI data centers had become a West Alabama issue: the Tuscaloosa Thread reported two currently proposed in West Alabama11, and Jones called for a pause on building them. Madison County, which includes Huntsville, went from Trump by 9.0 to Wess by 9.9 on 159,132 votes. The Alabama Reflector reported that Democrats flipped three Legislative seats in Madison County12, which the party had treated as a priority. Next door, Limestone County moved more than 22 points.

Mobile County moved more than 20 points, from Trump by 16.4 to Wess by 4.0. The new congressional map had removed Mobile from the district and instead included Montgomery through Alabama's Wiregrass region.13 In Jefferson County, home to Birmingham and the largest vote total at 246,288, the Democratic margin grew from 10.4 points to 31.6. Shelby County, Birmingham's Republican suburbs, went from Trump by 40.8 to Moore by 19.6. Baldwin County on the Gulf Coast moved 14 points but still gave Moore a 44.4-point margin. In the Black Belt, Clarke and Marengo counties flipped, Barbour went to Wess by a tenth of a point, and Conecuh ended 0.9 points short of flipping.

Who moved: turnout or persuasion

Counties are not voters, and these totals cannot show whether people changed their minds or whether different people turned out. Total turnout was 1,676,683 votes, 74 percent of the 2024 total.

The shift grew with county size. The 32 counties that cast under 10,000 votes, 12 percent of the total, moved 12.8 points on a weighted basis. The 27 mid-sized counties moved 18.3, and the eight counties above 50,000, which cast 54 percent of the vote, moved 19.5.

The counties that changed least were at both political extremes. The nine counties Harris won by 30 points or more moved 13.7 points weighted, and the typical one moved 10.6. They are mostly in the Black Belt, where turnout stayed high: Wilcox County cast almost 95 percent of its 2024 vote and moved 7 points, and Perry County cast 91 percent and moved 11. With Democrats already winning there by 30 to 56 points, there may simply have been less room to grow. The 41 counties Trump won by 30 or more moved 17.6 points.

Turnout does not explain the pattern well. If the shift came simply from Republicans staying home, the counties where turnout fell furthest should have moved most. They did not. The 10 counties that cast under 65 percent of their 2024 vote moved 15.3 points. Among them, Cleburne County cast 58 percent and moved 10, and Winston County cast 62 percent and moved 11. Counties that kept 65 to 80 percent, and those that kept 80 to 95 percent, both moved 18.6 points weighted. Across counties, the link between turnout change and the size of the move was weak and slightly negative (r = –0.22). Turnout differences inside counties could still matter, and the county data cannot rule them out. But the biggest moves were in Talladega, Tuscaloosa and Shelby counties, where turnout fell to about three-quarters of 2024. That pattern looks at least as much like voters changing sides as like voters staying home.
