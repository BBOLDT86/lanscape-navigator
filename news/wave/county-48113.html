Dallas County nearly doubles its Democratic lead as Talarico beats Paxton

Dallas County was already Democratic territory. Fewer people voted than in 2024, but James Talarico's lead in votes was close to double Kamala Harris's, and the county supplied nearly 30 percent of his statewide margin.

On election night, Paxton's campaign held its watch party in Dallas1, in a county that then voted heavily for his opponent. Democrat James Talarico won Dallas County 563,219 votes to 206,355, about 72 percent to 26 percent, a margin of D+45.6. Two years earlier, Kamala Harris carried the county by D+22.2. That makes a 23-point move toward the Democrats, with 99 percent of the expected vote counted.

What Dallas County did

Statewide, Talarico beat Republican Ken Paxton by D+12.1 in a state Donald Trump carried by R+13.7, a 25.8-point move. All 254 Texas counties moved left. Dallas ranked 67th by size of move, a little below the vote-weighted state average of 24 points. Among the state's big counties, Harris moved 27 points, Bexar 26 and Tarrant 24. Collin moved 23, the same as Dallas, while Denton moved 22 and Travis 19. Nationally the typical county moved 14 points. Among the country's largest counties, only Harris moved further than Dallas. Its move matched Pennsylvania's Allegheny and Montgomery counties (23 each) and was larger than Wake, N.C. (22), Queens (20) and Sacramento (10).

Census estimates give Dallas County 2,661,3972 residents. About 43.4%2 are Hispanic, 22.3 percent are Black and 25 percent are non-Hispanic white. The Dallas Morning News mapped the results by precinct and found that the parts of Dallas County that Paxton won were precincts in the Park Cities, Sunnyvale and a few other precincts with low vote counts.3 In the governor's race, Greg Abbott took a handful of precincts in north and northwest Dallas County, including Addison and Carrollton, but often only by single-digit margins.4

Who turned out, and who moved

The county cast 783,308 votes, 92 percent of its 2024 total. KERA reported that Dallas County voters shattered previous turnout records5 for a midterm. Even so, about 65,600 fewer people voted than in 2024, while the Democratic lead in votes grew from roughly 188,000 to about 357,000. County totals cannot show how much of that came from voters changing sides and how much from which voters turned out. Some 2024 Trump voters may have switched, and others may simply have stayed home. Across Texas, the Houston Chronicle traced the Democratic sweep partly to drops in turnout in rural areas GOP nominee Ken Paxton needed to win, and by a shift among Latino voters6. Former Texas GOP chairman Abraham George said he did not think every Republican became Democrat7.

No county-level voter survey has been published, so what we know about who moved comes from statewide polling. In The AP Voter Poll, roughly 7 in 10 Hispanic voters supported Democrat James Talarico8. In 2024 that group was essentially split8. AP also found that Hispanic voters moved toward Democrats this year, as did Black voters – particularly Black men.9 The Hill reported that a New York Times exit poll had 74 percent of people aged 18-2910 voting for Talarico.

On issues, KERA's interviews with North Texas voters found that the cost of everyday necessities was a central concern.11 The Dallas Morning News described Talarico's campaign built around lowering costs and fighting corruption12. The paper reported that he repeatedly reminded voters of Paxton's 2023 impeachment on corruption and abuse-of-office allegations13, charges on which the Texas Senate acquitted Paxton. According to CBS Austin, Paxton largely centered his campaign on affordability and continuing President Donald Trump's agenda1. The Texas Tribune reported that Republicans poured over $280 million into advertising, including over $200 million from Elon Musk14.

What it means for Dallas County

Talarico won statewide by about 1.2 million votes, and Dallas County's net margin made up nearly 30 percent of that. The county's move was about average for Texas. Its size is what made the difference.

Democrats also held the courthouse. County Judge Clay Lewis Jenkins, a Democrat, won against Republican Mike Immler15, and four of Dallas County's constitutional officers triumphed over Republican challengers4. The same voters, however, shot down a property tax hike that would have sustained homelessness resource efforts funding16. And CBS News projected that Republican U.S. Rep. Beth Van Duyne would win a fourth term in the 24th District, where she has represented portions of Dallas and Tarrant counties since January 202117. As Newsweek noted, it is an open question whether Talarico's victory represents a lasting political realignment or a uniquely favorable Democratic environment18.
