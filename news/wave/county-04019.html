Pima County doubles its Democratic margin as Arizona swings harder still

Katie Hobbs carried Pima County, home to Tucson, by 31 points, double Kamala Harris's 2024 margin. But the county's 16-point move trailed Arizona's 19-point swing, and Pima cast about a fifth fewer votes than in 2024.

What the county did

In 2024 Kamala Harris carried Pima County, Arizona's second-biggest county, by 15 points. On Nov. 3, Gov. Katie Hobbs carried it by 31 against Republican Rep. Andy Biggs, 261,121 votes to 135,336, with about 98 percent of the expected vote counted. That is a 16-point move toward the Democrats. Ballots are still being tallied1, and the results are unofficial.

The move was large, but it was below Arizona's. The state went from Donald Trump by 5.5 points to Hobbs by 13.1, an 18.7-point swing. All 15 counties moved left, and Pima ranked 10th. Maricopa moved 20 points, from R+3.5 to D+16.2. Pinal moved 19 points and came within 3 points of flipping. Cochise moved 16 and Coconino 18. Even so, Hobbs's lead of about 125,800 votes in Pima supplied more than a third of her statewide lead of about 334,000.

Among big counties elsewhere that Harris won by similar margins, Pima fell in the middle. It moved more than Hamilton County, Ohio (10 points), San Diego (5) and Orange County, Florida (15), and less than Cobb (19) and Gwinnett (26) in Georgia. The typical county nationwide moved 14.

Turnout matters here. Pima cast 404,819 votes for governor, 79 percent of its 2024 total, or roughly 108,000 fewer ballots. County totals cannot separate voters who changed sides from voters who stayed home or showed up, so the move mixes both. An election-eve statewide poll reported by Arizona Public Media showed how much depended on who voted: Hobbs led by nearly 30 points among the half of the sample that had already voted, while Biggs led by 15 among those who hadn't2.

Why: the campaign and who voted

The Arizona Daily Star reported that Hobbs often reminded voters how closely Biggs aligned with Trump, highlighting his votes in favor of Trump's tariffs and against ending the war in Iran.3 Medicaid was the other main line of attack. KJZZ noted that Biggs tried to abolish Arizona's Medicaid program when he served as a state senator in 20114. Biggs wanted to eliminate the state's lowest-in-the-nation personal income tax and root out waste, fraud and abuse in government programs.3 Money was lopsided. The Star reported that Hobbs' campaign spent $17.8 million through the end of September, while Biggs' campaign spent over $5 million3. Axios reported that Hobbs also courted the political center, backing Republican tax breaks on tips and overtime pay5 and choosing former Mesa Mayor John Giles as her running mate.

NBC News' statewide exit poll found the economy was the top issue for 33%6 of voters. Hobbs won that group 57% to 40%6. Those figures cover all of Arizona, not Pima alone.

Local reporting points to where some of the change came from. The Tucson Sentinel reported that Democrats have defeated Republicans in GOP-leaning legislative districts in the Tucson area1, including Legislative District 17, which spans the north and eastern suburbs around the Tucson metro area.7 In the 6th Congressional District, Democrat JoAnna Mendoza defeated Republican Rep. Juan Ciscomani, according to a call by The Associated Press on Thursday.8 KTAR noted the district was expected to be one of the most competitive House races in the country, but it did not turn out that way.8

Arizona Luminaria talked with voters at the polls. One Tucson voter said the current administration's handling of the economy, the war in Iran, and concerns over corruption, convinced him to vote for Democrats9. A newer Oro Valley resident told the outlet she backed conservative candidates such as Ciscomani. Not everyone read the result as a win for the party itself. Tucson Spotlight reported that the organizing group Organized Power in Numbers said the results should not be read as a return to the Democratic Party.10

What it means in Tucson

The Tucson-area legislative wins helped produce what the Sentinel called the first time in six decades that Democrats will control both chambers of the Legislature and the governor's office.1 Tucson-area Democrats will hold leadership posts. Kevin Volk, for example, will serve as one of the House whips in the upcoming legislative session1.

On local measures, Pima voters were more mixed. Arizona Luminaria reported that Tucson voters rejected a renewed 25-year franchise for Tucson Electric Power7. The county's spending-limit measure, Proposition 425, was headed for victory after more ballots were tallied Friday1, after being too close to call for days.

Whether the result lasts is a separate question. In an Arizona Daily Star preview, a political analyst named Reilly said that even if Democrats benefited this time, that doesn't reflect a permanent shift to them.11 Pima's own numbers support that caution. A county that was already solidly Democratic grew more so, but it did so on a much smaller electorate than in 2024. The election did not settle which of those two changes mattered more.
