In Tennessee's redrawn 4th, Broderick leads DesJarlais by 280 votes

A Republican map that pulled southeast Nashville into Scott DesJarlais's district has left the eight-term congressman 280 votes behind Victoria Broderick, a Democrat whose campaign raised under $100,000. The AP has not called the race.

Victoria Broderick, a Democrat, leads Rep. Scott DesJarlais by 280 votes in Tennessee's 4th Congressional District, 95,585 to 95,305, with 99 percent of the expected vote counted. Out of 195,576 ballots, that is 48.9 percent to 48.7 percent, a margin of one-tenth of a point. Two independents, Jacob Anders and Clay Faircloth, drew 4,686 votes between them, nearly 17 times the gap. As of Saturday morning, The Associated Press had not called the race.

The race

Broderick, 34, is a tech support worker and member of the Tennessee Democratic Party executive committee, who campaigned on affordability1, the Tennessee Lookout reported. The Nashville Scene reported that her platform ran from decriminalizing marijuana to holding AI companies and data centers accountable2 and keeping rural hospitals open. She cites the 2022 Supreme Court decision overturning Roe v. Wade as the reason she got involved in politics.2 She ran against DesJarlais in 2024, when the district had different boundaries, and lost by a wide margin.3 This year she won the primary over Nashville Metro Councilmember Mike Cortese, who had raised more than anyone else in the race, despite trailing him by nearly $700,0004, WPLN reported.

The general election looked much the same. DesJarlais outraised her about six to one5, according to WPLN, and Axios reported that her campaign raised less than $100,000.6 Fox News, citing AdImpact data reported by Axios, said her campaign had no record of paid TV ads and that she credited a grassroots operation built over more than two years.7

DesJarlais is a physician who won the seat in 2010 as part of the Tea Party wave8. Axios noted that he overcame sex scandals early in his political career and routinely won reelection with ease.9 The Nashville Banner called him a loyal supporter of President Donald Trump10. Democrats, Broderick among them, tied Republicans to the war in Iran and high gas prices, and DesJarlais defended the war in a campaign interview. "We don't like paying higher prices at the pump,"10 he said, then argued that national security matters too.10 Before the election, the Scene said voters were unusually energized by a historically unpopular president, national outrage over consumer prices and what many voters view as an aimless war in Iran.11 On Wednesday, a DesJarlais spokesperson declined to say whether he would concede.1

The district

The district was redrawn this year, along with the rest of the state, in a three-day special session called by Gov. Bill Lee. The new map split Memphis, home of the state's only Black-majority district, among three districts.12 According to the Banner, the map also added the southeastern corner of Davidson County to the 4th District, which still includes Murfreesboro and the southern half of the Cumberland Plateau.10

On The Watch's wave, all 11 of the district's counties moved toward Democrats between the 2024 presidential race and this year's statewide vote. Weighted by size, the district moved 18.3 points. Statewide, the Senate race moved 17.6 points and the governor's race 27.2. Rutherford County, home to Murfreesboro, moved the most: 22 points, from R+21.8 for Trump to D+0.2. It was the only county in the district that changed sides. Davidson County went from D+27.4 to D+44.8. The nine smaller counties, from Cannon and Warren in the north to Marion on the Alabama line, moved between 11 points (Sequatchie) and 19 (Warren). All nine still voted Republican by 37 to 56 points. In the House race, Broderick carried only Davidson and Rutherford.1

Two cautions. First, these are countywide results, and only parts of Davidson and Rutherford are in the 4th. Davidson alone cast 221,227 votes, more than the district's whole House total. The nine smaller counties cast 64,621 votes combined, so even if every one of them lies entirely inside the lines, roughly two-thirds or more of the district's votes came from the Nashville and Murfreesboro portions. Second, a move is not persuasion. County counts cannot separate voters who changed their minds from changes in who turned out. Tennessee papers reported that turnout was much higher than in 202213, but The Watch's figures cover only this year's ballots.

What the result means

DesJarlais has held the seat since 2011. If Broderick's lead holds, she will be the first woman to represent the district and the first openly queer member of Congress from Tennessee5, WPLN reported. In Memphis the map worked as Republicans intended: Republicans flipped District 9 and Democrats flipped District 412, so the delegation stays at eight Republicans and one Democrat. Broderick said "we got to keep our one Democratic seat in the state of Tennessee."5 She lives in Fayetteville2, which is not in any of the district's 11 counties. WSMV reported that she will have to move into the district if she wants to run for reelection in 2028.14

The count is not final. Axios reported that it's unclear if any provisional or mail-in ballots still needed to be counted9, and Chattanooga's Local 3 News noted that the result still has to be certified by the state before it's official.15 Reports disagree on whether a recount is possible. WSMV said state law does not allow automatic recounts, and candidates cannot request one, however close the margin.14 Axios wrote that a rare recount procedure could be triggered.9 With 252 seats called for Democrats and 161 for Republicans, this seat will not decide control of the House.
