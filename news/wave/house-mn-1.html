Southern Minnesota's 1st District hangs on 321 votes, recount ahead

Democrat Jake Johnson, a Rochester math teacher, leads Republican Rep. Brad Finstad in a district Donald Trump carried in 2024. The Associated Press has not called the race, and Finstad says he will seek a recount.

Southern Minnesota's congressional race is down to 321 votes. With 98.79 percent of the expected vote counted, Jake Johnson, the Democratic-Farmer-Labor candidate, had 163,054 votes. Republican Rep. Brad Finstad had 162,733, and there were 391 write-ins, out of 326,178 votes cast. The Associated Press has not called the race. Johnson declared victory early Wednesday. On Thursday Finstad said in a written statement, "We will allow the recount process to proceed and await the results," and his campaign confirmed he will make the request.1

The race

Johnson is 43, a former high school math teacher and first-time candidate for public office.2 Finstad, a fourth-generation farmer from the New Ulm area, was Minnesota's state director for USDA Rural Development3 before he went to Congress. He has held the seat since 2022, when he succeeded the late Jim Hagedorn.3

Johnson made the race about costs. MPR News reported that his ads attacked Finstad's record on data centers, Medicaid cuts, the Iran war and the diesel prices that followed, and tariffs that closed markets to Minnesota corn and soybeans.4 In a debate, Finstad defended tariffs, calling them a tool to give the country a fighting chance to trade fairly around the world.5 Finstad put fraud at the center of his campaign. Many of his ads linked Johnson to Gov. Tim Walz, whom Republicans accuse of letting fraud run rampant.4 One of those ads became its own dispute. Johnson's campaign said an image in it was fake and probably made with AI6, and KTTC reported that Finstad would not pull the spot.6

Johnson also campaigned on being available to voters. He kept pointing out that Finstad had not held a single town hall and held one in every county of the district.7 Finstad called that accusation inaccurate and said he held listening sessions with farmers across the district while the Farm Bill was being drafted.4

The money arrived late in the race. MPR reported that outside spending here outpaced the statewide Senate race and mostly went against Finstad.8 The Star Tribune cited an FEC-based count of roughly $5.8 million from outside groups through Oct. 24. That included about $2.6 million from the Democratic-aligned Your Community PAC against Finstad and $1.2 million from a PAC opposing Johnson.9 After that, House Majority PAC put $2.2 million into opposing Finstad.9

The district

The district runs largely through farm country in far southern Minnesota, from the South Dakota border to Wisconsin.10 It includes Rochester, Mankato, Albert Lea, Red Wing, Worthington and Lake City11, plus Winona and Austin.12

On The Watch's wave map, the district's 21 counties moved about 6 points toward Democrats between the 2024 presidential vote and this year's Senate and governor votes. Statewide, the Senate vote moved 5.9 points and the governor's vote 8.6. Peggy Flanagan beat Michele Tafoya by 10.1 points for Senate, and Amy Klobuchar beat Lisa Demuth by 12.8 for governor.

The largest gains for Democrats came in the cities. Olmsted County (Rochester) cast the most votes, 78,925, and went from D+10.7 to D+18.9. Four counties Trump carried now lean Democratic: Blue Earth (Mankato), from R+1.2 to D+7.0; Winona, from R+4.9 to D+4.3; Rice, from R+2.5 to D+3.3; and Nicollet, from R+0.5 to D+2.5. Mower County (Austin) moved 10 points to R+0.5. Nobles County (Worthington) had the district's biggest move, 15 points, but still voted R+21.0. Some farm counties barely moved: Martin shifted 1 point and is R+38.9, and Jackson is R+40.6.

Those county figures come from the statewide races, not the House race, and Johnson seems to have run ahead of them. The Post Bulletin of Rochester found that Johnson outperformed the DFL's statewide candidates in the district, especially in rural areas.13 Its analysis showed Demuth and Tafoya both finishing ahead within the district's lines.13

The moves also don't show how many people changed their minds. A county total like Olmsted's 78,925 votes can't separate voters who switched from voters who stayed home. The Post Bulletin reported that fewer people voted this year than two years earlier in Rochester, Mankato and Winona.13 It also found that Finstad's vote total fell steeply from 2024, while Johnson slightly topped the 2024 Democratic nominee's total.13 That fits a result shaped by who turned out at least as much as by who changed sides.

What the result means

Finstad won easily in the last two elections, and Trump carried the district in 2024.7 If Johnson's lead holds, Finstad will have lost the seat, and Johnson would be the first Democrat to represent the district since Tim Walz, who left the seat in 2019 to become governor.14 KTTC reported it is the narrowest margin the district has seen in modern history.15

Nothing will be settled quickly. Under Minnesota law, a publicly funded recount is allowed for federal office when the gap is less than one-quarter of 1 percent.1 County canvassing boards are scheduled to review results between Nov. 6 and Nov. 13.16 The State Canvassing Board meets to certify results on Nov. 1917, and the candidate then has 48 hours to request the recount.1 Johnson's campaign manager, Nik Marda, argued that "no U.S. House recount in over 40 years has resulted in a change that large to the vote totals."17

Control of the House does not depend on the outcome. The AP has called 252 seats for Democrats and 161 for Republicans, past the 218 needed for a majority. This is one of the 22 races still uncalled.
