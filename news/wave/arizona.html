Every Arizona county swings toward Hobbs as Maricopa and Yuma flip

Gov. Katie Hobbs beat Andy Biggs by 13 points, an 18.7-point swing from Donald Trump's 2024 win. All 15 counties moved toward the Democrats. Maricopa flipped, and so did Yuma, where the count is still partial.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026, from the count as of Nov. 7, 2026; the tables below refresh with the count (last Nov. 10, 9:43 PM ET, AP via The New York Times), and the piece is rewritten when a race is called or a margin moves more than a point. 13 sources. Katie Hobbs won the governor's office in 2022 by less than 1 percentage point.1 Four years later, with 98 percent of the expected vote counted, the Democrat leads Republican Andy Biggs, a congressman from Gilbert, by 1,414,266 votes to 1,080,353. That is a margin of 13.1 points in a state Donald Trump carried by 5.5 points in 2024, a move of 18.7 points toward the Democrats. KTAR reported that she is the first Democrat to win reelection as Arizona governor since Janet Napolitano in 2006.2

The shift reached every part of the state. All 15 Arizona counties moved toward the Democrats. The typical county moved 18 points, against 14 for the typical county nationally. Two counties Trump carried, Maricopa and Yuma, now show Hobbs ahead. None moved the other way.

Phoenix and its suburbs carry the swing

Maricopa County, home to Phoenix and 60 percent of the state's votes this year, went from a 3.5-point Trump win to a 16.2-point Hobbs lead. That 20-point move is larger than the statewide swing. Pima County (Tucson) moved 16 points, to a 31-point Democratic margin. Pinal County, the fast-growing stretch between the two metros, moved 19 points and finished 2.9 points short of flipping.

Biggs's home ground was part of the swing. The Deseret News reported that Andy Biggs lost the longtime GOP stronghold of Arizona's East Valley3. The paper quoted a Latter-day Saint who had run against Biggs in 2022 as saying that Giles on the ticket created a "permission structure" for Republican Latter-day Saints to vote for a Democrat3. John Giles, Hobbs's running mate and the former mayor of Mesa, is a lifelong Republican who re-registered as an independent this year.4

Outside the metros the swings were uneven. Greenlee County, a copper-mining county on the New Mexico line, posted the state's biggest move, 30 points. With only 2,066 votes cast, it stayed Republican at R+11.2. Santa Cruz County, on the border at Nogales, moved 27 points to a 45-point Democratic margin. Navajo County came within 1.6 points of flipping. Yuma County moved 27 points, from R+20.4 to D+6.7, but that flip is the least settled number on the map. Only 69 percent of its expected vote is in, the lowest share in the state. KAWC, Yuma's public radio station, reported that the county's closest races could see significant changes as counting continues.5

The smallest moves came in the most Republican places. In October, Trump visited Bullhead City, Ariz. to drum up support for Biggs6. Mohave County, which includes Bullhead City, moved 12 points and still gave Biggs a 44-point margin. Yavapai County (Prescott) also moved 12.

Who moved

Counties are not voters, and these figures are averages over places, not people. With that caveat, the charts show a move that was broad but not even. The six counties Trump won by 30 points or more, with 9 percent of the vote, moved 12.5 points weighted by votes. The four he won by 10 to 30 moved 19.2. Maricopa, the only county he won by less than 10, moved 19.7. The four counties Kamala Harris carried by 10 to 30 points moved 16.6. County size mattered less. The two counties with more than 250,000 votes moved 18.9 points, and the seven with 10,000 to 50,000 moved 19.0. The middle group, which includes Mohave and Yavapai, moved least, at 15.6.

Turnout fell everywhere. The counties cast 2,541,760 votes for governor, 75 percent of the 2024 presidential total. A county's move cannot separate voters who changed their minds from voters who stayed home. Counties where turnout fell furthest moved most, with a correlation of −0.58. The two counties below 65 percent of their 2024 vote moved 27.3 points. Apache and Yavapai, the two above 80 percent, moved 13.4. But one of the low-turnout pair is Yuma, and its figure is pulled down by ballots not yet counted. Most of the vote, 93 percent, came from 11 counties where turnout landed between 65 and 80 percent. Those counties moved 18.5 points, close to the statewide figure, so the swing was not confined to places where many 2024 voters stayed home.

Polls before the election pointed to persuasion as well. An Equis survey reported by AZPM found about 10% of 2024 Trump voters are backing Hobbs this year.6 Among Latino Trump voters, the same survey found 15% of whom are voting for the Democrat.6 A final Noble Predictive Insights poll found Hobbs is also drawing substantial support from Independents, 62% of whom back her7.

The campaign behind the numbers

Hobbs sometimes ran toward the middle. Axios Phoenix reported that she backed Republican tax breaks on tips and overtime pay, avoiding direct confrontation with President Trump4. She also cast Biggs as too extreme. She pointed to his votes against sex crimes bills earlier in his career and his role as state Senate president in blocking legislation to decertify the police force in the polygamous enclave of Colorado City.4 NBC News reported that she hammered Biggs for his support of Trump's tariffs, votes against resolutions to end the Iran war and refusal to acknowledge Joe Biden as the winner8 of 2020.

Biggs ran with Trump's endorsement. KJZZ reported that he argued Hobbs has spent her time in office vetoing a record number of GOP-backed bills.9 He was outspent. The Arizona Mirror reported that Hobbs raised a whopping $15 million this election cycle to Biggs' nearly $5.8 million10, and that the Republican Governors Association stayed out of the race10. Outside groups did help him. The Copper Courier reported that right-wing groups, including Turning Point USA, which spent more than $18 million in ads11, backed his campaign.

Voters' concerns were mostly economic. The September Equis research found that costs & the economy, and concerns about water, dominate Arizona's issue landscape12, and that a plurality of voters blame Trump – not Hobbs – for their financial hardship.12

The swing reached further down the ballot. AZPM reported that, as votes were still being counted, Arizona's state legislature appears set to be controlled by Democrats13 for the first time in 60 years. Those results are unofficial until the counties finish counting.
