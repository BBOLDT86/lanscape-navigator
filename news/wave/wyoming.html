The wave · state by state · Wyoming

All 23 Wyoming counties moved toward Byrd; Hageman still won by 30

Harriet Hageman won Wyoming's open Senate seat by 30.5 points, about 16 points less than Donald Trump's 2024 margin. Albany County, home to Laramie, flipped. Sweetwater, Natrona and Laramie counties moved the most.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026, from the count as of Nov. 7, 2026; the tables below refresh with the count (last Nov. 7, 11:03 AM ET, AP via The New York Times), and the piece is rewritten when a race is called or a margin moves more than a point. 12 sources. Harriet Hageman will be Wyoming's next U.S. senator. She took the seat that Sen. Cynthia Lummis is leaving, and the Associated Press called the race at 7 p.m. with zero votes counted1. With about 97 percent of the expected vote counted, Hageman, the Republican, leads the Democrat James Byrd 122,157 to 65,097. That is a margin of 30.5 points. Two years ago Donald Trump carried Wyoming by 46.2 points over Kamala Harris, so the Republican margin has shrunk by 15.7 points.

Every one of Wyoming's 23 counties moved toward the Democrat. The typical county moved 12 points, and about 15 once each county is weighted by the votes it cast. Across the 2,775 counties counted nationally, the typical move was 14 points. Wyoming moved about as much as the rest of the country, but from the most Republican starting point. WyoFile wrote that the national wave "largely spared Wyoming,"2 and noted that Wyoming's delegation will lose influence with the flip of the House and Senate.2

Laramie flips, and the cities move most

Albany County was the only county to change sides. Trump carried it by 3.2 points in 2024. Byrd carried it by 17.0, a 20-point move. The Laramie Boomerang reported that Byrd topped Hageman3 in the county. Albany voters also re-elected the Democratic sheriff, Aaron Appelhans3, while Barlow3 carried the county in the governor's race, so many voters there split their tickets.

The biggest move in the state came in Sweetwater County, around Rock Springs and Green River. It went from R+53.2 to R+31.6, a 22-point move. Natrona County, home to Casper, moved 19 points, from R+48.6 to R+29.9. Laramie County, home to Cheyenne and the most votes in the state at 30,177, moved 18 points, from R+32.4 to R+14.4. Cheyenne is also Byrd's hometown4.

The smallest moves came in the farm and ranch counties along the eastern border. Goshen County moved 4 points, from R+60.9 to R+57.1. Crook moved 6 and Platte 7. Teton County (Jackson), the state's most Democratic county, moved 11 points and is now at D+46.7, less than the statewide move. Campbell County (Gillette) has only 77 percent of its expected vote counted. So far it has moved 13 points, from R+76.8 to R+63.6, and those numbers may change.

Who moved: counties, not voters

The charts beside this section compare counties, not people. A county's margin can shift because some voters changed their minds or because different people turned out, and county totals cannot separate the two.

With that limit in mind, the bigger counties moved more. The nine counties that cast at least 10,000 votes accounted for 72 percent of the ballots and moved 17 points, weighted by votes. The 14 smaller counties moved 10. Most of the state started from deep red: the 21 counties Trump carried by 30 points or more cast 88 percent of the vote and moved 15 points. Albany, the only close county in 2024, moved 20. Teton, the only one Harris carried, moved 11.

Fewer people voted. The counties counted 187,254 votes for Senate, 70 percent of the 266,353 cast in 2024, a normal midterm drop. Turnout explains part of the pattern but not all of it. Counties where turnout fell further tended to move a little more toward Byrd, but the link is weak (a correlation of −0.31). Sweetwater and Natrona both cast about two-thirds of their 2024 vote and had the two biggest moves after Albany. Teton and Hot Springs cast about four-fifths of theirs and moved 11 and 9 points. But Albany cast 74 percent of its 2024 vote and moved more than any county except Sweetwater. Campbell is the only county below 65 percent, and that is because its count is unfinished. If many 2024 Trump voters skipped this election, that alone would narrow the margins. Even so, Wyoming Public Media reported that early voting ran higher than comparable periods prior to the 2022 and 2018 general elections5.

Survey-based estimates point the same way. WyoFile reported that a New York Times analysis found the biggest shift among young voters, specifically between the ages of 18 and 292. A University of Wyoming political scientist, Ryan Williamson, said not to read too much into the subgroup analysis2, citing small sample sizes.

A one-sided campaign, and a governor's race that doesn't compare

Byrd, 72, is a former state representative who served from 2009-20196. Hageman earned6 Trump's endorsement late last year and ducked a debate with Byrd in the general election6. Wyoming PBS canceled the debate because Hageman failed to respond7. Public lands were the main dispute. According to WyoFile, the Forest Service Roadless Rule that protected about 3.2 million acres in Wyoming6 was one issue that divided them. Cowboy State Daily reported that Hageman had backed Sen. Mike Lee's land-sale plan, arguing that the lands he wanted to put up for sale weren't in good hunting or recreation areas8. In a Wyoming Tribune Eagle questionnaire, Byrd also blamed Hageman for policies he said raised costs for Wyoming families, including tariffs9.

The governor's race can't be read the same way. The Watch counts Rebecca Bextel on the Democratic side because she finished second, ahead of the Democrat, Kenneth Casner. But Bextel was the Constitution Party's candidate, a conservative who began her campaign as "an insurance policy against Eric Barlow."10 Barlow, the Republican, won by 51.8 points. That margin is 5.6 points wider than Trump's, but it measures a contest between two conservatives, not Democratic strength. Bextel's vote share was large enough that the Constitution Party is set to become a major party in Wyoming10.

Lower on the ballot, little changed. Republicans secured 55 seats in the Wyoming House of Representatives and 29 seats in the Wyoming Senate11. Democrats held on to six House seats and two Senate seats.11 All of these results are unofficial until they are certified by the state canvassing board12.
