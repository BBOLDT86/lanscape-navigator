Every Nebraska county moved toward Osborn, but Ricketts held on

All 93 counties voted to the left of their 2024 presidential result, Sarpy flipped, and the biggest moves came in packing-plant and Panhandle counties. But Dan Osborn's statewide deficit looks a lot like his 2024 loss.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026, from the count as of Nov. 7, 2026; the tables below refresh with the count (last Nov. 12, 1:50 AM ET, AP via The New York Times), and the piece is rewritten when a race is called or a margin moves more than a point. 12 sources. Republican Pete Ricketts won a full Senate term on Nov. 3. Still, there was no county in Nebraska where his margin matched Donald Trump's from two years ago. With 97 percent of the expected vote counted, Ricketts leads independent Dan Osborn 378,281 to 331,268, a margin of 6.2 points. Trump carried the state by 20.6 points in 2024. That is a 14.3-point move toward the challenger, about the same as the national figure of 14.1.

The map counts Osborn as the Democratic side because the race had no Democrat. The Nebraska Democratic Party backed his campaign, and its nominee, Cindy Burbank, withdrew in July to clear the way for him.1 All 93 counties moved left. The typical county moved 13.5 points, or 14.1 when weighted by votes. Only one county changed sides: Sarpy, south of Omaha, went from Trump by 11.7 points to Osborn by 4.4. The governor's race moved less. Republican Jim Pillen beat Democrat Lynne Walz by 8.0 points, a move of 12.6.

That runs against how some described the night. Omaha's WOWT reported that Pillen pointed to a map showing Nebraska as a Republican exception in a national blue wave, but AP's review of Nebraska's governor race in 2026 compared to the 2024 presidential vote shows every county in the state shifted toward Democrats2.

Packing-plant towns and the Panhandle

The biggest moves came in counties built around meatpacking towns. Colfax County, home to Schuyler, moved 24 points, from Trump by 50.9 to Ricketts by 27.1. Saline County (Crete) moved almost as far, from R+33.2 to R+9.7. Dawson County (Lexington) moved 22 points. Hall County (Grand Island) moved 18 points on 18,115 votes. Nebraska Public Media has noted that cities across the state like Lexington, Schuyler and Columbus now have sizable Latino populations3. The Omaha outlet The Reader reported this spring that immigration crackdowns and job losses have shaken Nebraska's Latino community this year4. County totals cannot show that Latino voters caused these moves. And Platte County, which includes Columbus, moved 15 points, close to the state's typical county.

The Panhandle moved hard too: Box Butte County 23 points, Scotts Bluff County 19. Reporting from the campaign points to farm costs. NBC News reported that high diesel prices due to the war with Iran and Trump's sweeping tariffs also hit farmers in heartland states, including Nebraska.5 The Nebraska Examiner reported that Osborn made a campaign ad trying to capitalize on Trump's unpopular beef import plan6.

The exceptions were small. Blaine County moved under 5 points on 236 votes. Thurston County, home to the Omaha and Winnebago reservations and Trump's narrowest win in the state in 2024, moved 5 points, from R+6.9 to R+2.2. Cuming County moved 9 points on 3,312 votes.

Who moved: the red counties more than Omaha

Counties are not voters, but the patterns are clear. The 80 counties with fewer than 10,000 votes, about a quarter of the state's vote, moved 14.5 points weighted by votes. The 10 mid-sized counties moved the most, 16.2. The three big counties together moved 13.3, pulled down by Douglas County (Omaha). Douglas went from Harris by 10.2 to Osborn by 21.1, an 11-point move and the smallest among the state's dozen biggest counties. Lancaster County (Lincoln) moved 16 points, to D+20.0, and Sarpy moved 16.

The deep-red counties moved more than the blue ones. The 88 counties Trump won by 30 points or more cast 43 percent of the vote and moved 15.2 points weighted. Douglas, the one county Harris won by 10 to 30 points, moved 10.9.

Turnout does not explain much of this. The 2026 vote was 79.7 percent of the 2024 vote. WOWT reported that just under 60% of registered voters cast ballots7. The 56 counties where turnout fell to between 65 and 80 percent of 2024 moved 15.2 points. The 37 where it held between 80 and 95 percent moved 13.3. The correlation between turnout change and a county's move is +0.08, close to none. Colfax and Dawson, two of the biggest movers, cast 82 and 81 percent of their 2024 vote, above the state rate. The pattern fits people switching sides better than one party's voters staying home, but county totals cannot tell the two apart. A few counties, including Scotts Bluff and Saunders, are still at 95 percent counted.

Close to Osborn's 2024 line

The 2024 baseline is Trump's presidential margin, and Osborn already ran far ahead of Harris that year. He challenged Republican Sen. Deb Fischer in 2024, losing by nearly 7 percentage points1; the Examiner put it at 6. Measured against his own last race, his 2026 result barely changed.

This race was fought over Osborn. The Examiner reported that Osborn often had to play defense against controversies amplified by Ricketts and Republicans, including multiple audio leaks8. The best known was audio the Omaha World Herald released9 in September, in which he said some Trump supporters' lives "probably suck." Ricketts dubbed Osborn "Democrat Dan"10. Nebraska Public Media counted about $65.3 million11 in outside spending by Oct. 29, more than double the 2024 race. It also reported three new Republican-affiliated super PACs formed to boost third-party candidates11.

On election night, the North Platte Bulletin reported, Osborn jumped out to a substantial lead in the early returns — early ballots and votes in urban areas12, and Ricketts pulled ahead as rural votes came in. Osborn told supporters "unfortunately, money won."8 He conceded Wednesday. The results are not yet certified.
