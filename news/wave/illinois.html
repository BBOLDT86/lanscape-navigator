Chicago's suburbs swing hardest as Stratton wins Illinois by 24 points

Juliana Stratton's Senate margin more than doubled Kamala Harris's 2024 showing. Cook County and the collar counties moved 14 to 18 points; much of rural downstate barely budged, and a few western counties moved toward Republicans.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026, from the count as of Nov. 7, 2026; the tables below refresh with the count (last Nov. 8, 7:43 PM ET, AP via The New York Times), and the piece is rewritten when a race is called or a margin moves more than a point. 16 sources. Juliana Stratton will become Illinois' next U.S. senator after defeating former Illinois Republican Party Chairman Don Tracy1. She succeeds Dick Durbin, who is retiring. With 95.8% of the expected vote counted, she leads 2,499,776 to 1,510,621, a margin of 24.3 points. Kamala Harris carried the state by 11.0 points in 2024, so Illinois moved 13.3 points toward the Democrats. On the same ballot, Gov. JB Pritzker won a third term by 23.5 points, handedly defeating Republican Darren Bailey in a rematch of their 2022 race2.

The shift was uneven across the map. Of 102 counties, 95 moved toward the Democrats and 7 toward the Republicans. But the typical county moved only 6 points, well short of the national median of 14. Weighted by votes cast, Illinois moved 13.8 points. The gap between those two figures is the story: the biggest moves came where most Illinoisans live.

The collar counties break open

Cook County went from a 42.0-point Harris margin to a 60.1-point Stratton margin, an 18-point move on 1,453,596 votes, with 93% of its expected vote counted. Kendall moved the most of any county, 18 points, from D+1.5 to D+19.9. Will moved 18 points and Kane 17. DuPage moved 16, to D+28.9. McHenry went from Trump by 5.3 to Stratton by 11.0. ABC7 Chicago noted that the county has long been known as a Republican stronghold. A Democrat has only won a countywide office there once since 1978.3 On Tuesday Democrats won two.

Local results down the ballot point the same way. Shaw Local reported that in Kendall County, Democrats won the offices of sheriff, treasurer and clerk and recorder4, and Chronicle Media reported that Democrats swept all 13 races for Kane County Board.5 Bob Grogan, the state Republican chairman, told ABC7 that "there has been a migration towards the Democratic Party"6 across the collar counties.

On the campaign trail, NBC Chicago reported, both agree affordability is the top issue in the Senate race7. WTTW reported that Tracy repeatedly attacked Stratton as a socialist8, while Stratton blamed Trump's tariffs and the Iran war for rising costs. Immigration enforcement was also on voters' minds. The Chicago Sun-Times noted that the Trump administration's deportation campaign brought hundreds of federal agents to the Chicago area and resulted in nearly 4,000 arrests.9 The Daily Herald reported that suburban Latino hubs such as Addison, Aurora, Bensenville, Elgin and West Chicago also were caught in the crosshairs.10 Aurora and Elgin are in Kane County. Money was lopsided too: CBS Chicago reported that Pritzker spent nearly $24 million from July through September compared to less than $1 million spent by Bailey.11

Downstate: smaller moves, and a few reversals

Outside the Chicago area, the larger counties moved the most. Madison County, in the Metro East near St. Louis, went from R+13.3 to R+0.7. Rock Island moved 16 points, Winnebago (Rockford) 12 and Kankakee 14. Sangamon, home of Springfield and of Tracy, flipped narrowly, from Trump by 5.0 to Stratton by 1.0. Republicans still held local offices there: Republican incumbent Paula Crouch has won the race for Sangamon County Sheriff12, WAND reported.

The deep-red rural counties barely changed. Clay County, home of Bailey, a wealthy farmer from Louisville in tiny Clay County13, moved 2 points, from R+67.3 to R+65.4. Effingham also moved 2.

Of the seven counties that moved right, five are in west-central Illinois. Schuyler moved 16 points toward the Republicans, from R+46.8 to R+62.4. Brown and Hancock each moved 9, and Adams and Scott moved less than a point. In the south, Perry moved 1. In Vermilion, the 8-point move to the right should be treated as provisional: only 63% of the county's expected vote is counted. Danville counts its own ballots, and the News-Gazette reported updated results came from the city of Danville Wednesday afternoon14. In Alexander County, only 14% of the vote is counted.

Who moved: the big, blue places

The charts beside this section compare groups of counties, not individual voters. With that caveat, the pattern is clear. The 53 counties that cast fewer than 10,000 votes, 6% of the state's total, moved 4.1 points. Counties with 10,000 to 50,000 votes moved 8.6. The 12 counties with 50,000 to 250,000 moved 13.3. Cook and DuPage, 44% of the vote, moved 17.6.

The bluest places moved the most. The 64 counties Trump won by 30 points or more cast 12% of the vote and moved 4.0 points. The 10 counties Harris won by less than 10 moved 14.7. Cook, the one county she won by more than 30, moved 18.1.

A county count cannot tell voters who changed their minds from a different mix of people turning out. Votes counted so far equal 73% of the 2024 total. The drop was similar almost everywhere: 91 counties, holding 98% of the vote, came in at 65% to 80% of their 2024 vote, and together they moved 14.1 points. How much turnout fell had essentially no relationship to how far a county moved (a correlation of −0.01). The four counties with the steepest drops moved 8.3 points toward Republicans, but two of them, Vermilion and Alexander, are still partly uncounted. The seven where turnout held best, all Republican-leaning rural counties, moved 2.6 points toward Democrats.

That makes it hard to explain the state's result by Republicans simply staying home. But persuasion and a shift in who voted inside each county could both produce this pattern. Reporting on Chicago's turnout disagrees. Block Club Chicago reported early figures slightly below that of the midterm race in 202215, while the Sun-Times found officials expecting to meet or beat it. Counting continues: mail-in ballots will be counted over the next two weeks16 if they were postmarked by Nov. 3, so final margins, especially in Cook, may still change.
