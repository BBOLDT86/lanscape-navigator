Riverside County, a Trump county in 2024, swings 17 points to Becerra

Two years after Donald Trump narrowly carried it, the Inland Empire county gave Xavier Becerra a 15-point lead with just over half the expected vote counted. That is one of the larger moves among California's big counties.

In 2024, Riverside County was one of the few large counties in California that Donald Trump won. He carried it by 1.3 points out of 940,405 votes. In this year's governor's race, with 53% of the expected vote counted, Democrat Xavier Becerra leads Republican Steve Hilton 255,926 to 187,371. That is a 15.5-point margin and a move of about 17 points toward the Democrats.

What Riverside County did

The county moved further than California as a whole. Statewide, Becerra leads by 30.4 points with 71% of the expected vote counted, a 10-point move from Kamala Harris's 20.1-point win in 2024. Fifty-four of the state's 58 counties moved left, and the typical county moved about 6 points. Riverside ranks 12th by size of move. San Bernardino County, its Inland Empire neighbor, moved even more: 19 points, from R+2.1 to D+16.8. Los Angeles moved 12 points, while Orange and San Diego each moved about 5. Among similar big counties elsewhere that were close in 2024, Riverside moved less than Maricopa in Arizona (20 points) but more than Duval (12) and Hillsborough (12) in Florida. The typical move nationally was 14 points.

Riverside County is big and changing fast. It has about 2.54 million people, making it the 10th-most populous county in the US1. Census Bureau estimates put the population at 53.0% Hispanic or Latino2, with non-Hispanic white residents at about 29%. The county runs from suburbs such as Corona and Murrieta through the Coachella Valley, and its eastern part reaches all the way to the Arizona line3.

The count is far from finished. After its 10th update, the registrar estimated that 420,000 vote-by-mail ballots and 11,000 conditional voter registration ballots4 were still to be counted. Ballots postmarked by Election Day that arrive by November 104 will also count, and the registrar has 30 days to certify results5. So far, 443,297 votes have been counted, 47% of the 2024 total. That gap matters. A midterm electorate is smaller and different from a presidential one, and a county-level count cannot separate voters who switched sides from voters who simply stayed home or showed up. Riverside's 17-point move is a mix of both.

Why it moved

The statewide campaign was about money and Washington. KTLA reported that Becerra promised to bring down household costs while continuing to push back against President Donald Trump's agenda6, while Hilton appealed to voters who he said are frustrated by the state's punishing cost of living, high taxes and long-running homelessness crisis6. Hilton, a former Fox News host, was endorsed by President Donald Trump7. City News Service, in reporting carried by Patch, said voters went to the polls facing high gas and grocery prices, the effects of climate change on wildfires and rising seas amid a shortage in insurance coverage8, along with high housing costs.

Federal pressure on the county's election office also shaped the final week. Patch, citing The Press-Enterprise, reported that a letter from First Assistant U.S. Attorney Bill Essayli flagged 1,355 ballots as being submitted by "likely aliens."9 State officials argued that the claims lack evidence and represent an attempt to disrupt the election9. KESQ reported that the county's lawyers replied that, given a court ruling and the logistical impossibility, the ROV is unable to segregate the 1,355 ballots10.

The local reporting reviewed for this piece included no county-level exit poll, so who moved in Riverside cannot be said with confidence. Nationally, the AP reported that Latino voters moved sharply to the left in the midterm elections11, and that its AP VoteCast survey found about one-third of Hispanic voters said they backed the Republican in their local U.S. House race11. Riverside's population is majority Hispanic, so that national pattern is relevant here. But this county's numbers alone cannot show that Latino voters drove its move.

What it means for the county

Riverside's politics were redrawn before a single vote was cast. Under Proposition 50, all of the districts changed shape12 in Riverside County. Longtime Republican Rep. Ken Calvert was drawn into a contest with fellow Republican Young Kim. Patch reported on Friday that Calvert held onto a steady lead and declared victory13. CalMatters described that 40th District as a safe Republican district whose center of gravity shifted from Orange County to Riverside County14. The 48th District, which includes Palm Springs, shifted from deep red to slightly blue14 under the new lines.

Down the ballot, The Desert Sun found several Coachella Valley races close15 as counting went on. With nearly half the expected vote still out, Riverside's final margin may change. Whether the move lasts beyond one low-turnout midterm is not yet known.
