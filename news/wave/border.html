The wall, and the counties around it

Every one of the 24 counties on the Mexican border moved toward the Democrats, Starr County by 72 points and the border as a whole by a median 30. In the state that redrew its map to send five more Republicans to Washington, the border is where the plan came apart.

The wall was the promise, and the counties it runs through were supposed to be the proof. In 2024 Donald Trump carried 14 of the 18 Texas counties within twenty miles of the river; Starr County, 16,419 votes and ninety-seven percent Hispanic, went Republican for the first time since 1896.32, 33 The map the Legislature drew the next summer was built on those numbers. On Tuesday the numbers were gone. All 24 counties on the Mexican border, from San Diego to Brownsville, moved toward the Democrats, the typical one by 30 points; Starr moved 72, Zapata 63, Maverick 60, Webb 50, Hidalgo 49.1, 2 Nowhere else in the country moved as far.

The picture above is a flight along the line. The gold thread is the border itself; each county on it stands up by how far it moved from the Harris–Trump vote to this week’s vote for senator (Texas, New Mexico) or governor (Arizona, California). The Valley rises like a wall of its own at the end of the flight. The table below carries three elections for every county, and the shape is the same in nearly all of them: a Democratic county in 2020, a Republican or nearly Republican one in 2024, a Democratic one again by a wider margin than before. Webb County (Laredo) went from D+23.3 to R+2.2 to D+47.3; Starr from D+5.0 to R+16.0 to D+55.8.1, 2 The 13 border counties that Trump won and Talarico or Hobbs or Luján carried are the biggest group of flips in any region of the map.

What was built, and what happened around it

The wall is real and it is being built. The July 2025 reconciliation law set aside $46.5 billion for it, $33.6 billion of it awarded by August; Customs and Border Protection counted about 700 miles of primary wall standing, 59 new miles from the second term, 287 miles under construction and 280 more under contract, with a target of 250 new miles by the end of September.15 The Valley’s share is about 90 miles from the Brownsville Ship Channel to Falcon Dam, Starr County walled end to end, a 34-mile segment past the colonias near Roma, and 17 miles of river buoys off Cameron County, which the county’s commissioners voted unanimously against.16, 17 The state had built 82 miles of its own by the time it wound its program down in February, after $11 billion on Operation Lone Star and a $7.5 billion federal reimbursement in September; about 8,500 troops hold six “national defense areas,” one of them a 250-mile strip of Cameron and Hidalgo counties where crossing is a military-trespass charge.19, 20, 21, 22 On its own terms the policy succeeded: October 2025 had the fewest border encounters on record, 30,561 nationwide, against a daily Southwest average of 258 apprehensions where there had been 5,110.23

The vote cannot say that the wall is what the border counties voted against, and the reporting from the Valley does not say so either. What it names is what came with it: the immigration raids of the past year, which in Hidalgo County hit a subdivision called Monte Cielo at least six times and stalled enough homebuilding that a concrete supplier’s business fell 60 percent and went into bankruptcy; and prices, which Trump had promised to bring down.24, 28 A UnidosUS poll of Texas Hispanic voters a year before the election found 73 percent opposed to arrests based on appearance or language; Hart Research found the three Texas districts it tracked moving from an even 44–44 in 2024 to 56–36 Democratic by August.25, 26 The Tribune’s reporting from the border quotes an organizer whose group knocked on 90,000 doors, a political scientist who says Democrats “did not invest” in 2024 and did this time, and a consultant who says immigration is the economy in a place where the raids stopped construction.28 A Republican consultant put the swing among Hispanic voters at roughly 50 points, back to the levels of the Clinton years.31 Turnout in the border counties rose more than in any other region of the state: by the Tribune’s count, 9.8 million Texans voted, the most in a midterm since at least 1970, and LULAC estimated 1.6 million Latino voters, 400,000 of them for the first time.28, 29, 30

The map that was drawn for 30–8

On Aug. 5, 2025, the president said on CNBC that Republicans were “entitled to five more seats” in Texas because of how he had carried the state.3 The map the Legislature had unveiled a week earlier was built to deliver exactly that, up to 30 of 38 seats, by redrawing five Democratic districts to margins Trump had won by about ten points or more under the new lines: Henry Cuellar’s TX-28 around Laredo, Vicente Gonzalez’s TX-34 around Brownsville, Al Green’s TX-9 in Houston, Julie Johnson’s TX-32 in Dallas and Greg Casar’s TX-35 between San Antonio and Austin.4 More than fifty Democrats left the state for two weeks to deny a quorum; they came back, the House passed the map 88–52 and the Senate 18–11, and the governor signed it on Aug. 29.6, 5 A three-judge panel in El Paso blocked it in November as a likely racial gerrymander; the Supreme Court reinstated it in December over three dissents and reversed the panel outright in April, so the map Texans voted under on Tuesday was the one drawn for the five seats.7, 8

Three of the five run along the border or draw from it, and the border is where the plan came apart. Cuellar won the redrawn TX-28 by 36 points in a district Trump had carried by about ten; Gonzalez won TX-34 by 16; Johnny Garcia held Casar’s old TX-35 by 11; in Houston, Leticia Gutierrez won the redrawn TX-9 by 15 against a Republican who had led the polls and out-raised her.1, 9, 31 The one target Republicans took was TX-32 in Dallas, by 2.9 points in a district drawn at Trump +17; an SMU political scientist credited the winner’s campaign on the cost of living.1, 12 Along the way the party lost two seats it already held: TX-15 in the Valley, where Bobby Pulido beat Rep. Monica De La Cruz by 25 points, and TX-23, the vast district from San Antonio to El Paso, which Katy Padilla Stout won by 8 in a seat drawn at Trump +15.1, 9 The delegation that was meant to go from 25–13 to 30–8 went to 24–14: a net loss of one.9 All six districts Republicans failed to win or hold are majority-Latino.11

The map did its damage beyond Texas too. California answered with Proposition 50 in November 2025, a Democratic map the governor framed as a reply to “find me five seats,” and on Tuesday Democrats took five California seats under it, including David Valadao’s and Darrell Issa’s.14, 13 Of the nine states that voted under new maps this year, the redrawn Republican lines in North Carolina yielded five Democratic pickups and the defeat of the House Republican campaign chairman, Ohio’s commission map cost three Republican incumbents, and Florida’s hoped-for four seats came to none; Missouri’s voters repealed theirs by referendum.13 Political scientists have a word for a map that spreads a party’s own voters so thin that a bad year sweeps it away: Bernard Grofman and Thomas Brunell called it a dummymander, and their textbook cases are Georgia and North Carolina Democrats in the 1990s, who drew maps for 9–1 and 8–4 and were at 3–8 and 5–7 within a cycle or two.47 The analysts quoted this week say the same of Texas: a map built on 2024’s Latino numbers that evaporated.10

The case for the defense is real and worth stating. The national environment would have cost Republicans seats under any map: the president’s approval was 32 percent in Reuters/Ipsos on the eve of the vote and 38 percent among those who voted, gas was $4.33 a gallon and diesel $6.29 after the war with Iran, and the exit polls put the economy first.48, 49, 50 TX-32 shows the lines held where the swing was ordinary. The Supreme Court found the map lawful. And the border policy, measured by crossings, worked. What the county data says is narrower and harder to argue with: the voters the map was drawn around did not stay where the map assumed they would, and they left in larger numbers than anyone else in the country.

The all-time ranking of political own goals

The Watch’s list, in the spirit of the question readers asked this week: where does the Texas map rank among the moves that scored for the other side? The test is the gap between what a decision sought and what it produced, whether it was a choice rather than a corner, and how long the damage lasted. It is a judgment, not a measurement, and the entries are drawn from the record, not the mood of the week; the sources are at the foot of the page.

  1. The Kansas–Nebraska Act, 1854 Stephen DouglasSought: A transcontinental railroad through Chicago, Southern votes, and a path to the presidency.Got: Democrats lost 66 of their 91 Northern House seats the next year; only seven of the 44 Northern Democrats who voted for it survived; the Whigs collapsed, the Republican Party was founded in response, and the road to Fort Sumter opened. The gold standard for a move that destroyed what it meant to secure.43
  2. Proposition 187, 1994 Pete WilsonSought: A second term as governor of California, won on a measure to deny public services to undocumented immigrants.Got: He got the term. The measure passed 59–41 and was enjoined within three days, never enforced; over the next two decades Republican registration in California fell from 35.6 to 24.5 percent and Latino officeholding more than doubled. The state has not elected a Republican governor since 2006 nor backed one for president since 1988. The closest parallel to Texas 2025: a border-politics gamble that won the election it was meant to and lost the generation after it.34
  3. The Brexit referendum, 2016 David CameronSought: To settle a quarrel inside his party by winning a vote he was confident of.Got: Leave won 51.9 percent; he resigned the next morning; the country left the European Union and his party went through four prime ministers in six years.37
  4. The Texas map, 2025 The Texas Legislature, at the president’s urgingSought: Five more Republican seats, 30 of 38, from a map drawn to Trump’s 2024 margins in five Democratic districts.Got: One of the five; two seats of their own lost; the delegation down one, not up five. California’s answering map took five more. Provisional, since the damage is three days old; ranked here for the size of the gap between what was promised and what arrived, and for the fact that the voters the map was drawn around were the ones who left.9
  5. The July election, 2024 Rishi SunakSought: A reset, called six months early in the rain outside Downing Street.Got: The Conservatives fell from 372 seats to 121, the worst result in the party’s history.39
  6. The snap election, 2017 Theresa MaySought: A bigger majority to carry Brexit through, called with a 21-point lead.Got: She lost the majority she had, 330 seats to 317, and governed by arrangement with the DUP until her own party removed her.38
  7. The dissolution, 2024 Emmanuel MacronSought: A clarifying majority after a rout in the European elections.Got: A hung National Assembly with the left in first place and the far right in third; the government he appointed fell within six months.40
  8. “Who governs Britain?”, February 1974 Edward HeathSought: A mandate against the miners’ strike.Got: The voters’ answer was “not you”: more votes than Labour but fewer seats, no coalition, and Harold Wilson back in Downing Street.42
  9. The dissolution, 1997 Jacques ChiracSought: To catch the left off guard a year early.Got: The Socialists won 255 seats to his party’s 139, and he spent the next five years in cohabitation with Lionel Jospin.41
  10. The shutdowns, 1995–96 Newt GingrichSought: To force a Democratic president to accept a Republican budget.Got: Twenty-seven days of closed government over two rounds, 49 percent blaming the Republican leadership against 26 percent blaming Clinton, and a re-election narrative handed to the other side.45

The contrast that worked. The last time Texas redrew its map in the middle of a decade, in 2003, it did what it was drawn to do: Tom DeLay’s plan moved the delegation from 17–15 Democratic to 21–11 Republican in a single election, and the Supreme Court blessed mid-decade redistricting on its way to upholding nearly all of it. The architect fared worse than the map: indicted in 2005, out of Congress in 2006, convicted in 2010 and acquitted on appeal three years later.44 And the one historians have forgiven. Gerald Ford’s pardon of Richard Nixon took his approval from 71 percent to 50 in a month and to 37 within a year, and 55 percent still called it wrong on the eve of the election he lost by two points; it is on most such lists, and off this one, because the judgment of time has largely reversed.46
