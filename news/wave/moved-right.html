The wave · part 5 of 5 · The 101 counties that moved right

Where the map moved right

Only 100 counties moved toward the Republicans. They are in Vermont, Maine, upstate New York and a few corners of Maryland, and most of them have a governor to thank.

Of the 2,775 counties counted, 100 moved toward the Republicans. 14 are in Vermont, 14 in Maine, 31 in New York, 9 in Maryland; the rest are scattered across 9 other states, most of them small and most by a point or two.1, 2

Nearly all of them have a popular governor or senator to thank, not a change of heart about the president. The tallest red blocks in the country are Vermont’s: Phil Scott, the Republican governor, was re-elected by 6.6 points in a state Kamala Harris carried by 32, so every Vermont county reads as a move of 30 to 50 points to the right.1 Chittenden County (Burlington), which Harris won by 54, gave Scott’s opponent a margin of only 7. Vermont had no Senate race this year, so the governor’s race is the only measure the map has.

Maine

Maine is the subtler case. Troy Jackson beat Susan Collins by 6.2 points, in a state Harris won by 6.9: a move of -0.7, which makes Maine the only state with a race where the typical county moved right.1, 3 Collins, who had won every county but two in 2020, lost 7 of 16 this time, and the ones that moved toward her did so by single digits: Penobscot by 2.6, Aroostook by 3.9.1, 14 She ran ahead of Trump everywhere; she just did not run far enough ahead.

Upstate New York and the Eastern Shore

Kathy Hochul was re-elected by 19.7, up from Harris’s 12.7, but Bruce Blakeman ran ahead of Trump across upstate: 31 of New York’s 62 counties moved right, by at most 8.9 points (Jefferson County, around Fort Drum), while the city and its suburbs moved left, Brooklyn by 15 and Suffolk by 11.1 In Maryland, Wes Moore won by 32.2 against Harris’s 28.8, and the Eastern Shore and Carroll County moved a point or two toward Dan Cox.1 Maryland’s count was 87.12% complete when this was written, so those small moves may still change sign.

The lesson of the red blocks is about the measure, not the voters: a county “moves right” on this map when its Senate or governor vote is less Democratic than its presidential vote, and in New England that mostly means a Republican who is not Donald Trump. Elsewhere, the two Illinois counties with the biggest moves right, Schuyler and Brown, cast about 2,025 votes each.1
