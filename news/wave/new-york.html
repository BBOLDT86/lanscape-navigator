The wave · state by state · New York

The city swung back to Hochul; the north country kept drifting right

Kathy Hochul won by 19.6 points, seven better than Kamala Harris, on big leftward moves in the Bronx, Queens and the suburbs. Half of New York's counties moved the other way, nearly a third of all right-moving counties nationwide.

Across the country, only 101 of the 2,775 counties counted so far moved toward Republicans between the 2024 presidential race and this week's top-of-ticket contests. Thirty-one of them are in New York. The state split exactly in half: 31 counties moved toward the Democrats, 31 toward the Republicans, and the typical county did not move at all.

Gov. Kathy Hochul still won comfortably. She leads Bruce Blakeman, the Republican Nassau County executive, 3,638,231 to 2,441,385, a margin of 19.6 points. That is 7 points better than Harris's 12.7-point margin in 2024, because the counties that moved left are the ones where most New Yorkers live. The race was called minutes after polls closed.1 Blakeman conceded Tuesday night, saying he had called Hochul2. About 93 percent of the expected vote has been counted, and the count is less complete in parts of New York City and in Rockland County.

The city and its suburbs swing back

The biggest moves were in the boroughs that had shifted toward Donald Trump two years ago. The Bronx moved 21 points, from Harris by 45.4 to Hochul by 66.6. Queens moved 20 points, Brooklyn (Kings County) 15 and Staten Island (Richmond County) 13, though Staten Island still went Republican by 17. Manhattan barely moved. Hochul spent much of the campaign making appearances alongside Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez in neighborhoods where Democrats hoped to drive turnout3, ABC7 reported. The Queens Daily Eagle found that in Flushing, Democrats were holding the line, and reversing trends from two years ago4, although Blakeman still won most of the districts in Downtown Flushing.4

Four suburban counties went from Trump to Hochul. Suffolk County moved 11 points, from Trump by 10.0 to Hochul by 0.8. Nassau, Blakeman's own county, went from Trump by 4.2 to Hochul by 0.2. The Times Union reported that Blakeman expected a boost from Long Island's Nassau and Suffolk counties that never materialized.5 Blakeman ran on cutting taxes and utility bills, fighting crime and cooperating with federal immigration enforcement.2 Hochul ran on affordability measures, including rebate checks and universal school meals2, and linked Blakeman to Trump, who endorsed him.2

In the Hudson Valley, Rockland County moved 19 points, from Trump by 11.8 to Hochul by 6.9, and Orange County moved 18 points, from Trump by 8.4 to Hochul by 9.4. City & State reported that Trump's repeated endorsement of Rep. Mike Lawler, which even included a Rockland County campaign stop alongside Republican gubernatorial candidate Bruce Blakeman, was a major point of contention6. Lawler conceded to Democrat Cait Conley in NY-171. Only two-thirds of Rockland's expected vote is counted, so its margin could still change.

The other half of the map

North of the Adirondacks the map moved the other way. Jefferson County, home to Watertown, moved 9 points toward the Republicans, from Trump by 23.6 to Blakeman by 32.5. Lewis County moved 8 points, to a 52.7-point Republican margin. Clinton, St. Lawrence and Franklin each moved 6 to 7 points. Essex County was the only county in the state to flip toward the Republicans, from Harris by 0.5 to Blakeman by 3.5. Regional reporting found that Hochul failed to win a single county7 in the seven-county north country, and that Blakeman largely declined to campaign in the region, canceling his one planned event in Watertown on the last weekend of campaigning.7

The Southern Tier followed the same pattern. Chemung County moved 8.5 points right and Steuben County 5. On Election Day, City & State reported that turnout was trending higher in several of the counties in New York's Southern Tier.8 The upstate cities moved only modestly: Erie County moved 1 point toward Hochul, Monroe 4 and Onondaga 2. Buffalo's Investigative Post reported that Hochul did better in her home county than four years ago, while Blakeman beat Hochul in Niagara, Chautauqua, Cattaraugus and Allegany counties, and throughout the state's rural interior.9

Who moved: the big, the blue and the places that stayed home

Counties are not voters, but the patterns are sharp. The eight counties that cast more than 250,000 votes account for 60 percent of the state's ballots, and together they moved 8.4 points toward the Democrats. The 14 mid-sized counties moved 9.2 points. The 37 counties casting 10,000 to 50,000 votes moved 1.2 points toward the Republicans, and the three smallest moved 3.9.

By the 2024 result, the map pulled apart. The four counties Harris won by 30 points or more, with 23 percent of the vote, moved 10.4 points toward the Democrats. The 10 counties Trump won by 30 or more moved 3.2 points the other way. In between, the counties Trump carried narrowly moved 7.4 points as a group, carried by Long Island and the Hudson Valley, but the typical one moved just 1.4.

Turnout matters here. So far, 6,092,408 votes have been counted, 74 percent of the 2024 total. The county-level count cannot tell people who changed their minds from people who did not vote. Still, the correlation between a county's turnout and its move is negative (r = -0.51). Put simply, the counties that moved most toward the Democrats were usually the ones whose vote fell furthest from 2024. The Bronx has counted 67 percent of its 2024 vote and Queens 70 percent, and those two moved the most. The eight counties where turnout held up best, at 80 percent or more of 2024, are mostly small upstate places such as Essex, Hamilton and Clinton, and the typical one moved 3 points right.

That fits differential turnout: many of Trump's 2024 voters in the city may simply have stayed home. But the city's counts are incomplete, which makes its turnout look lower for now. Reporting also points to some real switching: the Eagle's Flushing findings, and AP's national voter survey, which found that young voters, Latinos and Asian Americans, men and independents all moved heavily to the left.10 The county numbers alone cannot settle how much of each happened.
