The wave · state by state · Oklahoma

Every Oklahoma county moved left, but Kevin Hern still won by 18

All 77 counties shifted toward the Democrats from 2024. Oklahoma and Cleveland counties flipped. The biggest swings came in the Panhandle, the Oklahoma City suburbs and a band of southern and eastern counties.

Kevin Hern won Oklahoma's special election for the U.S. Senate on Tuesday by about half the margin Donald Trump won the state by two years ago. With 99% of the expected vote counted, Hern, a Republican congressman from Tulsa, had 639,510 votes to 439,109 for the Democrat, N'Kiyla Jasmine Thomas. That is a lead of 17.9 points. Trump carried Oklahoma by 34.3 points in 2024, so the state moved 16.3 points toward the Democrats.

The seat opened when former Sen. Markwayne Mullin was confirmed as Trump's Homeland Security secretary earlier this year.1 All 77 counties moved in the same direction. Two of them, Oklahoma and Cleveland, went from Trump to the Democrat.

A quiet Senate race under a close one for governor

Oklahoma's U.S. Senate race has received little national attention2, Oklahoma Watch wrote before the vote. Thomas is a 31-year-old registered nurse, active-duty military spouse and Chickasaw Nation citizen from Ardmore2. She called herself a democratic socialist and campaigned for Medicare for all, codify federal abortion rights and raise the federal minimum wage.2 NonDoc reported that her campaign has largely been conducted through social media3 and that Hern had raised about $9 million.

The race for governor on the same ballot was much closer. Republican Mike Mazzei beat Cyndi Munson, the Democratic leader in the state House, by 6.0 points. That is a 28.2-point move from 2024, about 12 points more than the Senate race. The Oklahoman found that Mazzei's 52% was the lowest percentage for a Republican gubernatorial candidate since 2006.4 Late in the campaign, The Washington Post reported that Trump's decision in August to pause tariffs on imports of beef from Brazil, Paraguay and Argentina landed hard on Oklahoma5, the country's second-biggest beef producer.

Where the map moved

Oklahoma County, home to Oklahoma City and a fifth of the state's vote, went from a 1.7-point Trump win to a 13.6-point Thomas win. Cleveland County, around Norman, went from Trump by 14.9 to Thomas by 1.1. Tulsa County moved 14 points, from Trump by 15.2 to Hern by 1.3. On election night, News 9 reported that Thomas won Oklahoma and Tulsa counties6. In AP's count as of Saturday, Hern holds Tulsa by a sliver and Thomas carries Cleveland instead. Further down the ballot, Democrats flipped six state House seats, and Oklahoma Watch reported the victories were concentrated in urban and suburban areas of Oklahoma City and Tulsa.7

Canadian County, the suburbs west of Oklahoma City, moved 20 points, from Trump by 40.4 to Hern by 19.9. It still voted Republican for state offices. NonDoc reported that Rep. Mike Kelley (R-Yukon) won his first reelection campaign against Democrat Sharon Taylor with 58 percent support.8

Texas County, in the Panhandle, had the biggest move in the state: 23 points, from Trump by 67.7 to Hern by 45.0. Being 50.6% Hispanic, Texas is also Oklahoma's only Hispanic-majority county9. The Post found that nationally, majority-Hispanic counties, where voters have shifted nearly 30 points to the left compared with their 2024 presidential vote10, moved the most. That matches the top of the national map in South Texas and southwest Kansas.

A band of southern and eastern counties came next. Love moved 20 points. Cherokee, around Tahlequah, moved 19, from Trump by 32.6 to Hern by 13.2. Pontotoc moved 19, Carter (Thomas's home county, around Ardmore) 18, Muskogee 18, and Bryan and Adair 17 each. Several of these counties lie within the Chickasaw, Cherokee and Choctaw reservations. The count alone cannot say whether a Chickasaw nominee from Ardmore had anything to do with that.

The smallest moves came in the northwest, among the reddest counties in the country. Dewey County moved 6 points, from Trump by 80.1 to Hern by 74.2. Grant, Major, Alfalfa and Ellis each moved less than 8. Among counties casting more than 10,000 votes, Washington County, around Bartlesville, moved least, at 9 points.

Who moved, and what the count can't say

The charts beside this section compare groups of counties. Counties are not voters, and the county count cannot tell people who changed their minds apart from people who stayed home.

Size made only a small difference. The four largest counties (Oklahoma, Tulsa, Cleveland and Canadian) cast half the vote and moved 15.4 points as a group. The 49 counties with fewer than 10,000 votes cast 15% and moved 13.7. Partisanship made almost none. The 72 counties Trump won by 30 points or more cast half the vote and moved 14.6. The four he won by 10 to 30 moved 14.4. Oklahoma County, the only one he won by less than 10, moved 15.2.

Turnout fell everywhere. The counties cast 1,117,932 votes, 71% of their 2024 total, and none reached 80%. Oklahoma Voice reported that just over 45% of the state's 2.46 million registered voters participated11, down from more than 64% of registered Oklahoma voters12 in 2024. But the size of the drop did not track the size of the move. The 29 counties that cast less than 65% of their 2024 vote moved 13.9 points, and the 48 above that moved 14.8. Across all counties, the correlation between turnout change and move is −0.02, which is essentially none. Love County cast 58% of its 2024 total and moved 20 points. Oklahoma County cast 79% and moved 15.

Some reporting suggests who stayed home. In two Oklahoma City House districts Democrats flipped, The Oklahoman found that the number of people who voted Democrat in both House Districts 83 and 84 remained relatively stagnant compared to 2024.13 That points to missing Republicans rather than new Democrats. Statewide, NonDoc noted that Munson won more votes than the past four democratic candidates for governor14, so some voters did cross over or turn out. A move this even across all 77 counties, with no link to how far turnout fell, fits both explanations. The precinct and voter-file data that could separate them have not yet been studied.
