Every Texas county moved left as Talarico broke the GOP's statewide streak

James Talarico beat Ken Paxton by 12.1 points, a 25.8-point move from Donald Trump's 2024 margin. The swing ran from Paxton's own suburbs to the Panhandle. It was smallest in the reddest, least populous counties.

All 254 Texas counties moved toward the Democrats on Nov. 3. James Talarico, a state representative from Austin, beat Attorney General Ken Paxton by 12.1 points, 5,446,309 votes to 4,250,166, with 99 percent of the expected vote counted. Donald Trump carried Texas by 13.7 points in 2024, so the margin moved 25.8 points in two years. The Texas Tribune reported that the win gives Democrats their first statewide win here since 1994, ending the longest active streak of one-party dominance in any state.1 In the governor's race, Gina Hinojosa unseated Greg Abbott by 7.1 points, five points behind Talarico's margin.

Paxton came into the race with baggage. The Texas Senate acquitted Paxton in his impeachment trial, and prosecutors dropped the fraud charges after he agreed to a pretrial diversion deal.1 He then beat Sen. John Cornyn in a May runoff. The Dallas Morning News reported that about 910,000 Texans voted for Cornyn in the March Republican primary. A little more than 500,000 backed him in the May runoff2. Talarico ran a campaign built around lowering costs and fighting corruption3.

Paxton's own suburbs

Collin County, Paxton's home, went from Trump by 11.2 points to Talarico by 12.1, a 23-point move on 457,413 votes. The Dallas Morning News tied the change to growth driven by corporate headquarters moving to suburbs such as Plano and Allen, drawing racially diverse residents with college degrees.4 Next door, Denton County went from Trump by 13.2 to Talarico by 8.4.

Tarrant County, around Fort Worth, went from Trump by 5.1 to Talarico by 19.2. The Texas Observer reported that Democrat Alisa Simmons beat County Judge Tim O'Hare, signaling the first win for a Democrat at the county level since 1986.5 Williamson County, north of Austin, went from Trump by 2.5 to Talarico by 20.3. Fort Bend County, southwest of Houston, widened a 1.6-point Democratic edge to 28.6.

Montgomery County stayed Republican, but by 22.2 points instead of 45.5. The Houston Chronicle found that Collin County turnout fell 3 points from 61% in 2018 to 58% in 2026. In the Houston area, Montgomery County turnout fell 4 percentage points.6 In a Morning News analysis, a Republican pollster said many voters were more concerned about rising costs than attacks on Talarico7, and the paper noted that some Republicans publicly backed Talarico.7

Cities, the border and the Panhandle

Harris County cast 1,382,875 votes, more than any other county, and its Democratic margin grew from 5.5 points to 33.0. Dallas County moved 23 points, and Bexar County (San Antonio) moved 26. El Paso County moved 33, to a 48.0-point Democratic margin. Travis County moved least of the big counties, 19 points, partly because it started at Harris by 39.3.

The largest moves were on the border. Starr County moved 72 points, the most in the country, while Zapata moved 63 and Maverick 60. Webb and Hidalgo each moved about 49, both flipping from narrow Trump wins. The Watch's regional piece covers those counties. In reporting from the Rio Grande Valley, the Houston Chronicle pointed to inflation, ICE crackdown and more8. A UnidosUS poll reported by Axios found that James Talarico led Ken Paxton 65% to 31% among Latino voters statewide.9 The swing also reached West Texas. Reeves County went from Trump by 36.9 to Talarico by 7.7. In Culberson County, 867 people voted, more than the 781 who did in 2024, and it went from Trump by 16.9 to Talarico by 22.1.

The smallest moves came in ranch counties of the Panhandle and the Rolling Plains. King County, with 116 votes, moved 7 points and still went to Paxton by 84.5. Roberts, Borden and Motley counties moved about 8 each. In East Texas, Sabine County moved 9 points and Houston County 10, the second-smallest move among counties with more than 5,000 votes in 2024. The Chronicle reported that Paxton carried these regions easily, but turnout fell in almost every county in each of those regions of the state.6

Who moved

Counties are not voters, and these figures describe places, not people. With that caveat, the 10 counties that cast more than 250,000 votes each made up 59 percent of the statewide vote and moved 24.4 points, weighted by votes. The 156 counties with fewer than 10,000 votes, 5 percent of the total, moved 18.9. The 23 midsize counties, those casting 50,000 to 250,000 votes, moved most: 27.8 points.

By 2024 lean, the closest places moved most. The eight counties Trump won by less than 10 points moved 29.9 points weighted, and the typical one moved 41.3. The 211 counties Trump won by 30 or more moved 19.6. The two counties Harris won by 30 or more moved 19.2.

Turnout is the harder question. Statewide, 2026 votes came to 86.9 percent of the 2024 total. The 143 counties where turnout fell to between 65 and 80 percent of 2024, mostly small and Republican, moved 20.1 points. The 108 counties between 80 and 95 percent, holding 88 percent of the vote, moved 24.8. The three counties where turnout held above 95 percent moved 26.5. The link between turnout and a county's move is weak (a correlation of +0.23). That does not fit a simple story in which Republicans stayed home: if they had, the places with the biggest turnout drops would show the biggest moves. Without voter files, a county count cannot separate a voter who switched sides from one who skipped the race. Even so, a shift that showed up in every county, including those where turnout held, suggests persuasion played a real part.

These figures are unofficial. The count stands at 99 percent of the expected vote, and county canvasses have yet to finish.
