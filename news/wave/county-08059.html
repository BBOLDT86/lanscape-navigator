Jeffco moves toward Hickenlooper, but less than most big Colorado counties

Jefferson County, west of Denver, gave John Hickenlooper a 32-point margin, 13 points more than Kamala Harris won it by. It still moved less than Colorado as a whole, and its voters appear to have narrowly rejected new school taxes.

Jefferson County runs from the older suburbs on Denver's western edge into the foothills. The county seat is Golden, which was Colorado's capital twice, and its most populous city is Lakewood.1 The Census Bureau puts its 2025 population at 580,451, a 0.4% decline since 20202. It is an affluent, highly educated place. Median household income is $110,656, the poverty rate is 7.2%, and 52.0%3 of adults hold at least a bachelor's degree. It also has a higher share of a non-Hispanic white population than Colorado as a whole.4

What Jefferson County did

U.S. Sen. John Hickenlooper, the Democrat, beat Republican state Sen. Mark Baisley in the county 196,104 to 96,235, a margin of 32.4 points, with 98% of the expected vote counted. Harris won the county by 19.5 points in 2024, so it moved about 13 points toward the Democrats.

That is a large move, but it was a little smaller than the state's. Statewide, Hickenlooper won by 25.3 points, 14.3 points better than Harris's 11.0-point margin. Jefferson County ranked 23rd of Colorado's 64 counties by size of move, and all 64 moved left. Among the state's other big counties, Adams moved 23 points, Arapahoe 16, and Denver and El Paso 14 each. El Paso went from Republican to Democratic. Larimer moved 13 points and Douglas 11, and Douglas also flipped. Big counties elsewhere that started near Jefferson's 2024 margin split widely. Sacramento moved about 10 points and Broward 15. Allegheny and Dallas each moved 23.

Turnout fell. The county cast 308,515 votes for senator, 85% of its 2024 presidential total and about 53,000 fewer ballots. A county count cannot tell voters who changed sides from voters who stayed home or newly showed up, so the 13-point move reflects both. The Denver Gazette, using county clerk data, reported that voter turnout across Jeffco was roughly the same compared to the 2022 midterms5. It also reported that turnout was also better than across the state5.

Why

Reporting across the state describes a race that was never close. The Colorado Sun called it a race that was never really competitive6 and noted that the win came against a relatively poorly funded and much lesser known opponent who entered the race late.6 The Denver Gazette reported that Hickenlooper raised a reported $11 million through the middle of October to the Republican's $138,000.7 In a post-election analysis, the paper's Ernest Luning wrote that Baisley ran a lackluster campaign and didn't raise as much money as dozens of legislative candidates.8 A third candidate, Bob Chew of the Forward Party, pumped more than $3 million into a massive ad campaign9.

The two candidates offered sharply different platforms. Hickenlooper promised during the campaign to be a forceful check on the Trump administration6. Axios Denver reported that Baisley didn't waver from his conservative views, expressing support for the Iran war and mass deportations while opposing abortion.10 In his victory statement, Hickenlooper framed the choice as "more of President Trump's relentless corruption and higher prices AND change"11. Colorado Public Radio cited a bipartisan Colorado Polling Institute survey in which voters from across the political spectrum cited affordability as the top issue they want the state government to address12.

The reporting gathered for this piece included no exit poll broken out for Jefferson County. Statewide, a Keating Research poll taken Nov. 1–2 found Hickenlooper leading among unaffiliated voters, which the firm called the largest bloc in Colorado's electorate.13

What it means for Jeffco

Democrats did well down the ballot. The Denver Gazette reported that all countywide races went Democrat with 60% or better margins, with the lone exception being the uncontested county surveyor race5. That included state Sen. Lisa Cutter's win for Jefferson County Commissioner for District 3.14 Several local officials are moving to statewide jobs. Amanda Gonzalez, the county clerk whose office ran this count, won the secretary of state race. Commissioner Lesley Dahlkemper, Cutter's predecessor, was elected lieutenant governor as Phil Weiser's running mate.

Voters who backed Democrats were more reluctant to raise local taxes. Jeffco Public Schools asked for a $73 million mill levy override to be used for teacher pay increases and educational programs, and 5B, a $60 million special-purpose mill levy override15 for building maintenance. Both measures were trailing. On 5A, the Gazette reported, the margin is close enough to trigger an automatic recount under Colorado law.15 District officials told the paper they respected the initial election results showing that voters did not approve the tax rate increase.15 In Lakewood, a ballot issue for a $17.6 million tax rate increase seemed to be shot down5. The county's voters also leaned against Amendment 85, the plain-language ballot measure, even though more voters statewide supported it.

The Gazette concluded that the results show Jefferson County is still a strong county for Democrats.5 Results are unofficial and final results have not yet been certified.5
