The wave · state by state · Kentucky

Eastern Kentucky moved most as Barr held McConnell's seat by 12

All 120 Kentucky counties moved toward Democrat Charles Booker compared with Donald Trump's 2024 margins, and Franklin County flipped. Republican Andy Barr still won by 12 points. The mountain counties moved the most, and Lake Cumberland and the far west moved the least.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026, from the count as of Nov. 7, 2026; the tables below refresh with the count (last Nov. 10, 9:43 AM ET, AP via The New York Times), and the piece is rewritten when a race is called or a margin moves more than a point. 15 sources. Andy Barr will take Mitch McConnell's seat in the U.S. Senate, but the state that elected him looks different from two years ago. With 99% of the expected vote counted, Barr, a Republican congressman from Lexington, had 784,869 votes to 612,357 for Democrat Charles Booker. That is about 55.0% to 42.9%, a margin of 12.1 points. Donald Trump carried Kentucky by 30.6 points in 2024, so the Republican margin fell by 18.5 points. Every one of the 120 counties moved toward the Democrats, and the typical county moved 15.6 points. One county changed hands: Franklin, which includes the capital, Frankfort.

Barr had President Donald Trump's endorsement, and he painted Booker as too far left for Kentucky1. The Kentucky Lantern reported that he frequently called him "the Mamdani of Kentucky"2. Booker, a former state representative from Louisville running for the Senate for the third time, said affordability and public safety are two key issues for his campaign3. He also called for bolster[ing] Social Security and Medicaid in the short-term4. Louisville Public Media reported that Barr has a massive fundraising advantage over Booker4, and LEX18 noted that experts said his campaign was not considered a priority for national Democrats5. Kentucky Public Radio's Sylvia Goodman told NPR that Trump's approval in the state had fallen 34 points to a negative 11 point net approval rating - that's per Civiqs polling6. Further down the ballot, Democrat Zach Dembo defeated Republican Ralph Alvarado to flip Kentucky's 6th Congressional District7.

The mountains moved most

All ten of the largest moves were in eastern Kentucky. Breathitt County went from Trump by 59.3 points to Barr by 30.5, a 29-point move. Floyd moved 28 points. Rowan, home to Morehead State University, moved 26 points and finished at R+1.5, Barr's closest win in any county. Wolfe, Letcher, Elliott, Magoffin, Pike, Carter and Boyd each moved between 23 and 26 points. Barr still won all of them easily: by 31 points in Floyd and by 42 in Pike.

The Kentucky Center for Economic Policy, a progressive-leaning research group, found that the counties with the highest vulnerability to Medicaid cuts are all in eastern and southern Kentucky8. The southern counties did not follow the east, though. In the Lake Cumberland area, Monroe moved 7 points and Clinton 8. In the far west, Crittenden and Livingston moved 7 points each, the smallest shifts in the state. The county count cannot show whether Medicaid changed anyone's vote.

The bigger margins did not bring Democrats new seats in the east. A strategist interviewed by WKYT said "There was no growth for the Democratic Party in Kentucky last night outside of Lexington and Louisville"9. In the state House race that represents Floyd County and part of Pike County10, WYMT reported that Republican Tanner Hesterberg won. That was the seat WYMT had described in 2024 as the region's sole Democrat11. Still, the margins point the other way from the strategist's comment: Jefferson moved 15 points and Fayette 14, both below the typical county.

Suburbs, the capital and an exception

The Democratic margin in Jefferson County (Louisville) grew from 16.6 points to 31.9, and in Fayette County (Lexington) from 18.1 to 31.6. Northern Kentucky came close to the line. Kenton went from R+21.7 to R+2.3, and Campbell from R+19.7 to R+1.9. Trump rallied for Barr in Campbell County on Oct. 17, and WKRC reported that the crowd filled less than half of the arena12. In the middle of the state, Warren (Bowling Green) moved 21 points to R+3.5, Madison 21 points to R+10.1 and Scott 21 points to R+6.6.

Franklin went from R+4.8 to D+11.6, a move of more than 16 points. That puts it back where it was for Booker four years ago: the Lexington Herald-Leader noted that in 2022, Rand Paul won every county but Jefferson, Fayette and Franklin13. The State Journal's unofficial local returns showed Democrats also winning the county's sheriff and state Senate races.

The exception was Woodford, a horse-farm county next to Lexington, which moved only 9 points, to R+5.2.

Who moved: counties, not voters

Kentuckians cast 1,427,118 votes for senator, 69% of the 2024 total. Secretary of State Michael Adams had predicted turnout would be really modest compared to that14 2024 election, even though early voting exceeded both the 2022 turnout of 337,975 and the 2023 count of 313,00915.

The move was broad across counties of every size. The 87 counties with fewer than 10,000 votes, 28% of the total, moved 16 points weighted by votes. The 30 mid-sized counties, 41% of the vote, moved 17. Kenton and Fayette together moved 15.5, and Jefferson 15.4.

The reddest places moved slightly more than the bluest. The 107 counties Trump won by 30 points or more cast 54% of the vote and moved 16.5 points. Jefferson and Fayette, the only two counties Kamala Harris won, moved 14.8.

The drop in turnout does not explain the move. The 45 counties that cast less than 65% of their 2024 vote moved 16.5 points, almost exactly as much as the 71 counties between 65% and 80%. The correlation between a county's turnout change and its move was -0.01, close to zero. Only four counties kept more than 80% of their 2024 vote: Owsley, Nicholas, Woodford and Magoffin. Three of them moved 10 points or less, and Magoffin moved 25.

These are county totals, not voters. The count cannot tell people who changed their minds apart from a different mix of people turning out within each county. What it shows is that the shift reached nearly every kind of place in Kentucky, whether turnout fell a lot or a little.
