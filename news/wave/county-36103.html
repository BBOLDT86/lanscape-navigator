Suffolk County, a 10-point Trump win in 2024, tips to Hochul

Gov. Kathy Hochul narrowly carried Long Island's biggest county against Bruce Blakeman. It moved about 11 points toward the Democrats, less than the typical county nationally. Republicans still won key races further down the ballot.

What Suffolk did

Two years after Suffolk County gave Donald Trump a 10-point win, it voted for Gov. Kathy Hochul, though only just. With 97 percent of the expected vote counted, the Democrat led Bruce Blakeman, the Republican Nassau County executive, 270,778 to 266,738. Her margin was 4,040 votes, less than one point. In 2024, Trump's lead here was roughly 76,000 votes.

That is a move of about 11 points toward the Democrats. It ranks seventh among New York's 62 counties and is larger than the state's 7.0-point move. It is smaller than the moves in the New York City boroughs: 15 points in Brooklyn (Kings County) and 20 in Queens. Suffolk also remains far to the right of a state that Hochul won by 19.6 points. In neighboring Nassau, Blakeman's home county, the vote moved about 4 points, just enough to give Hochul a 0.2-point edge there.

Compared with the rest of the country, Suffolk's shift was modest. The typical county moved 14 points. Other big counties that Trump carried by 10 to 14 points in 2024 moved further: El Paso County, Colorado, by 14 points; Miami-Dade by 19; and Collin County, Texas, by 23. The closest match was Macomb County, Michigan, which moved 12 points and stayed narrowly Republican.

Suffolk is a large, affluent suburb. It had 1,546,0901 residents in the Census Bureau's 2025 estimate. Its median household income was $128,329, against $84,578 for New York2 as a whole.

The total vote for governor was 538,227, or 71 percent of the 2024 presidential vote. That is about 221,000 fewer ballots. A county's move mixes two things the count cannot separate: voters who switched sides and voters who stayed home. When nearly three in 10 voters drop out, a falloff among Trump's 2024 voters alone could account for much of a swing this size.

Why it moved

Greater Long Island reported that each candidate tried to tie the other to an unpopular figure. Hochul linked Blakeman to Trump, who endorsed him. Blakeman went after Hochul's alliance with New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani.3 Blakeman campaigned on cutting taxes and utility bills, fighting crime and cooperating with federal immigration enforcement. Hochul ran on affordability measures, including rebate checks and universal school meals.3

The Times Union in Albany wrote that Blakeman expected a boost from Long Island's Nassau and Suffolk counties that never materialized.4 Michael Dawidziak, a Long Island consultant who often works for Republicans, told the paper that Blakeman "hammered home the MAGA talking points at the expense of other things that could have excited the suburbs."4 CBS New York reported that analysts say Blakeman's close ties to President Trump likely hurt him, and dragged down members of the GOP across the island.5

There is no exit poll for Suffolk alone. Before the election, a Quinnipiac poll found that upstate and suburban residents were more likely to point to taxes and health care6 as their top issues, the Rochester Beacon reported. Nationally, voters in the AP VoteCast survey mostly blamed the administration for prices. About 6 in 10 say higher than usual prices are "more because of Trump's policies"7.

The shift was not even across Long Island. Greater Long Island cited NBC's data on the 2nd Congressional District: the Nassau portion voting essentially the same as it did for Trump in 2024, while the Suffolk portion moved toward the Democrats.3

What it means

Suffolk voters did not reject Republicans across the board. On election night, Suffolk Republican chairman Jesse Garcia said that if a "blue wave" came, it will be stopped in Suffolk County.8 Rep. Nick LaLota went on to win a third term. In the same election, Democrat Chris Murray won an upset victory in a State Senate district held by Republicans for more than a century.9 Rep. Andrew Garbarino led in the 2nd District, although Halpin led in the Suffolk-only portion of the congressional district.10 A South Shore Press column called the result a split decision, not a blanket rejection of Republicans.10

The two parties read the result differently. Nassau Democratic chairman Jay Jacobs told the Times Union that "Long Island hasn't gone red, it's still strictly purple."11 Local Republicans said it's only a temporary defeat12, NBC New York reported.

None of this is final. The Suffolk results are unofficial, and the count was not complete when this was written. In the 2nd District, Pat Halpin in the 2nd District has not conceded defeat.5
