Orange County widens its Democratic lead as Florida stays narrowly red

Angie Nixon carried Orlando's home county by more than 28 points, more than double Kamala Harris's 2024 margin, with fewer people voting. It was among Florida's biggest swings and still not enough to beat Ashley Moody statewide.

What Orange County did

Orange County, home to Orlando and Florida's theme-park belt, has 1.5 million residents and 75 million visitors a year1, Spectrum News 13 reported. On Nov. 3 it voted for Angie Nixon, the Democratic state representative, over Republican Sen. Ashley Moody, 300,941 votes to 166,337. That is a 28.5-point margin with 99% of the expected vote counted. Kamala Harris carried the county by 13.6 points in 2024, so the county moved 15 points toward Democrats. That ranks sixth among Florida's 67 counties.

The race was a special election. Gov. Ron DeSantis appointed Moody to succeed Marco Rubio when he became U.S. secretary of state in 2025.2 Nixon, a Jacksonville lawmaker, is a democratic socialist who upset establishment Democrats' more moderate favorite in the primary2, Alex Vindman.

Statewide, Moody won by 2.9 points, 4,210,242 votes to 3,972,095. That was a 10.2-point move toward Democrats from Donald Trump's 13.1-point win in 2024, and 66 of 67 counties moved left. Orange moved about twice as far as the typical Florida county (+7). It also moved further than Hillsborough and Duval (+12 each), Palm Beach (+10) and Pinellas (+6). It about matched Broward (+15) but trailed Miami-Dade, which swung 19 points and flipped from R+11.4 to D+7.1. Nixon's lead in Orange was about 134,600 votes. That covered more than half of her roughly 238,000-vote deficit statewide.

Compared with big counties elsewhere that voted about the same way in 2024, Orange is in the middle. It roughly matched DuPage County, Illinois, and Pima County, Arizona (+16 each). It moved further than Hamilton County, Ohio (+10) but well short of Cobb (+19) and Gwinnett (+26) in Georgia. Its move almost exactly matched the typical county nationally (+14).

Who turned out, and who moved

The county cast 471,829 votes, 78% of its 2024 total. The voters who came out leaned Democratic. By the end of early voting, Spectrum News 13 reported, 324,422 voters had cast ballots early or by mail3, and of those voters, 157,399 are Democrats and 86,229 are Republicans.3 Supervisor of Elections Karen Castor Dentel said the county was "surpassing the numbers all already for daily turnout"3 compared with the 2022 midterm. The University of Central Florida political scientist Aubrey Jewett told Florida Politics that in 2022 Democrats "were dispirited and did not show up while in 2026 they seem very determined and mobilized."4 Statewide, the Florida Phoenix cited the Fresh Take Florida Project's finding that approximately 68% of registered Democrats voted statewide, compared to 63% of Republicans5.

A county's move mixes who turned out with who changed sides, and the count alone cannot separate the two. Roughly 135,000 fewer people voted than in 2024, yet the Democratic margin grew from about 83,000 votes to about 134,600. That fits a more Democratic electorate. It cannot show how many voters actually switched.

There are signs that some did. Before the election, CNN noted that Puerto Ricans in Central Florida swung in huge numbers toward Trump in 20246. It also reported that many Venezuelans around Miami and Orlando were angry that Trump had ended their temporary protected status. NBC News' statewide exit poll, as reported by Newsweek, found that Puerto Rican voters gave Jolly 68 percent and Nixon 67 percent.7 Latino voters overall went 55% to 44% for the Democrats, after NBC's 2024 exit poll gave Trump 58 percent of them.7 These are statewide figures, not Orange County's. WKMG reported that the parties competed for Latino votes by clashing over the economy and immigration enforcement.8

Central Florida Public Media reported that voters were also galvanized by a divisive property tax ballot measure, Amendment 3, and by President Trump's unpopular governance over the past two years.9 WLRN found that a majority of Florida homeowners said it was property insurance, not property taxes that concerned them most.10

What it means for the county's politics

Before the vote, Orange County Democratic Party chair Samuel Vilchez Santiago told Florida Politics the county needed to "punch up above our weight"4 to make up for losses elsewhere. The county did its part, but the margin was not enough to win the state. The Florida Phoenix summed up the night as the near destruction of Republican power nationally, "Except in Florida."5

Local races went the Democrats' way too. Tiffany Moore Russell won the nonpartisan mayoral race over Republican Chris Messina, becoming the first Black woman elected Orange County mayor11. Kamia Brown, a Democrat, beat Mike Crabb, whom DeSantis appointed to the seat 19 days before the August Primary12. Under a charter change, the county commission will increase from seven voting members to nine13. Central Florida Public Media reported that Democratic Rep. Darren Soto, who currently represents Osceola and south Orange County9, claimed narrow victory9 in his race.

This year's count cannot say whether Orange County's bigger margin will hold when a presidential-year electorate returns.
