Manhattan stood still for Hochul while Brooklyn and Queens swung left

Kathy Hochul won New York County by almost exactly Kamala Harris's 2024 margin, while the state moved seven points toward Democrats. A neighborhood analysis finds she lost ground in the wealthy areas that a new pied-à-terre tax hits hardest.

What Manhattan did

Manhattan, which appears on the ballot and in the census as New York County, is the one big place in New York where this year's wave barely showed. With 89.4% of the expected vote counted, Gov. Kathy Hochul led Republican Bruce Blakeman, the Nassau County executive, 397,886 to 85,117, a margin of D+64.6. Kamala Harris carried the borough by D+64.8 against Donald Trump in 2024. That rounds to a move of zero. Strictly, it is two-tenths of a point toward Republicans.

The rest of the state looked different. Statewide, Hochul's D+19.6 margin ran 7 points ahead of Harris's D+12.7. Kings County (Brooklyn) moved 15 points left and Queens moved 20. Long Island's Suffolk and Nassau counties both flipped from Trump to narrow Hochul leads. Manhattan ranked 34th of New York's 62 counties by move. That puts it in the half of the state that stood still or drifted right, a group made up mostly of upstate counties.

Among the country's most Democratic big counties, the result stands out. DeKalb County, Georgia, matched Manhattan's 2024 margin exactly and then moved 13 points left this year. Philadelphia moved 20. Nationally, 2,673 of the 2,775 counties counted so far moved left, and the typical move was 14 points.

Manhattan is unlike the rest of the state. It has an estimated 1,664,862 residents on 22.66 square miles, and its median household income is $103,9311, compared with $85,974 for New York State2.

Why it held still

Start with turnout. The 484,204 votes counted so far equal 75% of the borough's 2024 total, and about a tenth of the expected vote is still out. Early voting was strong: amNY reported that Manhattan saw a 41% jump3 over the 2022 governor's race. County totals cannot separate voters who changed sides from changes in who turned out. So a flat Manhattan could mean a stable electorate, or offsetting changes in both.

Neighborhood data offers one clue. The City Reporter found that Hochul gained votes in 92% of neighborhoods in the city compared with her 2022 effort4. The exceptions were nearly all of them in Manhattan, in areas among the most exposed to the new pied-a-terre tax4. Her steepest drop was seven percentage points in Carnegie Hill in Manhattan's Upper East Side4, and Tribeca followed with a five percentage point decrease4. Those comparisons are with 2022, not with the 2024 baseline used on this map. The outlet also noted that most of those neighborhoods still voted heavily for Hochul.

Hochul proposed the tax in April, alongside Mayor Zohran Mamdani. It targets luxury second homes in New York City valued at $5 million or more5 whose owners live outside the city. The Legislature approved it on May 27 as part of the state budget.6 The City Reporter said it has since faced multiple legal challenges4.

Blakeman also campaigned in the borough. CBS News New York reported that he spent part of the day meeting with business owners in Manhattan's Chinatown7, where he criticized taxes and congestion pricing. amNY reported that he campaigned in Harlem3 before his final weekend. His closing message criticized Hochul's relationship with Mamdani8. He claimed the two had a pact to raise taxes, an argument that Hochul has rejected8.

Another explanation looks at where Democrats had ground to recover. New York Focus quoted Alex Elmasri, a consultant with the Parkside Group, who said Hochul's downstate gains owed mainly to traditional blue voters who returned to the party this year after defecting to support Trump two years ago9. In 2024, Gothamist reported that Trump's sharpest city gains came in the Bronx and Queens10. That fits Queens' large rebound this year, though no reporting so far has tied Manhattan's flat result directly to a smaller 2024 loss.

What it means

Manhattan remains one of the most Democratic big counties in the country, and its margin is almost exactly where it was. The open question is its wealthiest blocks, which drifted away from a Democratic governor during a Democratic wave. They also sit at the center of Albany's next fight over money. amNY reported that Hochul has resisted increasing taxes on wealthy New Yorkers and corporations11, while Mamdani argues that the election strengthened the case for doing so. Since the vote, she has signaled more openness. Asked about income tax increases on the wealthiest New Yorkers, she said she was "willing to do what has to happen" in "extraordinary" circumstances12.

The borough's count is not finished. With about a tenth of the expected vote outstanding, its final margin could still shift slightly in either direction.
