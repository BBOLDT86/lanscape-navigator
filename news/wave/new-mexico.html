Every New Mexico county moved left as Luján won by 26 points

Sen. Ben Ray Luján beat a Republican who got on the ballot through write-in votes. Every county moved toward the Democrats and five Trump counties flipped, a bigger shift than in Deb Haaland's governor's race.

Kamala Harris carried New Mexico by 6.0 points in 2024. On Nov. 3, Sen. Ben Ray Luján won a second term by 26.5 points, 458,756 votes to 266,434 for Republican Larry Marker. That is the AP count with 98.47 percent of the expected vote tallied as of Saturday morning. It is a 20.5-point move toward the Democrats, and it reached every one of the state's 33 counties.

The typical New Mexico county moved 18.9 points left. Across the 2,775 counties The Watch has counted nationally, the typical move was 14.1. Five counties that voted for Donald Trump in 2024 voted for Luján: Valencia, Luna, Socorro, Colfax and Hidalgo. None went the other way. The results stay unofficial until the canvass is done.

A write-in nominee, and a closer race for governor

Nobody expected the Senate race to be close. The Republican Party of New Mexico failed to officially field a candidate, so Marker, a Roswell Republican, ran as a write-in1, Source NM reported. His write-in campaign drew more than 31,000 votes, qualifying him for the general election2. The Albuquerque Journal said Luján's path back to Washington was "practically paved with rose petals."2

That matters when reading the map, because it is drawn from the Senate race. The open governor's race moved much less. Democrat Deb Haaland beat Gregg Hull, the former mayor of Rio Rancho, by 14.7 points, an 8.7-point move from 2024. The two races drew almost the same number of ballots, yet Hull got about 43,000 more votes than Marker, and Haaland about 42,600 fewer than Luján. A sizable group of voters chose both Luján and Hull. Haaland, the first Native American woman elected governor in U.S. history3, also had far more money: about $20.5 million in contributions compared to his $2.8 million4, according to the Santa Fe New Mexican.

The national mood shaped both races. Pollster Brian Sanderoff told the Journal that many Democrats in New Mexico were motivated to vote partially due to national politics and their anger or frustration with Donald Trump5. Source NM reported that Democratic turnout, predominately in the state's most populous counties, exceeded that of 20226, while unaffiliated or Decline to State voters6 turned out in relatively low numbers.

From the oil patch to Gallup

The biggest move came in Guadalupe County, around Santa Rosa. It went from a near-tie, Harris by 0.7, to Luján by 37.5, a 37-point swing on fewer than 1,500 ballots. McKinley County, which takes in Gallup and much of New Mexico's share of the Navajo Nation, moved 33 points. So did Luna County, around Deming, which went from Trump by 18.9 to Luján by 13.9.

The south moved hard. Doña Ana County (Las Cruces) went from Harris by 9.8 to Luján by 35.3, a 26-point move, and Socorro and Hidalgo counties flipped. Sierra and Otero counties stayed Republican, but by much less than before. In the same region, Rep. Gabe Vasquez won a third term. In an AP story about Latino voters moving left nationally, he said, "We showed our power and flexed our muscle during this election."7

The heavily Hispanic north moved too: Mora 28 points, Rio Arriba 27, San Miguel 24. In the Permian Basin, Lea County moved 24 points and still gave Marker a 38-point margin. Chaves County, Marker's home, moved 19, close to the state's median.

Bernalillo County cast 249,767 ballots, about a third of the state's total. It moved 17 points and went for Luján by 38.0. The smallest moves came in places that were already blue: Los Alamos (10 points), Santa Fe (15) and Sandoval (14). In Sandoval, the Journal reported, the governor's race was close8 in the county where Hull had served as mayor of Rio Rancho.

Who moved: turnout or persuasion?

Counties are not voters. A county count cannot tell people who changed their minds from people who stayed home. New Mexico cast 725,190 ballots, down from 923,403 in 2024, or 79 percent of the presidential turnout.

The charts show that mid-size counties moved most. The 11 counties casting 10,000 to 50,000 votes moved 22.2 points, weighted by votes. The four largest, with 62 percent of the vote, moved 17.4, and the 18 smallest moved 20.6. Counties that were close in 2024 moved more than the bluest ones. Socorro, the only county Trump won by less than 10, moved 28. The five Harris won by less than 10 moved a typical 25.5. The three bluest moved 15.1, and the 13 counties Trump won by 30 or more moved 19.4.

Turnout gives a mixed signal. Eighteen counties cast 65 to 80 percent of their 2024 vote, and they moved 23.0 points, weighted. The 14 counties that kept above 80 percent moved 16.7. Lea, the only county below 65 percent, moved 24. That pattern fits Republican-leaning voters sitting out. But across all 33 counties, the correlation between turnout change and move is -0.08, close to zero. Uneven turnout may explain part of the shift. The data cannot rule out persuasion, though, and the gap between Luján and Haaland shows that some voters split their tickets.
