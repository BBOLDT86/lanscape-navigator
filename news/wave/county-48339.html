In Montgomery County, Trump's biggest 2024 margin shrank by more than half

The county north of Houston that gave Donald Trump his largest raw-vote win in 2024 still voted for Ken Paxton. His margin was 22 points, down from Trump's 45.5, and about 57,000 votes, down from about 140,000.

Montgomery County is home to Conroe and The Woodlands, and it carries a particular reputation in Texas politics. In 2024 it provided the largest margin, by raw votes, to President Donald Trump of any U.S. county1, the Texas Tribune noted before this year's election. On Nov. 3 it stayed Republican, but by much less. With 99% of the expected vote counted, Republican Ken Paxton led Democrat James Talarico 155,781 to 98,324 in the U.S. Senate race, a margin of R+22.2. Trump carried the county by R+45.5 in 2024. In raw votes, the Republican lead fell from about 140,000 to about 57,500.

What Montgomery County did

The county moved 23 points toward the Democrats. That is well above the national typical move of 14 points and the typical Texas county's 18. It is still smaller than the state's own 26-point swing, which took Texas from Trump's R+13.7 to a D+12.1 win for Talarico. Every one of Texas's 254 counties moved left, and Montgomery ranked 68th by size of move. It moved exactly as far as Collin County, Paxton's home, which flipped from R+11.2 to D+12.1. It moved a bit more than Denton (21 points) and less than Harris (27). Big counties elsewhere that started at similar margins moved about half as far: Macomb County, Mich., moved 12 points and Suffolk County, N.Y., 11.

The county is also growing fast. Census Bureau estimates show it added +30,0112 residents from 2024 to 2025, the fourth-largest gain of any county, Fox 26 reported. The Houston Chronicle reported that voter registrations had risen 40% since 20183.

Who turned out, and who moved

The county cast 258,438 votes, 84% of its 2024 total, so about 48,700 fewer people voted. Talarico's 98,324 votes are more than Kamala Harris could have received in 2024 under any split of that year's vote: her ceiling was about 83,700. So the swing combines a smaller electorate with more Democratic votes. A county total cannot show how much came from Republicans staying home and how much from voters switching sides.

Republicans saw the risk coming. In a final stop in Conroe, Ted Cruz said he won his election against Beto O'Rourke in 2018 because of this county4, and he warned that Democrats could well win every single statewide position in Texas5, Nexstar reported. Three weeks earlier, county GOP chair Gwen Withrow told the Chronicle that her initial concerns about low enthusiasm among Republicans have been put to bed3.

After the vote, University of Houston political scientist Brandon Rottinghaus told KHOU that Republican turnout and party unity were major problems6. In his words, Republicans "weren't able to unify around their candidates and didn't come out to vote in big numbers"6. The Chronicle's map analysis listed Montgomery among the Houston suburbs that were some of the most important difference-makers7. It said Talarico spent much of that same time reaching out to independents and disaffected Republicans7. Statewide, Cruz said afterward that Republicans lost ground with evangelical Christians who didn't show up with the same numbers and fervor as prior elections8.

Money showed a similar pattern before the vote. A Houston Chronicle review of federal filings found that across four Woodlands-area ZIP codes, Talarico raised $238,622 to Paxton's $39,2819.

On issues, the Chronicle described Republicans closing the campaign amid growing discontent with Republicans over sky-high gas prices, an unpopular war with Iran and a violent immigration crackdown10. NBC's exit poll found that majorities of Senate voters in Georgia, Ohio, Michigan and Texas all said they disapprove of Trump's handling of his job11. Rottinghaus pointed to concerns about the economy, opposition to the direction of U.S. foreign policy and dissatisfaction with Trump's presidency6. None of these surveys was taken in Montgomery County alone.

What it means here

At the county level, the result held. Montgomery County Judge Mark Keough easily defended his seat against Democratic challenger James Graf12, according to unofficial results. Woodlands Online reported that Republicans won the area's state House seats and that the county maintained its historical red-centric voting tradition13. The Tribune noted the county has voted red in every U.S. Senate race in Texas since 19881, and that is still true.

What changed is the county's place in statewide math. The Chronicle had called it the most important red county in Texas3, and its Republican lead fell by about 82,000 votes in two years. Rottinghaus suggested the results could signal a longer-term political realignment if Republicans continue losing support in these communities14. That depends on whether this year's missing voters come back. Results remain unofficial until the county's canvass, and about 1% of the expected vote is still to be counted.
