Crowley wins Wisconsin by 8 as Brown, Kenosha and Racine flip

David Crowley's win for governor moved 71 of Wisconsin's 72 counties toward Democrats compared with 2024. Milwaukee and the mid-sized cities led the shift, while Tom Tiffany's Northwoods home barely moved.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026, from the count as of Nov. 7, 2026; the tables below refresh with the count (last Nov. 7, 11:51 AM ET, AP via The New York Times), and the piece is rewritten when a race is called or a margin moves more than a point. 13 sources. David Crowley, the Milwaukee County executive, beat Republican U.S. Rep. Tom Tiffany for governor by 8.0 points, 1,440,989 votes to 1,227,675, with 98.85% of the expected vote counted. Donald Trump carried Wisconsin by 0.9 points in 2024, so the state moved 8.9 points toward the Democrats. Crowley will be the first Black person to hold the office in the history of the state1, and WPR reported it is the biggest margin of victory in a race for Wisconsin governor since 19982.

The shift reached 71 of the state's 72 counties. Seven that Trump won two years ago went to Crowley: Brown, Racine, Winnebago, Kenosha, Sauk, Columbia and Vernon. None went the other way. Still, Wisconsin moved less than the country did. The typical county here shifted about 6 points toward Democrats, while the typical county nationally shifted 14.

Crowley's path was not smooth. WisPolitics reported that he won the Aug. 11 primary by 3,673 votes after dropping out and then re-entering, and that Tiffany and Republicans, who outspent Crowley and his backers nearly 2-to-13, put $123.6 million into the race. A Marquette Law School poll found that 45 percent of Wisconsin voters cited inflation and the cost of living as their most important issue4. The Associated Press reported that Crowley hammered Tiffany on economic issues, saying his loyalty to Trump and support of the war in Iran was to blame for the high gas prices5. Tiffany tried to portray Crowley as weak on crime and said Crowley would increase taxes5. The Wisconsin Examiner noted that some ads urged viewers not to let Crowley make the rest of Wisconsin look like Milwaukee6. The AP wrote that Tiffany was weighed down by the president's record-low approval numbers in Wisconsin7.

Milwaukee first, then the mid-sized cities

Milwaukee County had the biggest move in the state, nearly 14 points, going from D+38.5 to D+52.0 on 356,762 votes. Dane County (Madison) went from D+51.7 to D+60.9. Each county gave Crowley a lead of roughly 185,000 to 190,000 votes. Together those leads were bigger than his statewide margin of 213,314, so Tiffany led the other 70 counties by about 160,000. John Johnson of Marquette Law School found that Crowley ran well ahead of Tony Evers in the state's majority non-white Assembly districts, noting that all of the non-white majority districts are in Milwaukee8.

The mid-sized cities came next. Eau Claire County moved nearly 13 points, La Crosse and Kenosha 12 each, Portage (Stevens Point) 11 and Rock (Janesville and Beloit) 10. Kenosha went from a 6.2-point Trump win to a 5.7-point Crowley win. Next door in Racine, the margin went from R+6.2 to D+2.2. It's the first time that Racine County has voted in a Democratic governor since 2002.9 In the Fox Valley, Brown County (Green Bay) went from R+7.5 to D+2.1, and Winnebago County (Oshkosh), with 95% counted, went from R+4.8 to D+5.1. Outagamie County (Appleton) moved nearly as far but stopped at R+0.7.

The hilly farm country of the southwest moved too. Crawford County went from R+13.8 to R+1.9, a 12-point move. Grant moved 11 points, Trempealeau 10, and Vernon flipped narrowly to D+0.3. Before the election, WPR reported that the cost of diesel fuel is breaking records. Tariffs and trade wars are hurting crop sales10. But farmers were not all of one mind. Wausau Pilot & Review found that the president of the Grant County Farm Bureau Federation said he's voting for Van Orden11, the Republican congressman.

Who moved

Counties are not voters. The charts beside this section sort places, not people. With that caution, the pattern is clear: the bigger the county, the bigger the move. The 22 counties that cast fewer than 10,000 votes, 6% of the statewide total, moved 5.4 points (weighted by votes). The 35 counties casting 10,000 to 50,000 votes moved 6.5. The 13 counties casting 50,000 to 250,000 votes, 39% of the total, moved 7.9. Milwaukee and Dane, a quarter of the vote between them, moved 11.5.

The 2024 result shows the same pattern. The 20 counties Trump won by 30 points or more moved 4.5. The 32 he won by 10 to 30 moved 6.0. The seven he won by less than 10 moved 9.5, and the eight Kamala Harris won narrowly moved 10.1. Democrats gained the most where they were already close or ahead. The reddest counties gave up the least.

Turnout fell everywhere. The 2,668,664 votes cast for governor were 78% of the 2024 total. Johnson counted 776,000 fewer voters than showed up in November 20248. But how far turnout fell did not track how far a county moved. The 61 counties that cast 65% to 80% of their 2024 vote moved 8.2 points, and the 11 that cast 80% to 95% also moved 8.2. The correlation between the two is essentially zero (r = -0.06). Kenosha cast 74% of its 2024 vote and moved 12 points; Dane cast 85% and moved 9. County totals cannot tell voters who changed their minds from differences in who showed up within each county. WPR's comparison with the last midterm suggests both played a part: close to 2.7 million votes were cast, about the same number as in 2022, but Crowley got almost 80,000 more votes than Evers, and Tiffany got about 47,000 fewer votes than Michels2. Johnson's district-level analysis, which compares Crowley with Evers in 2018 rather than with Trump in 2024, found that in the most educated parts of the state, Crowley won 8 percentage points more of the vote8. Crowley did slightly worse in the least-educated places, and his improvement over Evers was lowest in the oldest places8.

Where the map barely moved

The smallest moves came in Tiffany's home region. He is Republican U.S. Rep. Tom Tiffany of Minocqua1, and it was the first time he had run statewide12. Vilas County moved less than a point, from R+23.1 to R+22.9. Oneida County, which includes Minocqua, moved 3 points. Iron, Burnett, Adams and Marquette counties all moved 3 points or less.

The Milwaukee suburbs moved, but modestly. Washington County moved 3 points and stayed at R+33.4. Waukesha County, the largest county Tiffany carried, moved 5 points to R+15.2 on 218,445 votes. Milwaukee Magazine reported that Butler and the city of Waukesha were the latest Waukesha County communities to flip blue13. Ozaukee County moved 5 points to R+5.1. Door County, which Harris won narrowly, moved only 3.

Only Menominee County moved toward Republicans. It shares its borders with the Menominee Indian Reservation and is the most Democratic county in the state. It went from D+61.7 to D+57.9, a 4-point move, on just 1,179 votes. With numbers that small, a few hundred voters can swing the margin, and local reporting has not yet explained the shift.

The count is not finished: Winnebago stood at 95% and Sheboygan at 97%, and WXPR noted that all results are unofficial until they're certified12.
