Helena and Livingston flip as Montana shifts left, but Alme wins

Independent Seth Bodnar ran 9 points better than Kamala Harris did in 2024 and carried two counties Trump had won. Republican Kurt Alme still won by about 11 points while Bodnar and the Democratic nominee split the opposition.

Kurt Alme, a Republican, will take the Senate seat Steve Daines is giving up. The Associated Press called the race shortly before 1 a.m. Wednesday.1 With 97.65 percent of the expected vote counted, Alme leads independent Seth Bodnar 232,916 to 181,674. That is a margin of 10.6 points. Donald Trump carried Montana by 19.9 points in 2024, so the state moved 9.3 points away from the Republican.

On this map the Democratic side is Bodnar, because he finished ahead of the Democratic nominee, Alani Bankhead. Bodnar has about 37.7 percent of the vote and Alme about 48.3 percent. The other 14 percent went to Bankhead, Libertarian Kyle Austin and minor candidates. The margin here measures Alme against his strongest challenger. It is not a two-party result.

Fifty-two of Montana's 56 counties moved toward Bodnar's side. The typical county moved 10.4 points. That is smaller than the typical move of 14.1 points across the 2,775 counties The Watch has counted nationally.

A seat that changed hands in March

The race changed suddenly in March. Daines withdrew his Senate reelection paperwork minutes before the deadline — and minutes after U.S. attorney Kurt Alme filed to run for the seat himself.2 President Donald Trump quickly endorsed Alme3. Alme is a former state budget director under Gov. Greg Gianforte and was twice appointed U.S. attorney for Montana by Trump1. Bodnar resigned as president of the University of Montana to run as an independent4 and filed the same day.

Democrats spent the year divided over the race. Montana Free Press reported that four of the state Democratic Party's five most recent chairs endorsed Bodnar5, and so did Republicans such as former Gov. Marc Racicot. One of those chairs, Shannon O'Brien, resigned from the job in October to back him. Bankhead repeatedly said she would not drop out6. Montana Public Radio wrote that the race was defined by Bodnar and Bankhead fighting over a similar voting base7.

The campaign was fought mostly over costs, trade and the war. Bodnar argued that Alme would rubber-stamp Trump's agenda, particularly the war with Iran, which Alme has said he supports5. He also called for ending the trade war with Canada, Montana's largest agricultural trading partner5. Alme answered that an independent who did not caucus with either party would leave Montana "seriously underrepresented"8 because the Senate's work is done in committees. KTVQ reported that spending on the race did not reach the levels of Montana's Senate elections in 2018, 2020 or 20249.

Where the map moved

The largest moves came in small ranching counties that are still heavily Republican. Judith Basin County moved 19 points, and Sweet Grass, Powder River and Liberty counties moved about 17 each. Garfield County moved 15 points and still went to Alme by 74.

Two counties flipped. Lewis and Clark County, home of Helena, went from Trump by 5.7 to Bodnar by 7.5. Park County, around Livingston, went from Trump by 7.7 to Bodnar by 2.2. About 96 percent of Park's expected vote has been counted, so that lead is narrow and the count is not final.

The large counties moved in the same direction. Missoula County went from D+21.4 to D+32.1 and Gallatin County from D+3.2 to D+12.0. Yellowstone County, around Billings, where Alme declared victory1, went from R+27.1 to R+18.4. The northwest moved least: Flathead County by 6 points and Lincoln County by less than 1. The Daily Montanan found the same leftward drift in the U.S. House races, which swung left in nearly every county10 compared with 2024.

Four counties moved toward Alme: Blaine (down 9 points), Glacier (down 8), Roosevelt (down 7) and Big Horn (down 4). All four contain reservations. Big Horn includes the Crow Reservation11, Glacier the Blackfeet Reservation11, Roosevelt most of Fort Peck12, and Blaine includes Fort Belknap. Lake, Rosebud and Hill counties, which also include reservation land, are among the smallest moves left.

Turnout fell more than average in some of these places. Roosevelt cast 65 percent of its 2024 vote and Blaine 72 percent, against 80 percent statewide. The county count cannot show why these counties moved. Because the measure counts only Bodnar against Alme, a county where Bankhead or a minor candidate drew a larger share would also look like a move toward Alme. These were not good areas for Republicans in every race: Montana Free Press reported that the 2027 American Indian Caucus will be the largest cohort yet13.

Who moved: turnout or persuasion?

Counties are not voters, and a county's move does not show whether people changed their minds or whether different people voted. The 482,014 votes cast so far are 80 percent of Montana's 2024 total. The Daily Montanan still reported that the turnout was the second highest of any midterm, surpassed by only 201810.

The move was spread evenly by county size. Counties with fewer than 10,000 votes, 47 of the 56, moved 8.5 points weighted by votes. The five mid-sized counties moved 10.5. The four largest, which cast nearly half the vote, moved 8.6.

The reddest places moved as much as anywhere. The 42 counties Trump won by 30 points or more moved 9.1 points weighted, and the typical one moved 11.3. Among the five counties Trump won by less than 10, the typical move was 3.6 points toward Alme. Three of those five are the reservation counties that moved right, while Lewis and Clark and Park moved left and outweigh them in votes.

Turnout explains part of the pattern but not the direction. The 30 counties that cast less than 80 percent of their 2024 vote moved 8.8 points, and the 26 that held between 80 and 95 percent moved 9.1. Across counties, the correlation between keeping turnout and moving left is +0.40. That is a modest relationship, and it runs the opposite way from a shift driven only by Republicans staying home. The counties that kept more of their voters moved somewhat more toward Bodnar. The Daily Montanan had found lifelong Republicans backing Bodnar14 before the vote. The county totals are consistent with some crossover but cannot measure it. Counting and canvassing are still under way.
