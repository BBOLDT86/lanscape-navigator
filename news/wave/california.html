California moves 10 points left as inland counties swing back to Democrats

Xavier Becerra won the governor's race by about 30 points with 71 percent of the expected vote counted. Nine counties Donald Trump carried in 2024, from Riverside to Imperial, now show Democratic leads.

Xavier Becerra, a Democrat and former state attorney general and U.S. health secretary, will succeed Gov. Gavin Newsom. He defeated Republican Steve Hilton, a former political adviser in the United Kingdom and Fox News host.1 The Associated Press declared Becerra the winner shortly after polls closed.2 He will be California's first Latino governor since the late 19th century.3

As of Saturday morning, with about 71 percent of the expected vote counted, Becerra leads 6,378,410 to 3,404,640, a margin of 30.4 points. Kamala Harris carried the state by 20.1 points in 2024, so the statewide margin has moved about 10 points toward the Democrats. Fifty-four of California's 58 counties moved that way. Marin, San Mateo and El Dorado moved slightly toward Hilton, and Amador did not move at all. Nine counties Trump won in 2024 now show Democratic leads: Riverside, San Bernardino, San Joaquin, Fresno, Stanislaus, Merced, Imperial, Lake and Inyo. No county went the other way. California counts slowly, so every figure here will change.

The swing was real but smaller than in much of the country. The typical California county moved about 6 points; nationally the typical county moved 14.

A quiet campaign, run against Trump

Becerra did not have to do much. Axios reported that after his June primary win he debated Hilton once and did a few media interviews and very little campaigning.4 Mike Madrid, a Republican consultant, told Axios that Becerra's win is largely because of anti-Republican, anti-Trump feelings in California.4

LAist reported that Hilton struggled to rid himself of the baggage from President Donald Trump's endorsement2. Meanwhile Becerra cast himself as a fighter who would, like Newsom, challenge decisions in Washington such as Trump's aggressive deportation campaign.2 Hilton argued one-party rule was to blame for the state's high cost of living and homelessness crisis.1 According to CalMatters, he proposed to eliminate state income tax on the first $150,000 of Californians' earnings5 and to roll back the state's climate rules. The last Emerson poll, taken in mid-October, places Becerra 27 percentage points ahead6.

The Valley and the Inland Empire swing back

The biggest moves came in the farm counties and in Southern California's inland suburbs. Imperial County, on the Mexican border, went from a 0.9-point Trump win to a 31-point Becerra lead. That 32-point move is the largest in the state. Kern County moved 20 points and is now nearly even. Merced moved 20 points and flipped. San Bernardino County moved 19 points to a 17-point Democratic lead, and Riverside moved 17 points to a 16-point lead. Tulare, Madera, Kings, San Joaquin and Stanislaus each moved about 17 points.

Los Angeles County moved 12 points, to D+45.1, and is far from the biggest mover. It still matters most to the total: its 2.7 million votes are more than a quarter of everything counted.

Reporting from the Valley points to immigration enforcement and prices. Al Jazeera reported that residents and officials said ICE raids sent shockwaves through immigrant and Latino communities, with arrests of farmworkers and family members7. In the same region, Democrat Randy Villegas defeated Republican incumbent David Valadao in the race for California's 22nd congressional district7. Fresno's ABC30 reported that immigration emerged as a major issue during the campaign, particularly as concerns mounted over increased ICE activity across the Central Valley.8 ABC7 cited experts who said high gas and grocery prices, as well as immigration enforcement and deportation policies, were among the issues that drove Latino voters to the polls.9

Who moved: counties Trump barely won, and the question of turnout

The charts beside this section sort counties, not voters. They show where the vote moved, not which people changed their minds.

The largest swing came in places Trump won narrowly. Eleven counties he carried by less than 10 points hold 15 percent of the vote. Weighted by votes they moved 15.4 points, and the typical one moved 13.6. Counties Trump won by 10 to 30 points moved 12.7 points weighted but only 5.8 typically. That gap shows Kern and Tulare pulling far ahead of foothill counties like Calaveras and Tuolumne, which barely moved. The 12 bluest counties hold almost half the vote and moved 8.6 points weighted, mostly because of Los Angeles. The typical one moved just 4.4. By size, counties casting 10,000 to 50,000 votes moved most, 11.1 points weighted.

Turnout is harder to read. So far 2026 votes equal 62 percent of the 2024 total. Counties below 65 percent moved 9.3 points weighted and 9.2 typically. Those between 80 and 95 percent moved 5.8 weighted and 2.8 typically. The correlation between turnout and move is -0.34. But part of that pattern is the count itself: counties that have counted less look like low-turnout counties. Lake County, for example, has counted 35 percent of its expected vote.

Counties that are nearly done counting give a cleaner view. Kings County, 94 percent counted, has cast 79 percent of its 2024 total and moved 17 points. Amador, 99 percent counted, has cast 84 percent and did not move. The swing is not only an artifact of the count. Still, county totals cannot say whether Trump's 2024 voters switched sides or stayed home. The data block has no census measures, so it also cannot confirm on its own the reporting about Latino voters.

The exceptions are in the Bay Area. Marin moved 4.8 points toward Hilton and San Mateo moved 1.9, though both are still at 52 and 63 percent counted. Santa Clara barely moved. Hilton and his wife live in the affluent Silicon Valley community of Atherton10, in San Mateo County. The region also fought hard over Proposition 40, the billionaire tax, which Newsom, his successor, Xavier Becerra, and San Francisco Mayor Daniel Lurie have opposed11. Nothing in the count ties either fact to the result, and late ballots could still change it.
