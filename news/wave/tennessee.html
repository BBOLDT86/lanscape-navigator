Hagerty holds Tennessee as Murfreesboro, Clarksville and Chattanooga swing away

Bill Hagerty won a second Senate term by 12 points, but all 95 counties moved toward the Democrats from 2024, and three metro counties (Rutherford, Montgomery and Hamilton) narrowly went for Marquita Bradshaw.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026, from the count as of Nov. 7, 2026; the tables below refresh with the count (last Nov. 13, 2:59 PM ET, AP via The New York Times), and the piece is rewritten when a race is called or a margin moves more than a point. 10 sources. Bill Hagerty, the Republican senator, won a second term on Tuesday. He beat Marquita Bradshaw by 12.2 points, 1,138,457 votes to 886,974, with 99 percent of the expected vote counted. Two years ago Donald Trump carried Tennessee over Kamala Harris by 29.7 points. That is a 17.6-point move toward the Democrats, and none of the state's 95 counties moved the other way. The typical county moved 13.7 points, a little less than the typical county nationally, which moved 14.1.

Nobody treated the race as close. Public radio station WKMS reported in August that Hagerty faced no Republican challengers in the primary, has President Trump's endorsement and his fundraising totals dwarfed those of Democrats.1 The Nashville Banner reported that Hagerty raised more than $13 million for the current election cycle since 20212, while Bradshaw raised around $38,000.2 She is a Memphis environmental activist, and this was a rematch of their 2020 race. Democrats haven't won a U.S. Senate election in Tennessee since Al Gore in 1990.1

Three metro counties cross the line

Rutherford County, home to Murfreesboro, moved the most of any county in the state: 22 points, from a 21.8-point Trump win to a 0.2-point edge for Bradshaw. The Murfreesboro Daily News Journal wrote that a 16-year Republican dominance in Rutherford County shifted to Democrats Nov. 3 for federal and state offices.3 WPLN reported that three of the six statehouse seats that flipped Democratic in Tuesday's election were in Rutherford County.4

Montgomery County, which includes Clarksville, went from Trump by 18.4 to Bradshaw by 0.1. Clarksville Online described its Senate race as essentially a tie.5 In Hamilton County (Chattanooga), the margin went from Trump by 13.1 to Bradshaw by 2.6. The Chattanooga Times Free Press reported that Democrats carry the county in the races for governor, U.S. Senate and U.S. House on the same night for the first time in 36 years.6

Knox County (Knoxville) moved 18 points, the same as the state as a whole, but stopped short of flipping: Hagerty kept it by 1.9. Davidson County (Nashville) moved 17 points to give Bradshaw a 44.8-point margin. Shelby County (Memphis), which cast the most votes, moved a smaller 12 points. Williamson County, south of Nashville and the fifth-largest county by votes, moved only 9 points and is still Republican by 23.7. The smallest move in the state was in Cumberland County: 6 points, leaving it at R+53.3. Not every rural county moved by little, though. Warren, Hamblen and Bedford counties each moved about 19 points.

Who moved

Counties are not voters, so the charts beside this section show places, not people. Even so, the pattern is clear. The nine counties with 50,000 to 250,000 votes cast 55 percent of the vote and moved 15.6 points when weighted by votes. The typical one moved 17.0. The 56 counties with fewer than 10,000 votes cast 15 percent of the vote and moved 12.6. The largest moves came in six counties Trump won by 10 to 30 points: the four metro counties above, plus Madison (Jackson) and Hardeman. Together they moved 17.9 points. The 86 counties Trump won by 30 or more moved 13.6, but they still cast 54 percent of the vote, which is why Hagerty won comfortably. Shelby and Davidson, the two counties Harris won by 10 to 30 points, moved 14.4.

Turnout is the main caveat. Tennessee cast 67.5 percent as many votes as in 2024, so about a third of the presidential electorate was missing. That gap is easily large enough to change a margin even if no one changed their mind. The county counts cannot tell the two explanations apart, but they argue against a simple story of Republicans staying home. The 73 counties where turnout fell below 65 percent of the 2024 total moved 14.1 points. The 22 where it held better moved 15.1. The correlation between a county's turnout and its move is effectively zero (r = +0.03). Scott County cast less than half its 2024 vote and still moved 16 points.

A closer race for governor, and the reasons offered

The governor's race was much closer. Marsha Blackburn beat Jerri Green, a Memphis city councilwoman, by 2.5 points, a 27.2-point move from 2024. Nearly one governor's vote in nine went to someone other than the two nominees, compared with about one in fifty in the Senate race. WKRN reported that one of Blackburn's toughest hurdles that complicated her race was having a conservative-leaning independent with David Hatley7 in the race. The same report quoted Robb McDaniel, a political scientist at Middle Tennessee State University, who said this narrow vote doesn't mean Tennessee is turning blue.7

The Times Free Press reported that across the 14-day early voting period, 45 percent more people cast ballots in 2026 (nearly 1.3 million) than in 20228. It said turnout was comparable to the 2018 elections, which was the last time the governor's race lacked an incumbent.8 The paper also wrote that strong opposition to this year's frontrunner – and ultimate winner – Marsha Blackburn brought some to the polls8.

Explanations differ. NewsChannel 5 in Nashville reported that affordability and grassroots organizing9 were behind the Democrats' wins. Those included the 4th District, where Victoria Broderick narrowly beat Rep. Scott DesJarlais. Martin Daniel, the Knox County Republican chairman, told WVLT he believes inflation and the war in Iran caused the shift to the left.10 None of those explanations can be tested county by county. What the counts do show is that the move reached every corner of the state, from Memphis to Mountain City, even though Bradshaw had almost no money to spend on her campaign.
