Mike Flood holds eastern Nebraska seat by 3.5 points after overnight comeback

Republican Mike Flood trailed Democrat Chris Backemeyer for much of election night before rural ballots put him ahead. Every county in the district moved toward the Democratic side in the statewide races, and Sarpy County flipped.

Mike Flood kept his eastern Nebraska seat, but he spent most of election night trying to catch up. With 96.82% of the expected vote counted, the Republican had 127,001 votes to 118,135 for Democrat Chris Backemeyer. That is a lead of 8,866 votes, or 3.5 points, out of 254,139 cast, and it leaves Flood just short of half the total. AP has called the race. Flood said the race was called in his favor moments before a press conference at the Nebraska Republican Party headquarters, and that Backemeyer called to concede earlier that morning.1

A diplomat comes home

Backemeyer is a Lincoln native who, according to the Nebraska Examiner, is a former deputy assistant secretary of state for the Middle East2. He left his longtime post with the State Department when Trump began slashing the federal workforce3. The Lincoln Journal Star reported that he confronted Flood at a town hall in Lincoln in August 20253 before deciding to run against him.

He ran mainly on prices. Nebraska Public Media reported that he said Flood hasn't done enough to thwart rising costs and international trade disputes initiated by President Trump4. One of his ads played on Flood's last name, blaming his tariff votes for high prices.5 He also wanted the war in Iran ended but differed with Flood on how, arguing that only Congress, not the president, can declare war4.

Flood, a Norfolk resident and former speaker of the Legislature2, acknowledged the higher gas and fertilizer prices the Examiner tied to the war. He pointed to Trump's tax cuts and a landmark housing law he helped pass.6 In the closing weeks he questioned Backemeyer's decision to rent rather than buy a home in the district7, saying "Chris Backemeyer still has a lot of Washington in him."5 Backemeyer said renting reflected the cost of buying a home, a problem he said other Nebraskans share7.

Flood raised more than $2.7 million, compared to just over $1 million for Backemeyer4. Even so, the Omaha World-Herald reported that national Democrats had, for the first time in two decades, identified a Nebraska House contest outside of Omaha as worthy of investment8. In the fall, Cook Political Report and Inside Elections moved the race from safe to likely Republican6. At Flood's July town hall in Lincoln, people asked about the cost of the Iran war, health care, the Epstein files and immigration9.

Lincoln, Sarpy and the small towns

The district includes Lincoln, Bellevue, Fremont, Norfolk, Columbus, Papillion and La Vista, along with northeastern Polk and eastern Sarpy counties.4 The Examiner has called Lincoln's Democratic vote "often drowned out by the region's more conservative smaller cities and rural areas."10

The Watch's wave map compares the 2024 Harris–Trump vote with this year's Senate and governor's races. On that map, all 12 counties moved toward the Democratic side, by a weighted 15.7 points. The statewide moves were smaller: 14.3 points in the Senate race, where independent Dan Osborn was on the Democratic side, and 12.6 in the governor's race. Lancaster County, which holds Lincoln, went from D+4 to D+20. Sarpy County went from R+12 to D+4, the district's only flip. Those numbers, like Polk's, cover the whole county, though only part of it votes in the 1st. The Flatwater Free Press reported that Sarpy voted against Gov. Jim Pillen and U.S. Sen. Pete Ricketts11, and that Backemeyer carried the district's Sarpy County precincts11.

The rural counties moved almost as far, but they started from far more Republican ground. Colfax County, around Schuyler, moved 24 points, the most in the district, and still went R+27. Dodge County (Fremont) moved 17 points to R+16. Madison County, Flood's home, moved 15 points and still went R+41. Cuming County moved the least, 9 points, and stayed at R+51.

A shift on the map does not prove that voters were persuaded. Lancaster alone cast 127,622 votes in the statewide races, and KOLN reported that turnout there beat the county election commissioner's prediction12. A county count cannot separate voters who changed their minds from changes in who turned out.

That geography explains how the night went. KMTV reported that Flood trailed in early returns, but his count surged as rural ballots were tallied, and he overtook Backemeyer after midnight13.

A narrower hold

Flood was elected in 2022 during a special election14. He goes back to a House that Democrats will control: as of Saturday morning, 252 seats had been called for Democrats and 161 for Republicans, with 22 still open. In the neighboring 2nd District, Republican Brinker Harding lost to Democrat Denise Powell1. Flood said the party in power during a midterm can often face political "headwinds."4

The two minor candidates, Libertarian Nik Sandman and Dan Hartong of the America First Party4, won 4,611 and 4,392 votes. Together that is 9,003, slightly more than Flood's margin. The count cannot say how those voters would have voted otherwise.

The House race was closer than either statewide race: Ricketts won Nebraska by 6.2 points and Pillen by 8.0, though the state and the district have different electorates. Flood said it was tough to predict if the close results held any implications for future elections for the seat1, and told supporters there is more work to do on affordability15. About 3% of the expected vote is still to be counted, and Backemeyer has conceded.
