Collin County, Paxton's political home, swings 23 points toward Talarico

The fast-growing suburbs north of Dallas where Ken Paxton started his career went from an 11-point Trump county in 2024 to a 12-point James Talarico win. That matched the statewide margin exactly.

Collin County, which takes in Plano, Frisco, McKinney and Allen, is where Ken Paxton built his political career. It sent Ken Paxton to the state house and state senate, priming him to become State Attorney General and the Republican U.S. Senate nominee.1 On Nov. 3 it voted against him. Democrat James Talarico won the county 252,403 to 197,096, a margin of 12.1 points, with 99 percent of the expected vote counted. Two years ago, Donald Trump carried it over Kamala Harris by 11.2 points.

What Collin County did

That is a move of 23 points. Talarico's county margin was the same as his statewide margin, D+12.1. In 2024 Collin was about two and a half points more Democratic than Texas as a whole. This time the state moved 26 points, a little more than the county, so Paxton's home county ended up even with Texas rather than to its left. It ranks 69th of Texas' 254 counties by move. Every county in the state moved left, and the typical move was 18 points. Its neighbors moved about as much: Dallas 23 points, Tarrant 24 and Denton 22, where the margin went from R+13.2 to D+8.4. Big counties elsewhere that started near Collin moved less. Miami-Dade moved 19 points to D+7.1. Suffolk County, N.Y., moved 11. Macomb County, Mich., moved 12 and stayed Republican at R+1.8.

Before the vote, the Texas Tribune noted that Collin County is the most populous county in America that a statewide Democratic candidate has never won this century.2 It has also become one of the most diverse places in Texas — about 45% white, 23% Asian, 17% Hispanic and 12% Black2. The Dallas Morning News reported that more than 58% of Collin County residents have bachelor's degrees, compared with about 35% statewide3.

Who voted, and why

Collin cast 457,413 votes in the Senate race, 89 percent of its 2024 presidential total. That is high for a midterm, but it is still about 58,000 fewer voters than two years ago. A county count cannot separate voters who changed sides from changes in who showed up. Collin's move contains both. Results remain unofficial until canvassed4, the county elections office says.

Local reporting points to both. The Dallas Morning News reported that Democratic candidates credited the party's improved outreach strategy and voters mobilized by Republican policies on education and religion5. Chanda Parbhoo, who leads a group that organizes South Asian American voters, told the paper she saw an increase in participation driven by mounting anti-South Asian rhetoric in Collin County.5 Her group chartered a bus to bring students at UT Austin who are registered in Collin County back home to vote early.5 Before Election Day, the Tribune wrote that after months of anti-Indian attacks from GOP activists with little pushback from party officials, Indian American voters in Frisco and West Plano6 would be a group to watch. The Morning News editorial board noted that most of Frisco voted Democratic, particularly the east near the city's prominent Hindu temple7. It also cautioned that it cannot say what motivated individual Collin County voters.7

The candidate mattered too. The Republican-led Texas House impeached Paxton in 2023 on corruption and abuse-of-office allegations, but the Senate acquitted him.8 In 2025, his wife, state Sen. Angela Paxton, filed for divorce in July 2025, accusing him of adultery, an allegation he denied.8 In the AP Voter Poll, a statewide survey with no Collin breakout, about two-thirds of voters said Paxton did not have the right personal character to serve as a U.S. senator9. Republicans pointed to turnout on their own side. Sen. Ted Cruz said the party lost evangelical Christians who didn't show up with the same numbers and fervor as prior elections10.

What it means here

Further down the ballot the results were mixed. NBC DFW reported that former Plano council member Julie Holmer won a seat on the Commissioners Court, becoming the first Democrat elected to the court in decades.11 KERA reported that County Judge Chris Hill, a Republican, appears to have barely pulled ahead1 of his Democratic challenger. Republican state Rep. Matt Shaheen kept his seat. Voters also rejected the county's $817.5 million bond package to fund roads, courts and detention facilities12, columnist Tyra Damm wrote.

Republicans had said beforehand that the county was competitive. Shelby Williams, a former county GOP chair who lost the commission race to Holmer, told the Tribune: "If we can take it from them, they can take it right back from us.2" The current county chair, Byron Henry, said the suburbs continue attracting conservatives from Democratic-led states.3 The Morning News editorial board warned that Democrats would be naive to see the results and assume that Collin is now blue for good.7 The county Democratic Party is small. Its chair, Jeremy Sutka, said only half of the county precincts have Democratic chairs5 and the party has one paid staff member. He hopes Tuesday's results will lead to more funding for the county party in 20285.
