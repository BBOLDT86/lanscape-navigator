Brooklyn gives Hochul a 59-point margin, 15 points past Harris

Kings County moved 15 points toward the Democrats from the 2024 presidential race, more than twice New York's statewide shift, as Orthodox leaders backed Kathy Hochul and Democrats won back southern Brooklyn seats they had lost.

What Brooklyn did

Kings County is the borough of Brooklyn, the most populous of New York City's five boroughs. It stretches from brownstone neighborhoods and Hasidic Williamsburg in the north to the immigrant neighborhoods of southern Brooklyn. It cast more votes this year than any other county in New York, and the 16th-most of any county in the states that held a statewide race.

Gov. Kathy Hochul, the Democrat, beat Bruce Blakeman, the Republican Nassau County executive, in Brooklyn by 493,734 votes to 126,899. That is roughly 79 percent to 20 percent, a 59-point margin, with about 90 percent of the expected vote counted. In 2024 Kamala Harris won the county over Donald Trump by 44 points. The 15-point move toward the Democrats is more than double the statewide move of 7 points: Ms. Hochul leads New York by 19.6 points, where Ms. Harris won by 12.7. Brooklyn ranks fifth of the state's 62 counties by the size of its move.

New York's gain was concentrated downstate. Only half of the state's counties moved left, and the typical county did not move at all. Queens moved about 20 points. Manhattan, already at 65 points Democratic, did not move. Suffolk and Nassau both went narrowly to Ms. Hochul after voting for Mr. Trump. Brooklyn's move is close to the national typical move of 14 points and matches big Democratic counties like Fulton County, Ga. (14 points) and Cook County, Ill. (18 points). It is much bigger than the moves in Hennepin County, Minn., or Santa Clara County, Calif.

Turnout matters here. The 622,033 votes counted so far equal 74 percent of Brooklyn's 2024 total of 835,229. A county's move mixes two things: voters who changed sides, and changes in who showed up. The county count cannot separate them. With a quarter fewer ballots than in 2024 so far, uneven turnout alone could explain much of the shift.

Why: endorsements, Trump and the cost of living

Gothamist reported that Ms. Hochul won by relentlessly linking him to President Donald Trump1. CBS New York reported that Blakeman also bashed Hochul's alliance with Mamdani, saying her working relationship with the democratic socialist would lead to her undoing.2 According to WABC, Hochul appeared alongside Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and campaigned in the city, while Blakeman focused his efforts on more moderate neighborhoods and suburban counties.3 Before the election, a Quinnipiac poll found that the cost of living was the top issue named by 28 percent of likely voters4. That poll covered the whole state. The Watch found no voter survey of Brooklyn alone.

Brooklyn's early voters turned out in force. The Brooklyn Paper reported that Brooklyn led the five boroughs with 219,536 early voters5. The paper also reported that day-of voter turnout is about on-par with the last midterm election in 2022, though early voting turnout in Kings County was up about 60%5. It found that older voters made up the largest share: ages 60+ led the charge in Brooklyn, per BOE data, and made up around 28% of early votes6.

In Brooklyn's Orthodox Jewish neighborhoods, the leadership changed sides. City & State reported that these communities turned out in big numbers against the governor in 20227. This year, both competing Satmar sects in Williamsburg7, Chabad-Lubavitch leaders in Crown Heights and the Bobov sect, the largest voting bloc in Borough Park7 endorsed her. Orthodox insiders told City & State that enthusiasm for Blakeman is low in such circles7. Mr. Blakeman disputed that. Mr. Blakeman did not lose everywhere. Documented, which covers immigrant New York, found he still had a stronghold of support in several dozen census tracts in South Brooklyn where over half the population is foreign-born.8

In Bensonhurst, Documented reported that many Chinese immigrant voters told Documented that issues that directly affect their daily lives, such as affordability, have taken precedence9 over broader political debates. Some voters also told the outlet they were worried about public safety.

What it means for Brooklyn's politics

The clearest local result was down the ballot. City & State reported that Democrats swept four state legislative races in southern Brooklyn10, winning back one of the few parts of New York City the party had lost their grip on in recent years.10 Southern Brooklyn did not swing entirely, though: Republican Rep. Nicole Malliotakis retained New York's 11th Congressional District11, which reaches from Staten Island into Bay Ridge and Bensonhurst.

Republicans blamed national conditions. Liam McCabe, the Brooklyn Republican chairman, called it a "Democratic blizzard" fueled by "national headwinds."12 He said turnout had been very low. But the Brooklyn Paper found that in the four contested districts in southern Brooklyn, turnout was about equal to the 2022 Red Wave, BOE records show.12 McCabe said the party would try to rebuild by registering new voters and recruiting new prospective candidates.12

Democrats won without a united county party. City & State noted that the southern Brooklyn wins came even as the Brooklyn Democratic Party is mired in a civil war.10 About a tenth of Brooklyn's expected vote is still being counted, so these margins will change before the results are certified.
