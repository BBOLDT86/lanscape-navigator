Miami-Dade backs Nixon by 7 as Moody holds Florida's Senate seat

Florida's biggest county by votes cast moved 19 points toward Democrats from 2024, the fourth-largest swing in the state. Black and white neighborhoods led it; Hispanic precincts still leaned Republican, by less, on a high midterm turnout.

In Miami-Dade County, Democrat Angie Nixon beat Sen. Ashley Moody by 415,941 votes to 359,981, a margin of 7.1 points, with 99% of the expected vote counted. Two years ago, Donald Trump carried the county by 11.4 points over Kamala Harris. That is a 19-point move. It did not change the statewide result. Moody, a Republican, was appointed to the seat after President Donald Trump nominated Marco Rubio as secretary of state1, and she won Florida by 2.9 points. In the governor's race, Byron Donalds beat David Jolly by 1.3 points.

What Miami-Dade did

Miami-Dade is not a typical Florida county. The Census Bureau counts about 2.8 million residents. 70.6% are Hispanic, and 54.5% were born abroad, against 21.9% statewide.2 In 2024, Latino voters' rightward shift helped the party sweep Miami‑Dade County in the 2024 presidential election for the first time in more than three decades3, Reuters reported.

This year, 66 of Florida's 67 counties moved left, and Miami-Dade ranked fourth by the size of its move. The typical county moved 7 points, and the vote-weighted average was 10. Among the state's other big counties, Broward and Orange moved 15 points, Hillsborough and Duval 12, Palm Beach just under 10, and Pinellas 6. Nationally, Miami-Dade's move was above the typical county's 14 points and close to Cook County's 18, but smaller than Harris County's 27 or Dallas's 23. Among big counties elsewhere that started from a similar Republican lean, Collin and Denton in Texas moved even more, about 23 and 22 points. Macomb in Michigan moved 12 and stayed Republican.

The county alone could not carry Nixon statewide. Her lead of about 56,000 votes there offset less than a quarter of Moody's statewide lead of about 238,000.

Who moved, and why

According to Axios Miami, a Miami Herald analysis found that voters in Miami's majority-Black and -white neighborhoods propelled the leftward swing4. Nixon won Black-majority neighborhoods by a wide margin and carried white-majority ones. Meanwhile, Miami-Dade's Hispanic voters sided with the GOP — though by smaller margins than in previous elections4. Newsweek reported that across Florida, an NBC News exit poll put Latino voters at 55 percent to 44 percent for both Jolly and Nixon5. Cuban voters gave Moody 59 percent, down from the 70 percent 2024 exit polls gave Trump5.

Reporting before the election pointed to two main issues: prices and immigration enforcement. Florida Trib listed the problems facing South Florida Republican incumbents as a lackluster economy, sky-high gas prices, and a growing voter backlash against the Trump administration's aggressive deportation policies6. Axios described Doral, which has a large Venezuelan population, as the center of that anger. There, legal workers lost the authorization to work7 after Venezuelans' Temporary Protected Status was canceled. On election night, U.S. Rep. María Elvira Salazar said her Republican Party — needs to course-correct on the economy and on immigration8.

Moody's campaign focused on Nixon's membership in the Democratic Socialists of America, and it was a theme Moody revisited in her victory speech1. WLRN reported that that socialism label did not resonate in Miami-Dade County8. Democratic strategist Steve Schale offered a different view to Axios: he said "the hangover" from 20204 kept Democrats' margins among Hispanic voters from being bigger.

Turnout also changed. In The Watch's count, the Senate race drew 72% as many votes as the 2024 presidential race, which is high for a midterm. Miami-Dade Supervisor of Elections Alina Garcia credited the open governor's seat and said "there were a lot of very important issues on the ballot."9 Preliminary figures showed registered Democrats narrowly outvoting Republicans, though that doesn't indicate how those voters cast their ballots9. That caveat matters here. A county's move combines two things: who showed up and who changed sides. County totals alone cannot separate them.

What it means for Miami-Dade

The clearest local test is still undecided. In the 27th Congressional District, Salazar narrowly led Democrat Eliott Rodriguez, and outlets disagree on what happens next. WLRN reported the race appears destined for a mandatory recount8. A News Service of Florida report carried by NBC 6 described the margin as one that would narrowly avoid a recount10. In the neighboring 26th, WSVN reported that Rep. Mario Díaz-Balart already declared victory11 even though the race had not been called.

Some local reporting found voters splitting their tickets. One Venezuelan voter who objected to Donalds' comments on deportation feels Republican U.S. Rep. Mario Díaz-Balart has helped him and the Venezuelan community12, and he backed Díaz-Balart and Moody. Jared Nordlund of the Latino advocacy group UnidosUS said Latino voters will likely be a "swing vote" in the coming years12. After the result, Miami-Dade has gone from a county Republicans swept in 2024 to one that is competitive again.
