The wave · the biggest counties · Dakota County, Minnesota

Dakota County's south-metro suburbs give Flanagan a 17-point margin

Peggy Flanagan carried Minnesota's third-biggest county by 17.5 points, almost five points better than Kamala Harris did in 2024. About 89 percent as many people voted. The move matched the state's weighted county average but trailed comparable suburbs elsewhere.

What Dakota County did

Situated in the southeast corner of the Twin Cities Metropolitan area, it is the third-most populous county in the state of Minnesota.1 The county government describes its land as one-third urban, one-third suburban and one-third rural.1 The Census Bureau estimated its population at 457,7102 in 2025. Much of the recent growth has come in second- and third-ring communities in Dakota County (Apple Valley, Farmington, Lakeville and Rosemount)1.

In the race for the open U.S. Senate seat, Peggy Flanagan of the Democratic-Farmer-Labor Party beat Republican Michele Tafoya in Dakota County 132,809 to 92,314. That is a margin of 17.5 points, with 99 percent of the expected vote counted. Harris won the county by 12.9 points in 2024, so the county moved 4.7 points toward the Democrats. Statewide, Flanagan won by 10.1 points, a move of 5.9 points from Harris's 4.3-point margin. Dakota's move was a little smaller than the state's, exactly equal to the vote-weighted average of Minnesota's counties, and 33rd largest of 87. Among the state's other big counties, Ramsey moved 8.2 points and Anoka 6.3, enough to flip it from Republican to Democratic. Hennepin moved 3.3 points, and Washington and Scott 3.1 each.

Next to big counties elsewhere that started from a similar 2024 margin, Dakota moved modestly. Cobb, Ga., moved 19.3 points; DuPage, Ill., 15.6; Orange, Fla., 14.9; and Hamilton, Ohio, 10.4. Only Oakland, Mich., at 3.7 points, moved less. The typical county nationally moved 14.1 points.

Dakota cast 231,084 votes, 89 percent of its 2024 total of 258,590. Flanagan's lead of about 40,500 votes was larger than Harris's lead of roughly 33,000, even with some 27,500 fewer ballots cast. County totals like these cannot separate voters who changed sides from shifts in who turned out, and Dakota's move contains both. Statewide, MPR News reported that this year's state election saw the largest voter turnout for a midterm election in at least 12 years.3

Why it moved

Most of the reporting points to the president. Axios reported that voter anger over President Trump's policies — including his administration's immigration crackdown — helped propel the progressive Democrat to victory.4 The Minnesota Star Tribune reported that Tafoya sought to localize the race in Minnesota with an emphasis on fraud and public safety5, while Flanagan also attacked Tafoya over Operation Metro Surge, which not only energized the Democratic base but was unpopular with independents and swing voters.5

In the last week of the campaign, the Star Tribune spoke to about 100 voters in several swing-district suburbs6. Some right-leaning independents and historically Republican voters who are Trump critics said they weren't convinced by Demuth and Tafoya's posture toward the president.6 The fraud message did persuade some voters: some DFL and independent voters said they were troubled enough by fraud that they split their tickets.7 In October, the Star Tribune/KARE 11 Minnesota Poll found the metro outside Hennepin and Ramsey a draw, with 46% for Tafoya and 44% for Flanagan.8 Dakota, the largest county in that ring, ended up well short of a draw.

After the election, the Star Tribune reported that national Republican money never arrived in force, and critics say the former broadcaster spent too little time connecting with Minnesota voters.9 Nationally, the AP Voter Poll found most voters appear exhausted and exasperated by him10, meaning Trump. No Minnesota-specific breakdown of that survey turned up in the searches for this piece. The county's election office says all results are unofficial until certified.11

What it means for the county's politics

Dakota sits entirely within the 2nd Congressional District, which CBS Minnesota describes as known for being evenly divided between Democrats and Republicans.12 Former Lakeville mayor Matt Little defeated Republican state Sen. Eric Pratt12 for the open seat. The Associated Press noted that unlike Rep. Angie Craig, who generally took moderate positions, Little has supported "Medicare for All"13. At a forum at Dakota County Technical College in Rosemount, Little called the federal immigration enforcement in Minnesota earlier this year "abhorrent."13

The gains reached down the ballot. The Sun Thisweek and the Dakota County Tribune reported that DFLers flipped one House and one Senate seat14 in their coverage area, including a Lakeville House seat where Brian Cohn defeated state Rep. Jeff Witte, R-Lakeville14. The local DFL margins are now 7-2 in the House and 4-1 in the Senate, according to the unofficial results.14 A Star Tribune precinct analysis found that DFLers flipped legislative seats held by Republicans in Anoka, Blaine, Maple Grove, Afton, Cottage Grove, Shakopee, Rogers, and Lakeville.15 Officeholders who appear on the ballot without party labels did fine: Dakota County voters decided to send all county government incumbents to office on election night.16

Next door, Scott County still voted Republican, though by less than in 2024. Dakota's Republican areas are now concentrated in its fast-growing southern edge, and in this election even some of those seats changed hands.
