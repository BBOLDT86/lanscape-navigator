Brown retakes Ohio as all 88 counties move toward Democrats

Sherrod Brown beat Jon Husted by 4.9 points in a state Donald Trump carried by 11. The biggest moves came in small, deep-red Appalachian counties where turnout fell the most. Eight Trump counties flipped.

Sherrod Brown won Ohio's special election for the U.S. Senate by 4.9 points. He took 2,301,042 votes to 2,077,748 for Sen. Jon Husted, the Republican who held the seat by appointment. Donald Trump carried Ohio by 11.2 points two years ago, so the race moved 16.1 points toward the Democrats. Every one of the state's 88 counties moved the same way. As of Saturday morning, about 97.75 percent of the expected vote had been counted, so the totals will still shift a little. The result restores the split Senate delegation Ohio had for 18 years, before Republican Bernie Moreno unseated Brown in 2024.1

The biggest moves did not come in Columbus or Cleveland. They came in small Appalachian counties in the south and east of the state. Scioto and Lawrence counties each moved 22.5 points, and Pike, Jackson and Vinton each moved about 22. Husted still won all of them. Scioto went from Trump by 48.0 points to Husted by 25.5. The smallest moves came in Putnam (+9.5) and Mercer (+9.8), two farm counties in the west-central part of the state, and in Hamilton County, home to Cincinnati (+10.4), which was already Democratic.

A campaign about power bills

Husted, a former lieutenant governor, was appointed to the Senate in January 2025 after JD Vance left the seat to become vice president.2 According to NBC4 in Columbus, Brown focused on prescription drug and utility costs, challenged Husted's support for tariffs and incentives for energy-intensive data centers, and presented himself as a check on President Donald Trump.1 Brown called Husted "the face of data centers" and highlighted his involvement in a scandal in which FirstEnergy paid $61 million in bribes to get a $1.3 billion ratepayer bailout.3 CNN reported that in August, the Senate Republicans' campaign committee warned in an internal memo that "data centers are the anchor hanging around Husted's neck."4 Brown also raised far more money: more than $68 million, compared to Husted's $20 million.5 In the AP's voter survey, most Ohio voters saw Husted as "too supportive" of Trump, while less than half said Brown was "too extreme."6

Amy Acton did better than Brown, beating Vivek Ramaswamy for governor by 7.9 points. The race turned hostile in its closing days after Ramaswamy's campaign aired an attack ad featuring Acton's estranged mother7, and NPR noted that Acton ran on lowering the cost of health care.8 WOSU reported that she carried the same 15 counties Brown did. The two races drew almost the same number of ballots: 4,601,571 for Senate and 4,595,782 for governor. That means the 3-point gap between them comes from voters splitting their tickets, not from a different set of voters showing up.

Where the map changed hands

Eight Trump counties flipped. The biggest single turnaround among them came in the Mahoning Valley. Trumbull County moved 19.4 points, from Trump by 16.8 to Brown by 2.6. Mahoning County, home to Youngstown, went from Trump by 9.4 to Brown by 7.2. WKBN reported that Brown's vote share was 3% higher in both counties than in his 2024 race9. Still, the Vindicator found that the chairmen of both parties in Mahoning and Trumbull say the area is still up for grabs.10

Other flips came along Lake Erie and in the industrial suburbs. Lorain County went from Trump by 5.7 to Brown by 11.2, and Lake, Erie, Portage and Wood counties also changed hands. Delaware County, the fast-growing suburb north of Columbus, flipped with the smallest move of the eight (+11.1), going from Trump by 6.6 to Brown by 4.5. According to the Secretary of State's figures reported by 10TV, Delaware had the highest turnout of any county, with 69.53% of registered voters casting ballots.11 Several large counties came close to flipping but stayed Republican: Stark (R+5.3), Fairfield (R+6.6) and Greene (R+7.5). Montgomery County, home to Dayton, went from a near-tie (D+0.5) to Brown by 14.9.

Who moved: small, red counties where turnout fell

These are county results, not individual voters. Still, the pattern is clear. The 16 counties that cast fewer than 10,000 votes moved 19.3 points, weighted by votes. Ohio's three largest counties (Franklin, Cuyahoga and Hamilton) moved 12.4 points, though they cast 29 percent of the state's vote. By 2024 result, the 63 counties Trump won by 30 points or more moved 16.5 points. The four Harris won by 10 to 30 moved 12.7, and Cuyahoga, the only county she won by more than 30, moved 12.2.

Turnout is the main reason this is hard to read. Ohioans cast 4.6 million votes, 80 percent of the 2024 total. The Secretary of State's office counted an unofficial turnout of 58.1%, compared with 52% in 2022 and 56% in 2018.11 Across counties, turnout dropped more in some places than others. The 69 counties where turnout fell to between 65 and 80 percent of 2024 moved 16.6 points. The 18 where it held between 80 and 95 percent moved 13.5. Across all 88 counties, the correlation between turnout change and the size of the move is -0.47: the more turnout fell, the more the county tended to move. On election night, Wright State political scientist Lee Hannah told the Ohio Capital Journal that Brown probably "didn't convert a lot of people in rural areas, but turnout wasn't as high there."12 His comparison was to Brown's own 2024 race. Compared with Harris, rural counties moved the most.

County counts cannot separate voters who changed their minds from voters who stayed home, and some counties break the pattern. Wyandot County moved 21.8 points, one of the largest moves in the state, even though its turnout held at 88 percent of 2024. Mercer and Putnam saw turnout fall to roughly 76 and 78 percent, yet they moved the least. The best summary is that lower turnout in Republican areas fits the statewide pattern best. But the Acton–Brown gap and counties like Wyandot show that some voters did switch.
