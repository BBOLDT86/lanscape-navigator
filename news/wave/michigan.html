The wave · state by state · Michigan

In Michigan, all 83 counties moved left; Wayne and Macomb led

Abdul El-Sayed won the open Senate seat by 7.1 points in a state Donald Trump carried by 1.4. The largest moves came in metro Detroit, where Wayne and Macomb counties swung 12 to 14 points.

Abdul El-Sayed, a Democrat, beat Mike Rogers, a Republican, for Michigan's open U.S. Senate seat by 7.1 points: 2,520,388 votes to 2,180,541, with 98% of the expected vote counted. Donald Trump carried the state by 1.4 points in 2024, so the Senate race moved 8.5 points toward Democrats. In the governor's race, Jocelyn Benson beat John James by 10.8 points.

El-Sayed is a doctor and former head of health departments in Detroit and Wayne County1. He won the nomination in a close August primary. Then Haley Stevens, who lost to El-Sayed in this summer's Democratic primary, roiled the race1 by endorsing Rogers days before the election. The Michigan Advance reported that more than 2.3 million Michigan residents had already voted2 by the time she did. Rogers tried to tie him to the Muslim Brotherhood, a tactic that many Democrats and local religious leaders criticized as anti-Muslim rhetoric3. The Detroit News found that Rogers and outside groups backing him outspent El-Sayed and his allies by roughly 3:24. El-Sayed tried to make the race about Trump, whose approval rating had fallen sharply in Michigan5, Time reported. Tariffs on Canada have hit Michigan particularly hard6, NBC News noted.

All 83 counties moved toward Democrats. The typical county moved 6.4 points, or 7.9 when weighted by votes cast. That is a broad shift but a modest one next to the national typical move of 14.1 points.

Detroit and its suburbs

Wayne County moved most, from D+29.0 to D+42.9, a 14-point shift on 738,765 votes. NPR reported that El-Sayed consolidated support from Black voters, many of whom supported Stevens in the primary7, and that Rogers lost Detroit by more than he did in 2024. In Dearborn, the mayor said turnout was the highest turnout in the city's history8.

Macomb County went from R+13.7 to R+1.8, a 12-point move. Rogers still carried it. One analyst told WWMT that "Rogers won Macomb, but by an underwhelming margin."9 WXYZ found that Democrats narrowed margins in communities such as Sterling Heights10.

Oakland County was the exception among the big suburbs. It moved just 4 points, from D+10.6 to D+14.3. Bridge Michigan reported that Rogers heavily courted Jewish voters turned off by El-Sayed's opposition to US support for Israel11. He gained ground in some of those communities, including West Bloomfield Township, but he did not gain enough ground to make up for Democratic shifts in other parts of the county and state11. Next door, Livingston County also moved less than 4 points.

Who moved

The biggest counties moved most. Wayne, Oakland, Macomb and Kent each cast more than 250,000 votes, together 45% of the state's total, and they moved 9.5 points (weighted). Counties with 10,000 to 250,000 votes moved between 6.5 and 6.7 points. The 18 counties with fewer than 10,000 votes moved 5.9.

The reddest places moved too, just less. The 39 counties Trump won by 30 points or more cast 13% of the vote and moved 6.2 points. The four counties Harris won by 10 to 30 points cast 35% of the vote and moved 8.9. Washtenaw, the only county Harris won by more than 30, moved 5.3 points.

Turnout fell everywhere, but evenly. The counties cast 84.7% as many votes as in 2024, and every county fell between 65% and 95% of its 2024 total. The link between a county's turnout drop and its move was weak (r = -0.14).

Counties are not voters, and county totals cannot separate people who changed their minds from people who stayed home or showed up. What the numbers do show is that turnout dropped by similar amounts across the state while every county moved left, so the result is not simply Republican voters sitting out. The reporting points to both causes. Dearborn's turnout rose above its presidential-year level. In Detroit, officials estimated turnout only a few percentage points lower than the 47% turnout seen in the 2024 presidential election12. And in Wexford County, which moved 8 points, Bridge Michigan met a former Republican who said she voted for El-Sayed, other Democrats and "anything against Trump."11

College towns and the north

Five counties Trump carried in 2024 voted for El-Sayed: Saginaw, Muskegon, Eaton, Grand Traverse and Isabella. Local outlets counted four because they measured against counties that Rogers won two years ago8. Against the presidential result, Saginaw (R+3.3 to D+2.0) is the fifth.

Isabella County, home to Central Michigan University, made the biggest of those moves, 11 points, from R+7.5 to D+3.1, with 92% counted. Other university counties moved by similar amounts: Marquette (Northern Michigan University) and Houghton (Michigan Tech) in the Upper Peninsula, and Ingham (Michigan State), each 9 to 10 points. Bridge Michigan reported that El-Sayed and Benson outperformed Harris in Ingham, Kalamazoo, Isabella and Kent counties, all home to universities and large groups of young voters11.

The smallest moves were in the rural northeast of the Lower Peninsula. Montmorency moved 2 points, and Alcona and Cheboygan 3 each. Leelanau, a Democratic county near Traverse City, also moved just 3. Along the Indiana line, Branch and St. Joseph moved about 10 points each, but only 85% and 90% of their expected vote is in.

Benson ran ahead of El-Sayed in a race shaped by the controversy over data centers and Flock cameras13. Bridge Michigan concluded that James could not overcome national trends and the unpopularity of President Donald Trump, who had endorsed him14.

The count isn't final. About 2% of the expected statewide vote is still outstanding. Huron County is at 73%, and Newaygo and Clare at about 80%.
