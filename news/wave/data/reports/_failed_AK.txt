 "doc": "HEADLINE: Alaska's Senate race is a near tie after a 14-point shift left\n\nDEK: Mary Peltola leads Sen. Dan Sullivan by 637 first-choice votes, with about three-quarters of the expected vote counted. Ranked-choice ballots may decide the race. The Democrat running for governor has a wider lead over a split Republican field.\n\nSUMMARY: Peltola leads Sullivan by 637 votes, 14 points better for Democrats than 2024; Kreiss-Tomkins leads for governor.\n\nBODY:\nTwo years after Donald Trump won Alaska by 13.8 points, its U.S. Senate race is close to a dead heat. About 74 percent of the expected vote has been counted. Democrat Mary Peltola has 102,962 first-choice votes and Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan has 102,325. That is a lead of 637 votes, or 0.3 points, and a move of 14.1 points toward Democrats since the 2024 presidential race. The Associated Press has not called the race.\n\nThe final result could take a while. The Anchorage Daily News reported that the results of the state's most competitive races will likely not be known until Nov. 18, after all mail-in ballots have been counted and ranked choice tabulation occurs. Peltola has about 48.2 percent of the votes counted so far. The other two candidates share the remaining 8,446 votes. If no one gets a majority, the ranked-choice count starts. Reporting is split on what that would do. Pollster Ivan Moore told Alaska Public Media that \"the early vote is much, much, much more Democratic, and the Election Day vote is much more Republican\", which suggests the uncounted ballots favor Peltola. But an Alaska Beacon analysis of the ranked ballots found that Sullivan will gain hundreds more redistributed votes than Peltola if the race goes to a ranked choice runoff.\n\n## How the race got here\n\nPeltola, who is Yup'ik, became the first Alaska Native to serve in Congress when she won special and regular elections in 2022. In 2024, she lost her seat to Republican Nick Begich. Alaska Public Media reported that she campaigned mostly on the themes that Democrats around the country focused on: affordability, health care costs and accusing Republicans of flagrant corruption. Health care led to some of the sharpest exchanges in their debates. The Daily News noted that last year's federal budget law cuts spending on Medicaid, a federal program that serves nearly a third of Alaskans, and other assistance programs. New Medicaid work requirements could cause more than 9,000 Alaskans to lose access to the program, according to a state projection. Sullivan had Trump's endorsement, and both the president and vice president came to Alaska to campaign for him.\n\nThe ballot also had a second Dan Sullivan, a retired teacher from Petersburg. Just over a week before Election Day, The New York Times reported that he was recruited by operatives aligned with Democratic challenger Mary Peltola. Peltola's campaign said it had no involvement with either Sullivan campaign. After the Times story, Gov. Mike Dunleavy ordered an investigation, and the Justice Department ramped up a criminal probe. Nobody has been charged with a crime. Alaska Public Media looked at the ranked ballots and found that the Petersburg Sullivan got most of his support from left-leaning Alaskans.\n\n## Who moved, and what the count cannot say\n\nIn most states, the map under this story shows each county's move and the charts sort counties into groups. Alaska can't be sorted that way. Alaska does not have counties, instead separating its votes by state house district. The Watch's data has no county-level count for Alaska, so this piece can't say whether Anchorage, the Matanuska-Susitna Borough or rural villages moved most. It also can't test the patterns the charts track elsewhere: big places against small ones, the reddest against the bluest, or richer against poorer.\n\nWhat the data can say is this: Alaska moved about as much as the rest of the country. Across 2,775 counties in states with a race this year, 2,673 moved toward Democrats. The typical move was 14.1 points, the same as Alaska's statewide move.\n\nThe shift also doesn't show how many voters changed their minds. So far 213,733 Senate votes have been counted, about three-quarters of what AP expects. A midterm electorate is not the same group of people as a presidential one. A 14-point move can come from Trump voters switching sides, from Trump voters staying home, or from both, and a statewide total can't tell these apart.\n\nSome reporting suggests real ticket-splitting. On Election Day, independent voters in South Anchorage told the Daily News they've voted for both Republicans and Democrats in the past, but this time they voted Democratic. Voters also appeared to keep the election system Republicans had campaigned to repeal. The Daily News reported that Ballot Measure 2 is on track to narrowly fail. That measure would have ended ranked-choice voting and open primaries.\n\n## A governor's race shaped by a split field\n\nThe governor's race shows a bigger shift, but much of it comes from how the field was divided. Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins, a former state representative from Sitka, has 103,837 first-choice votes. Republican Bernadette Wilson has 61,734. That is a lead of 19.9 points and a move of 33.7 points from 2024. But Wilson was one of three Republicans in the race: Kreiss-Tomkins and three Republicans — Bernadette Wilson, Dave Bronson and Treg Taylor — advanced from an August primary. The two Republicans not shown in the map's comparison together have 45,766 votes. Because of that split, the 33.7-point figure overstates the move compared with the Senate race.\n\nTrump endorsed Wilson before his October rally in Wasilla. At the rally, Bronson and Taylor urged Republicans to rank all three candidates, but Trump spoke against that appeal, calling ranked choice voting a \"scam.\" Alaska's News Source reported that Kreiss-Tomkins ran his campaign as a referendum against Dunleavy, the term-limited Republican governor. Republicans attacked Kreiss-Tomkins as \"Just Keep Taxing,\" a play on his initials, over his plan to expand corporate income taxes on oil producers.\n\nKreiss-Tomkins has about 49.1 percent of the votes counted, just short of the majority he needs to win without a ranked-choice count. Alaska Public Media found that even if a ranked count is needed, Republicans' path to a win is extremely narrow. It would require nearly every Republican voter to rank every other Republican candidate, and for nearly none to rank Kreiss-Tomkins. If he wins, KTOO noted, he would be the youngest governor in state history and Alaska's first Democratic governor since Tony Knowles left office in 2002. The state aims to certify the election results by Nov. 25.\n\nCALLOUTS:"
