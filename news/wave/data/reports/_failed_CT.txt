 "doc": "HEADLINE: Lamont wins third term as Connecticut moves 4.6 points left\n\nDEK: Gov. Ned Lamont beat state Sen. Ryan Fazio by about 19 points, nearly five points better than Kamala Harris did in 2024. That is a real move toward the Democrats, but a modest one next to the swing in much of the country.\n\nSUMMARY: Lamont won by 19.1 points, 4.6 better than Harris in 2024; Connecticut reports by town, so its map shows no counties.\n\nBODY:\nNed Lamont, the Democratic governor, has won a third term. In the Associated Press count as of Saturday morning he had 773,556 votes to 525,015 for Ryan Fazio, the Republican state senator from Greenwich. That is a margin of 19.1 points, with 98.5 percent of the expected vote counted. The Associated Press called the race at around 9:20 p.m. Tuesday, and Fazio conceded the race in a call to Lamont.\n\nKamala Harris carried Connecticut by 14.5 points in 2024, so the state moved 4.6 points toward the Democrats. Nationally the picture was different. Of the 2,775 counties counted in states with a race, 2,673 moved left, and the typical county moved 14.1 points. Connecticut's move is about a third of that. Readers will find no county shading for the state on the map above: Connecticut reports its results by town, so every figure here is statewide. About 20,000 ballots are still expected. That is far fewer than Lamont's lead of nearly 250,000 votes, so they cannot change the winner.\n\n## Two candidates from the same town\n\nThe race matched two men from Greenwich who were born more than three decades apart. The 72-year-old became the oldest elected governor in Connecticut state history Tuesday night, Fox61 reported. Fazio, 36 and in his second term in the state Senate, would have been the youngest governor elected in Connecticut in more than century. The Connecticut Mirror described Lamont as buoyed by Connecticut's extraordinary string of budget surpluses. It also reported that Lamont won Greenwich, the wealthy town where both he and Fazio reside.\n\nFazio first won statewide attention in Greenwich. The Mirror noted that in 2024 he outperformed Trump, whom he rarely if ever mentioned during this campaign. He tried the same approach statewide. The Hartford Courant reported that Trump is not featured in Fazio's own ads or digital media, and there is no mention of Trump on Fazio's campaign website.\n\n## Electric bills, ICE and the president\n\nFazio built his campaign on what things cost. WTNH reported that he named an elimination of the public benefits charge on ratepayers' electric bills, a middle-class tax cut and a property tax cap as his priorities. Late in the race he also attacked state tax breaks for data centers. He told Connecticut Public that such companies should not get special tax benefits at the expense of middle-class families who are struggling to pay their bills.\n\nPolls found that voters shared his concern but did not give him the credit. In Quinnipiac's October survey, the economy and cost of living were the most important issues identified by voters, and Lamont was deemed more capable in those areas. A Wesleyan poll designed with the Mirror found Lamont appears immune to concerns over the high cost of living, with voters blaming the president instead.\n\nLamont's campaign pushed that comparison. Its first attack ad comes days after U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement Agents raised their profile in Connecticut with arrests of migrant parents at school bus stop in Danbury. The ad ties the Republican nominee to Donald Trump and his immigration crackdown. Fazio called it misleading. He said he would have supported the original Trust Act but favored repealing later revisions that hampered local authorities from cooperating with ICE.\n\nAfter the vote, both parties described the result in national terms. WFSB reported that Lamont and the state's Democratic constitutional officers said President Donald Trump is what drove voters to vote for Democrats. Senate Minority Leader Stephen Harding, who lost his own seat, said the national wave that flipped control of the U.S. Senate and U.S. House to Democrats was more than his party could hold off. Republicans lost as many as eight of their 11 seats in the Connecticut Senate and at least nine in the House. That includes the Greenwich-area seat Fazio gave up, which Democrat Jill Oberlander of Greenwich captured.\n\n## Who moved, and what the count cannot say\n\nOn most state pages, this section uses county results to show who moved: big counties or small ones, the reddest or the bluest, places where turnout fell or held. Connecticut has no county-level results in our data, and there are no Census comparisons for it. The Watch therefore cannot say whether the cities, the shoreline suburbs or the small towns of the northeast and northwest moved most. Even where county figures exist, they describe places, not individual voters.\n\nWhat the numbers do show is a much smaller electorate at work. In Saturday's count, 1,298,571 votes had been cast for governor, out of about 1.32 million expected. A midterm turnout is not a presidential turnout, and The Watch's data for Connecticut does not include the 2024 total for a direct comparison. So the 4.6-point move compares two different groups of voters. It could reflect people who voted for Donald Trump in 2024 staying home as much as anyone switching sides. The count alone cannot tell turnout from persuasion.\n\nPre-election polling suggests some persuasion, but it does not prove it. In the Sacred Heart University/WFSB/CT Insider survey, Lamont's support among independents rose to 49.7%, from 40.9% in September, while Fazio's fell to 28.6%, from 37%. Quinnipiac found that 30% said they did not know enough about him as late as mid-October. These are surveys of voters' intentions, not records of who actually turned out. A fuller answer would need town-level returns and voter files.\n\nCALLOUTS:"
