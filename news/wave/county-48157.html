Fort Bend, Texas' most diverse county, swings 27 points to Talarico

A county Kamala Harris carried by under two points in 2024 gave James Talarico a 29-point win over Ken Paxton, beating his statewide margin as Democrats also took the county judge's seat.

What Fort Bend did

Two years ago, Fort Bend County, the fast-growing suburban county southwest of Houston, was about as evenly split as a big county gets. Kamala Harris carried it by 1.6 points out of 362,259 votes. On Nov. 3, Democrat James Talarico beat Republican Ken Paxton there 210,780 to 115,978, a margin of 28.6 points with 99% of the expected vote counted. That is a 27-point move toward the Democrats. A lead of roughly 6,000 votes became one of nearly 95,000.

Talarico's win statewide, according to the Texas Tribune, delivers his party its first statewide win here since 19941. He won Texas by 12.1 points in a state Donald Trump carried by 13.7, a move of 25.8 points. All 254 Texas counties moved left, and the typical county moved about 18 points. Fort Bend ranked 45th. It moved about as far as neighboring Harris County (27 points) and a little further than Tarrant (24) and Collin (23). Among big counties elsewhere that were about as close in 2024, only Bergen County, N.J., came near, at 21 points. Clark County, Nev., moved 13, Palm Beach 10 and Orange County, Calif., 5.

Who turned out, and who moved

Using Census data, Houston Public Media describes a county nearly divided among four major racial and ethnic groups: 28% white, 25% Hispanic or Latino, just over 23% Asian and just under 23% Black.2 Local reporters noticed heavy voting before Election Day. Community Impact reported that more than 271,000 Fort Bend County voters cast in-person ballots during early voting, representing about 46.5% of registered voters3, which was more than the total turnout in the 2022 county judge election, which was about 250,000.3

Even so, the Senate count came to 331,775 votes, 92% of the 2024 total, or about 30,000 fewer than voted for president. County totals cannot tell voters who switched parties apart from voters who stayed home or came out for the first time, so the 27-point move mixes both.

Polling gives some clues. A Texas Southern University survey of 1,200 likely voters, conducted between September 9 and October 2 by pollsters Mark Jones and Michael Adams4, found Talarico ahead 56% to 38%5 in Fort Bend. The final margin was wider than that. The same poll put him ahead 56% to 36% among Asian American voters6. Jones, a Rice University political scientist, told the Houston Press that "Black voters punch above their population weight in Fort Bend County as a share of the electorate and they're voting at a 90-10 margin"7 for Democrats.

Statewide, ABC News' Election Day voter poll showed Hispanic voters backing Talarico 68%8 to 30% and Asian American voters 71% to 28%. The voter poll has no separate Fort Bend figures. On the Republican side, Sen. Ted Cruz said in a post-election podcast that his party lost "evangelical Christians who didn't show up with the same numbers and fervor as prior elections"9, along with blue-collar voters, farmers and ranchers. In other words, he blamed turnout as well as defections.

On issues, Houston Public Media's pre-election survey of Fort Bend voters found priorities ranging from health care access and property taxes to cultural and ideological issues.2 The Dallas Morning News described Talarico's race as a campaign built around lowering costs and fighting corruption10. That message had a local echo in a county whose own government had been mired in scandal.

What it means at the courthouse

The Senate result came with a change at the top of county government. Democratic Commissioner Dexter McCoy defeated Republican Daniel Wong for county judge. The Houston Chronicle reported that his win ended months of political infighting that brought Fort Bend County government to a standstill and a turbulent year that began with former County Judge KP George's felony money-laundering conviction in March.11 Wong had been appointed interim county judge in April following George's suspension.12 The county judge leads Fort Bend County Commissioners Court, which oversees the county's budget and decisions involving roads, public safety, emergency management and other county services.12

Houston Public Media reported that, according to unofficial totals, Democrats won the county judge and commissioner races13. Covering Katy News found the sweep was not complete: Republicans won two justice of the peace contests.14 Results stay unofficial until the canvass is done.

Fort Bend had been leaning Democratic before this year. Jones told The Texan this fall that the county has been trending blue for some time, although 2022 and 2024 were very good years for Republicans.4 This year undid Trump's near-tie of 2024. One election can't show whether that comes from lasting change in a diverse, growing county or from a single cycle with an unpopular Republican nominee at the top of the ticket.
