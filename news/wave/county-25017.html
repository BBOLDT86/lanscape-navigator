In Middlesex, Markey's home county, a modest move left on lower turnout

Edward Markey won Massachusetts' biggest county by 44 points, about five points better than Kamala Harris did there. About a quarter fewer ballots were cast, so the count alone can't show how much of that shift was persuasion.

Middlesex County starts at Cambridge and Somerville on the Charles River and runs out through Newton, Framingham and Lowell to small towns on the New Hampshire line. On Nov. 3 it cast more votes than any other county in Massachusetts: 614,200 in the U.S. Senate race, nearly a quarter of the state's total. It is also the winner's home ground. Edward Markey is the Malden Democrat1, and Malden sits inside the county.

What Middlesex did

Markey took 428,431 votes in the county to 157,579 for the Republican, John Deaton, a margin of 44.1 points. In 2024 Kamala Harris carried Middlesex over Donald Trump by 39.5, so the county moved about five points toward the Democrats. The county alone supplied about a third of Markey's statewide lead. He won Massachusetts by 31.2 points; Harris had won it by 25.3.

By the state's own standard, the shift was middling. All 14 Massachusetts counties moved left, and Middlesex ranked ninth, a little below the typical county's move of about six points. Bristol County, around Fall River and New Bedford, moved 11 points. Essex moved 9 and Suffolk, which is mostly Boston, moved 7. Norfolk moved only 3. Markey also ran ahead of his own ticket: statewide, his margin grew 5.9 points over Harris's, while Gov. Maura Healey's grew 1.9.

The national picture makes the move look smaller still. The typical county in the country moved 14 points. Cook County, Ill., which gave Harris a similar margin, moved 18, and Travis County, Texas, moved 19. Middlesex looked more like the big Bay Area counties: Contra Costa moved 5 points and Santa Clara 1.

Turnout matters to all of this. The 614,200 votes are 76 percent of the 809,513 cast in 2024, about 195,000 fewer ballots. A county total cannot separate voters who changed sides from voters who stayed home in 2026 or showed up only this time. The five-point move is some mix of both.

Why, according to the reporting

Markey, 80, has said that, if reelected, this would be his last term in office2. Deaton, a 59-year-old attorney, former Marine, and cryptocurrency booster3, was running for the Senate for the second time. At their only debate, Boston.com reported, Markey invoked the president's name more than 40 times4, while Deaton subsequently distanced himself from the MAGA base and painted himself as a bipartisan politician4. The Boston Globe reported that Deaton went after Markey's record on energy, pointing to the state's energy prices.

Preelection polling suggested the national mood counted for a lot. The University of New Hampshire's final survey found many voters seeing their vote as a way to oppose President Trump5, and it named affordability or inflation and stopping Trump or MAGA5 as the top issues. The New Bedford Light reported on a UMass Amherst/WCVB poll in which voters worried about the cost of living were more likely to blame Trump than Healey6. NBC News' statewide exit poll found 37 percent of voters naming the state of democracy as the top issue and 34 percent naming the economy. None of these surveys breaks out Middlesex on its own.

Massachusetts has no county election office; city and town clerks run the vote. Secretary of State William Galvin said more than 1.1 million people voted early statewide, which he described as a "dramatic" increase over past years7. On Election Day his office reported high turnout and unusually long ballots, and long wait times at polling places in Lowell8 because the ballot ran to four cards.

What it means here

Middlesex was already one of the most Democratic big counties in the country, and this election's sharpest local change came down the ballot. In Billerica, 19-year-old Daniel Darris-O'Connor defeated longtime Republican state Rep. Marc Lombardo, who has represented the 22nd Middlesex District for 16 years9, by a narrow margin. He told WCVB that his campaign focused on issues facing the town, including rising living costs10. MultiState, a firm that tracks state legislatures, reported that Democrats also took the seat outgoing House Minority Leader Brad Jones (R) had held for three decades11, a district that takes in Reading and North Reading.

The ballot questions split the county by type of place. WBUR found that Cambridge, Somerville and Boston all overwhelmingly voted for12 Question 7, the measure to make it easier to build single-family homes on small lots, while smaller communities largely voted no. When WBUR published, the statewide result was still too close to call.

For Republicans in Massachusetts the weakness runs deeper than one race. WBUR reported before the election that fewer than 9% of Massachusetts voters are registered Republicans13. Afterward, Deaton told NBC Boston that the national party "became the party of Trump and they paid the price for that."14
