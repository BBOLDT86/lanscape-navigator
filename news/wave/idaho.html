Ada County flips as Idaho's Senate margin shrinks by 13 points

Republican Jim Risch won a fourth term by 23 points over independent Todd Achilles. Still, 43 of Idaho's 44 counties moved left of their 2024 presidential vote, with the biggest moves in the east and the south-central counties.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026, from the count as of Nov. 7, 2026; the tables below refresh with the count (last Nov. 14, 1:32 AM ET, AP via The New York Times), and the piece is rewritten when a race is called or a margin moves more than a point. 15 sources. Jim Risch won a fourth term in the U.S. Senate comfortably. He took 401,788 votes to 238,352 for Todd Achilles, the independent, a margin of 23.0 points with 98.96 percent of the expected vote counted. Donald Trump carried Idaho by 36.5 points in 2024, so the gap closed by 13.5 points. Forty-three of the state's 44 counties moved toward the challenger.

The flip came in Ada County, home to Boise. Mr. Trump won it by 10.3 points. Mr. Achilles carried it by 2.9, with 226,077 votes cast there, nearly a third of the statewide total. The governor's race had a Democrat on the ballot, and it moved almost as far. Gov. Brad Little beat Terri Pickens by 24.7 points, a shift of 11.8. Idaho's typical county moved 11.8 points, less than the national figure of 14.1. The results become official only after the Idaho State Board of Canvassers certifies them.1

An independent instead of a Democrat

There was no Democrat in the Senate race. David Roth won the Democratic primary but then dropped out. He announced the end of his campaign after facing pressure from some prominent Democrats to drop out and rally behind Mr. Achilles2, and he never endorsed him.2 Mr. Achilles is a U.S. Army veteran and former state legislator3. The Idaho Capital Sun described him as a former Democratic state legislator from Boise who left the party and Statehouse seat to run1. Mr. Risch used that history against him. He told the Spokesman-Review, "There were two Democrats in this race."4

The challenger raised real money but still trailed. His $1.8 million was the most of any federal election challenger in recent years5, the Capital Sun reported. Mr. Risch raised several times that much. Mr. Risch declined to take part6 in a September candidate forum in Caldwell. His campaign said the organizer had publicly endorsed his opponent. The Lewiston Tribune wrote that the race was marked by concerns over affordability and international conflict7. The paper's editorial board argued afterward that Mr. Risch was saddled with an unpopular war in Iran.8

About one Senate ballot in ten, some 70,600 votes, went to neither man. The other candidates were independent Natalie Fleming, 55, of Meridian, and Libertarian Party nominee Matt Loesby, 35, of Eagle9. That means a smaller Republican margin here does not have to mean Mr. Achilles won over Trump voters.

Who moved

Idaho's counties moved by similar amounts whatever their size or politics. The 31 counties with fewer than 10,000 votes cast hold 18 percent of the vote, and they moved 11.2 points. The 10 mid-sized counties moved 13.3. The three largest, Ada, Canyon and Kootenai, account for 53 percent of the vote and moved 12.1. The 38 counties Mr. Trump won by 30 points or more moved 12.2. Some of the biggest shifts came in deep-red eastern Idaho. Franklin County went from R+77.6 to R+57.4, and Bonneville County, around Idaho Falls, went from R+45.4 to R+27.1. In the south-central counties, Twin Falls moved 17 points and Jerome 16. Canyon County, west of Boise, moved 15.

Counties are not voters, and these counts cannot tell people who changed their minds from people who stayed home. Turnout was 79 percent of the 2024 level. Even so, 2026 saw the highest participation of eligible voters of any midterm election in four decades10, Boise State Public Radio reported. Proposition 1, a ballot measure that would have restored abortion access, explains part of that. Secretary of State Phil McGrane says Proposition 1 is the main issue driving early voting11. He also warned beforehand that the turnout could be driven by people opposed to it, which could dilute the effects of a blue wave11. The state Republican chairwoman, Dorothy Moon, told the Statesman she thought opposition to the initiative was a "main driver" of Republican voter turnout12. Voters soundly defeated the measure.13

Turnout and movement are only weakly linked (r = −0.23). Counties where turnout fell further tended to move a little more. Franklin and Madison both cast about two-thirds of their 2024 vote, and they shifted 20 and 15 points. But the eight counties that kept the most voters moved 12.8 points, weighted by votes, against 11.9 for the rest. Ada County is among those eight. Neither pattern is strong enough to settle whether turnout or persuasion did more.

Where the shift ran thin

The smallest moves were in the north. Kootenai County, which includes Coeur d'Alene, cast 74,167 votes and moved just 6 points, from R+51.8 to R+46.0. Benewah County barely moved at all. Shoshone, Clearwater, Boundary, Lewis and Idaho counties each moved 5 to 7 points.

Latah County, home to the University of Idaho in Moscow, was the only county that moved right. It went from R+8.0 to R+9.6 on 15,230 votes, 75 percent of its 2024 turnout. The counts cannot say whether that reflects fewer student voters or something else, and local reporting has not explained it yet. The Lewiston Tribune reported that GOP candidates appear to be holding on in Idaho's District 6 races14, the legislative district that includes Latah.

Idaho's two Democratic counties both moved further left. Blaine County, home to Sun Valley, went from D+31.7 to D+42.1. Teton County, on the Wyoming line, went from D+6.8 to D+14.0. Further down the ballot, Democrats picked up six seats in the Idaho Legislature15, Mr. McGrane said.
