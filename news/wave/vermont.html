The wave · state by state · Vermont

Vermont's counties all swung right, and the reason was Phil Scott

All 14 counties moved toward the Republican column between Kamala Harris's 2024 win and Gov. Phil Scott's sixth-term victory. On the same ballot, voters restored Democratic supermajorities in the Legislature, so the shift tracks one governor more than a party.

Nationally, 2,673 of the 2,775 counties counted so far moved toward Democrats. Vermont went the other way: all 14 of its counties moved toward the Republican column, because the Republican on the ballot was Gov. Phil Scott.

Scott beat Democrat Amanda Janoo 162,025 votes to 141,618, a margin of 6.6 points, with 98.6 percent of the expected vote counted. Kamala Harris carried the state by 31.8 points in 2024, so the map shows a 38.4-point move to the right. Nine counties Harris won went to Scott: Addison, Bennington, Caledonia, Franklin, Grand Isle, Lamoille, Orange, Rutland and Windsor. Janoo carried three: Chittenden, Washington and Windham.

The move measures one man more than his party. On the same ballot, Vermont voters severely limited his executive authority by restoring Democrat and Progressive supermajorities in both chambers of the Legislature1. On election night Scott acknowledged that "It's been a difficult night for Republicans across the nation, and unfortunately Vermont wasn't spared"2. The map compares a presidential race with a governor's race. WCAX noted that in 2024, Scott won every single town and city3. This year Janoo only turned about 50 towns and cities blue, but that included some larger population centers, like Burlington, South Burlington, Montpelier, and Brattleboro.3

The narrowest of six

Janoo, a Strafford native, entered the race in early March with virtually no name recognition.1 Vermont Public reported that she built a following among voters drawn to her proposals for publicly funded universal primary healthcare and tax hikes on the rich.1 Her campaign raised more than $1 million from nearly 9,000 individual donors and outspent Scott by close to $100,0001. On Scott's side, VTDigger reported, the Republican Governors Association poured $950,000 into a pro-Scott super PAC4 in the final weeks.

Scott made property taxes the center of his campaign and billed school consolidation as a path to lowering property taxes.5 He acknowledged that his vision for education reform and his decision to make state employees return to regular in-person work made him less popular with some voters.2 The polls disagreed with each other. The University of New Hampshire's final survey had Janoo ahead, while Vermont Public's September poll had Scott up by 10 points. After the election, WCAX reported that experts believe UNH overestimated younger voter turnout, who may skew toward Janoo, and underestimated the turnout of those over 656.

Who moved

Counties are not voters, so this section describes places, not people. The vote-weighted move was 39 points toward Scott, and the typical county moved 37.

The bluest places moved the most. The six counties Harris won by 30 points or more cast 64 percent of this year's vote and moved 41 points, weighted by votes. The four she won by 10 to 30 points moved 33. The two counties Trump carried moved least: Orleans by 32 points and Essex by 28. Some of that is arithmetic, since the most Democratic counties had the most Democratic votes to lose to Scott. Size followed the same pattern. Chittenden, the only county with more than 50,000 votes, cast 27 percent of the total and moved 47 points. The 11 mid-sized counties moved 36.

Vermont cast 309,023 votes in this race, 84 percent of the 2024 total. The secretary of state's preliminary figures showed turnout for Tuesday's election set an all-time record for a non-presidential year.7 The three counties where turnout fell most, to between 65 and 80 percent of 2024, were Essex, Orleans and Rutland, all among the state's reddest. They moved 35 points. The other 11 counties kept 80 to 95 percent of their 2024 turnout and moved 40 points. The correlation between a county's turnout change and its move is −0.57: places that kept more of their voters moved further toward Scott. If Democrats had simply stayed home, the pattern would run the other way. The county data point more toward Harris voters choosing Scott than toward Democrats skipping the race, but they cannot prove it.

Surveys suggest the same thing but give different sizes. Vermont Business Magazine's account of exit polling says Scott captured 31 percent of voters who backed Kamala Harris in 2024.8 State Navigate's pre-election survey, as reported by Newsweek, put that share at 25 percent.

Burlington's suburbs, Scott's home turf

Chittenden County, the state's population center, went from Harris by 53.6 points to Janoo by 6.6. VTDigger reported that Janoo outperformed her primary margin of victory in the county's deep-blue heart of Burlington and Winooski9 but couldn't score enough votes in surrounding suburbs — Shelburne, Williston, Colchester — to overcome Scott's support there.9 Senate President Pro Tempore Phil Baruth described those ring towns as socially liberal but more fiscally conservative.9

Washington County, where Scott grew up in Barre10 and still lives, in Berlin, nearly flipped. It went from Harris by 43.4 to Janoo by 1.1. The Waterbury Roundabout reported that Janoo still won Waterbury and nearby Middlesex, Moretown, Waitsfield and Warren11.

Windham County had the smallest move in the state, 23 points, and stayed with Janoo by 20.1. VTDigger noted that she did well in some other blue pockets of the state, including the areas around Brattleboro and Montpelier.9 The largest move came on the Lake Champlain islands. Grand Isle went from Harris by 21.1 to Scott by 28.6, a 50-point shift on 4,278 votes. Farther north and east, Franklin went to Scott by 36.1 and Rutland by 31.1. Essex County, already Trump country, gave Scott a 44.3-point margin.
