Tarrant County, long Texas Republicans' urban anchor, swings 24 points to Talarico

Trump carried Fort Worth's county by five points in 2024. Two years later James Talarico won it by 19 with fewer voters, and Democrats took the county judge's seat for the first time since 1986.

What Tarrant did

Donald Trump carried Tarrant County, home to Fort Worth and Arlington, by 5.1 points in 2024, out of 822,680 votes. On Nov. 3, with 99% of the expected vote counted, Democrat James Talarico led Republican Ken Paxton 427,879 to 287,783 in the county. That is a margin of 19.2 points and a lead of about 140,000 votes. The county moved 24 points to the left and switched parties at the top of the ticket. Talarico won the Senate seat by 12.1 points statewide.

Tarrant's move was in line with the rest of Texas. All 254 counties in the state moved left, and the state as a whole moved 26 points. Tarrant's 24 matched the vote-weighted average for Texas counties, which put it 58th in the state. Harris moved 27 points, Bexar 26 and Dallas 23. North of Dallas, Collin moved 23 and Denton 22, and both also went from Republican to Democratic. Tarrant moved further than any of the other big counties Trump won narrowly in 2024. Maricopa in Arizona moved 20 points, Hillsborough in Florida 12, Pinellas in Florida 6 and Nassau in New York 4. The typical county nationally moved 14.

This is a big, mixed county. The Census Bureau estimates its population at 2,248,4661 in 2025, with Hispanic residents at 31.9%1 and non-Hispanic white residents at 39.1%1. A Dallas Morning News precinct map found that residents predominantly chose Talarico in Fort Worth and Arlington, as well as the Hurst-Euless-Bedford area. Support for his candidacy was strongest in southern Fort Worth2. The same analysis found that Paxton had more support outside of the county's major cities, including in Keller, Southlake, Azle and Benbrook.2

Who turned out, and who switched

Tarrant cast 728,259 votes in the Senate race, 89% of its 2024 total, or about 94,000 fewer ballots. A county count can't tell how much of the 24-point move came from Trump voters who stayed home, new or irregular voters who showed up, and voters who changed sides. It was probably some of each.

The early signs pointed to turnout. The Star-Telegram reported that the midterm consistently drew more early voters in Tarrant County than in 2022 and 20183. During early voting, county Republican Party chair Tim Davis told CBS News Texas that Republican turnout in the county is a little lower.4 Voters got to the polls even though there were fewer places to vote. The Tarrant County Commissioners Court voted 3-2 to reduce the total number of polling sites in the county to 224, down from 316 polling sites in 2022.5 ABC News, citing its Dallas affiliate WFAA, reported that many found themselves waiting in long lines for hours.6

People disagree about who made the difference. Ross Hunt, a Republican consultant and data analyst, told the Texas Tribune that his modeling showed a large share of the county's Republicans voting Democratic all the way down the ballot. Speaking to Nexstar, he said the margin is indicative of a split within the Republican Party.7 Democrats see it differently. Tiffany Burks, who won the race for criminal district attorney, told reporters that "Tarrant County has never been a red county, Tarrant County has been a nonvoting county."8

Paxton's record was part of the race. The Texas Tribune pointed to the ethical baggage Paxton was carrying — a yearslong felony case, his impeachment, a massive unexplained rise in his net worth9 and his divorce. Glen Whitley, a Republican and former county judge who backed Talarico, told the Star-Telegram that voters were very fed up with the corruption10. In NBC's statewide exit poll, the economy11 and the state of democracy11 were the two issues voters named most often.

What it means for the county

The change went beyond the Senate race. Democrat Alisa Simmons beat Republican County Judge Tim O'Hare, and the Fort Worth Report noted she is the first Democrat to hold the countywide seat since Mike Moncrief left office in 1986.12 CBS News Texas reported that Democrats took victories in all contested countywide races, as well as a majority on the commissioner's court.13 The Star-Telegram added that Democrats unseated the Republican incumbents for Tarrant County district attorney, district clerk and county clerk.14

The new majority takes over a commissioners court that has spent several years in partisan fights. Simmons, a sitting commissioner, ran against O'Hare after the court's current 3-2 Republican majority redrew her precinct's boundaries to favor GOP candidates.12 KERA reported that before the election, Republicans hoped to grow to 4-115 under the new lines. Instead they lost control of the court.

Republicans lower on the ballot ran ahead of Paxton in Tarrant, but KXAN reported it wasn't much better.7 Hunt noted that some of the loss was expected in a midterm that went heavily Democratic across the country. Democrats, for their part, told Fox 4 that they'll need to take charge and really deliver results to Texans in order to keep Tarrant County blue.16 The county's first real test of that comes in 2028.
