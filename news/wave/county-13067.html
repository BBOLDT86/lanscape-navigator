In Cobb, Ossoff doubles Democrats' lead as the last GOP footholds fall

Jon Ossoff won Cobb County by 34 points. Kamala Harris had won it by 15. Fewer people voted than in 2024, and Democrats also took the school board and a long-held East Cobb commission seat.

What Cobb did

Cobb County is the suburban county northwest of Atlanta that takes in Marietta, Smyrna and Kennesaw. On Nov. 3 it gave Sen. Jon Ossoff, the Democrat, 67 percent of its vote. With 99 percent of the expected vote counted, Mr. Ossoff led Rep. Mike Collins, the Republican, 225,293 to 110,573, a margin of 34.2 points. In 2024, Kamala Harris carried the county by 14.9 points. That 19-point move to the left ranks 27th among Georgia's 159 counties, and every one of them moved toward the Democrats.

Cobb stayed close to its state. Georgia went from a 2.2-point Trump win to an 18.0-point Ossoff win, a move of 20 points. Cobb moved about a point less than the state but stayed about 16 points more Democratic. Among the big metro counties, Cobb landed in the middle. Gwinnett moved 26 points and Henry 23. Fulton (14) and DeKalb (13) were already heavily Democratic and moved less. Cherokee, Cobb's Republican neighbor to the north, moved 18. Harris won several big counties elsewhere by about the same margin as Cobb, and Cobb swung further than all of them: Hamilton County, Ohio (10 points), Orange County, Fla. (15), DuPage County, Ill. (16) and Pima County, Ariz. (16).

Fewer people voted this time. Cobb cast 335,866 votes in the Senate race, 84 percent of its 2024 total of 401,369. Mr. Ossoff's lead was about 115,000 votes, nearly double Ms. Harris's lead of roughly 60,000, even with about 65,000 fewer ballots. County totals cannot tell how much of that came from voters switching sides and how much from who turned out and who stayed home.

Why it moved

Turnout was strong for a midterm. The Marietta Daily Journal reported that Cobb County beat its early voter turnout record for a midterm election1. The Atlanta Journal-Constitution reported that federal Justice Department monitors were sent to polling places in six Democratic-majority Georgia counties, Cobb among them. Even so, county elections director Leigh Phillips said the day "went smoothly"2.

The exit polls published so far cover the whole state, not Cobb. NPR reported that Georgia Republicans had nominated a candidate whose personal baggage alienated independents and some Republican-leaning voters.3 The Georgia Recorder wrote that Mr. Collins was likely hurt by his close ties to President Trump, whose approval rating is at a low point in Georgia as voters struggle with high gas prices and inflation.4 WABE reported that the two candidates clashed on tariffs, cost of living, healthcare and immigration5, and that Mr. Ossoff outraised Mr. Collins by more than 10-to-1.5 In a poll CBS News reported, only about 4 in 10 Senate voters said6 Mr. Ossoff's views were too extreme. About half said that of Mr. Collins.

Cobb's population has changed a great deal. The 2020 census put it at 766,1497, with non-Hispanic white residents at 48.2% of the population7. Only Gwinnett scored higher on the Census Bureau's diversity index (Cobb's diversity index was 67.3%, second only to Gwinnett County.7). The AJC has described the northern suburbs, Cobb among them, as once considered the cradle of the state's modern Republican Party8.

Local fights mattered too. Atlanta News First reported that the Democratic school board candidate, Micheal Garza, made the district's book bans and its decision to stop livestreaming public comments a focus of his campaign.9

What it means for Cobb

The change reached down the ballot. The Democrat Kevin Redmon narrowly beat JoAnn Birrell, a four-term Republican commissioner, in unofficial returns. East Cobb News reported that this gives Democrats four of the five commission seats and called East Cobb one of the last Republican strongholds in Cobb County10. Mr. Garza defeated David Chastain, the school board's vice chairman. That flips the board from a 4-3 Republican majority to a 4-3 Democratic majority.9 The Cobb Courier reported that three of Cobb's Republican legislative incumbents lost their seats11. One was Sen. Ed Setzler, the main sponsor of Georgia's 2019 six-week abortion ban, who lost to Darcy Castro. Cobb voters also approved the sales-tax renewal, according to the Marietta Daily Journal.

Republicans did not lose everything. East Cobb News reported that Sen. Kay Kirkpatrick was barely re-elected, and that longtime East Cobb State House members Sharon Cooper and John Carson won re-election but by very narrow margins.12 All of these results are unofficial. The county elections board is scheduled to certify them next week.

The new Democratic majorities may also be short-lived, though for a reason other than the voters. Under state law, Cobb's commission and school board races become nonpartisan in 2028. East Cobb News noted that the school board's Democratic majority will exist for only two years13 as a result.
