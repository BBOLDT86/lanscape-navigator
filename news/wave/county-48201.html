Harris County swings 27 points toward Talarico, outpacing Texas' other big counties

Texas' most populous county gave James Talarico a 33-point win over Ken Paxton, up from Kamala Harris's 5.5-point edge in 2024. It did so on fewer votes than two years ago, so it is unclear how much came from turnout and how much from changed minds.

A year ago, Republicans had marked Harris County as the place to win. Gov. Greg Abbott pledged to spend at least $25 million to flip Harris County from blue to red1, and the Houston Chronicle reported that he recruited candidates so that a Republican was running for each of the county's 24 state House seats2. Kamala Harris had carried the county by about 5.5 points in 2024.

On Nov. 3, Democrat James Talarico beat Republican Ken Paxton there by 33 points, 907,617 votes to 451,780, with 99% of the expected vote counted. That is a 27-point move toward the Democrats and nearly two-thirds of the vote for Talarico.

What Harris County did

Statewide, Talarico won by 12.1 points in a state Donald Trump carried by 13.7, a move of 25.8 points. Harris County moved a little more than that. All 254 Texas counties moved left. Harris ranked 44th by the size of its move, and the typical county moved 18 points.

Among Texas' biggest counties, Harris County moved the most. Bexar moved 26 points, Tarrant 24, Dallas and Collin 23 each, Denton 22 and Travis 19. Tarrant, Collin and Denton all flipped. The Austin American-Statesman reached the same conclusion: out of the big metro counties, Harris experienced the largest shift since 2024, followed by Bexar County.3 Neighboring Fort Bend County was nearly even in 2024 and moved 27 points as well. Big counties elsewhere that started from a similar margin moved less: Bergen, N.J., 21 points; Clark, Nev., 13; Kent, Mich., 8; Orange, Calif., 5. Among the nation's largest counties in The Watch's data, none moved as far as Harris.

The scale matters. Harris is the nation's third largest county4 and Texas' most populous. It covers Houston's Black and Latino neighborhoods as well as fast-growing suburbs like Cypress, Katy and Tomball.

Who turned out, and why

The county cast 1,382,875 votes in the Senate race. That is 89% of its 2024 presidential total, or about 173,000 fewer votes. A shift this size mixes Republicans who stayed home, Democrats who showed up and voters who switched sides, and county totals cannot separate those groups. The Chronicle reported the raw total set a midterm record, but a record number of voters does not mean the county had its strongest turnout as a percentage of registered voters5. Registration had grown, and turnout outstripped all of the past 10 midterms except 20185.

The Chronicle's precinct analysis found the Democratic surge was especially strong among Harris County's Black and Hispanic voters, who waited in lines at polling centers where few voters turned out in previous elections.6 In those precincts, voters cited concerns about immigration, corruption and Republican leaders' policies and rhetoric.7 Plurality-white precincts gave Talarico his narrowest margins, with Republican candidates winning in suburban outskirts of the county.7 Before the election, a local analyst told KPRC he was seeing more voters with a Democratic primary history in suburban areas, including ZIP codes around Katy, Cypress and Tomball.8

Polling suggested some voters were also changing sides. An October University of Houston Hobby School survey found 58 percent plan to vote for Talarico, 36 percent for Paxton9 among likely Harris County voters. The same poll found just 85 percent of Republicans said they plan to vote for Paxton, 7 percent for Talarico9. The result ended up well beyond that poll's lead. Statewide, AP VoteCast found swings of around 20 percentage points toward Talarico among Hispanic voters in Texas under 65 years old.10 The Texas Tribune reported before the election that affordability concerns and anger over the administration's mass deportation campaign11 were driving the expected Latino swing. Paxton's closing message to Houston voters was that a vote for Talarico is a vote for a guy you've heard of, [Senate Democratic Leader] Chuck Schumer [of New York], to run the Senate12.

What it means at the courthouse

Democrats did well further down the ballot too. Letitia Plummer defeated Republican Orlando Sanchez for the Harris County judge race, keeping the county's top elected office in Democratic hands13, and she becomes the first Black woman and first Muslim elected Harris County judge.13 The Chronicle reported she was among a slew of Democrats who won races across county government this year4. Abbott also lost statewide to Gina Hinojosa, by 7.1 points.

That reverses the trend Republicans pointed to two years ago, when they won 10 of the 16 Harris County judicial races they contested in 20242. Before the vote, Rice University political scientist Mark Jones argued that even if Republicans lost the county, "they need a dignified loss."2 Losing by 33 points was not that.

How long this lasts is not settled. Newsweek wrote that whether Talarico's win represents a lasting political realignment or a uniquely favorable Democratic environment is an open question.14 In Harris County, part of that depends on who turns out in future elections, not only on who changed sides this time.
