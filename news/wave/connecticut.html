Connecticut moves 4.6 points left as Lamont wins a third term

Ned Lamont beat Ryan Fazio by 19.1 points, 4.6 points wider than Kamala Harris's 2024 margin. That is a real move, but well short of the 14-point move typical of counties nationally.

Ned Lamont, a Democrat, won a third term as governor of Connecticut on Nov. 3. He beat Republican state Sen. Ryan Fazio of Greenwich 773,556 votes to 525,015 in the Associated Press count, with about 98.5 percent of the expected vote counted. That is a margin of 19.1 points, or about 248,500 votes. In 2024 Kamala Harris carried the state over Donald Trump by 14.5 points, so the governor's race ran 4.6 points more Democratic than the presidential vote two years earlier.

Measured against the rest of the country, that is a small move. Across the 2,775 counties The Watch has counted, 2,673 moved toward Democrats, and the typical county moved about 14 points. WTNH reported that the win makes Lamont the first Connecticut governor to win three consecutive elections since John G. Rowland in 2002.1 Connecticut also doesn't appear county by county on the map. The state reports its vote by town, and as of 1960, counties in Connecticut do not have any associated county government structure.2

Electric bills against Washington

Fazio, 36, ran on cost of living. When he entered the race he promised to cut electric rates by 20% and eliminate the public benefits charge3, cut income taxes by $1,500 for the average family and cap property taxes. In the debates on WFSB, he said Lamont has too close of a relationship with the CEO of Eversource4, the state's largest utility. One of his most prominent ads attacked the governor's tax breaks for data centers, the Hartford Courant's reporting showed. Lamont answered that "Politicians love to make promises they can't keep,"5 and said he had cut income taxes and brought electric prices down from a year earlier.

The Connecticut Mirror described Lamont as buoyed by Connecticut's extraordinary string of budget surpluses after a lost decade of chronic fiscal crisis6. It called the election a midterm rout dominated by the policies and unpopularity of President Trump. In an October Wesleyan poll reported by the Mirror, only 27% of those surveyed by Wesleyan approved of Trump's performance and 70% disapproved7. As early voting opened, Lamont's campaign aired an ad that paired Trump's record with a clip of Fazio saying, "I think he's doing a good job."7 For years Fazio had declined to say how he voted for president. Only when he opened his campaign for the Republican nomination for governor did he divulge he voted all three times for the president.8 In 2025 he also commended the ICE action9 in Connecticut. On election night, WFSB reported that Lamont and the other Democratic statewide officers said President Donald Trump is what drove voters to vote for Democrats.10

Who moved, and what the count can't say

On most state pages, this section charts which counties moved: big or small, red or blue, places where turnout rose or fell. Connecticut has no county-level count on the map, so those charts are empty here. Counties aren't voters in any case, and neither are towns. A place that moved left doesn't show that any one person changed their vote.

Town-level reporting gives a partial picture. The Connecticut Mirror found that 57.3% of eligible Connecticut voters cast a ballot, according to unofficial numbers from the Secretary of the State's office11. That was a slight drop from the 2022 midterm, but turnout rose sharply in a few cities. Danbury saw the highest increase, with turnout jumping 12.4 percentage points to 60.8%.11 Bridgeport and New London also saw high jumps in turnout and swings towards the Democrats.11 Most of the towns where turnout rose have majority-minority populations, and the only majority-minority town to see a decrease in turnout from 2022 was New Haven.11 Danbury was the site in August of a four-day ICE operation in Danbury that resulted in 118 arrests12. Its mayor, Roberto Alves, told the Mirror the turnout rise reflected trust in local leaders and feelings about national issues, especially immigration.11

That points to turnout as at least part of the story in those cities. But the 4.6-point move statewide compares a presidential electorate with a midterm one, here 1,298,571 votes for governor. The count can't separate people who changed their minds from changes in who showed up. In places where turnout held steady, the record so far doesn't settle it. Lamont also carried Greenwich, the wealthy town where both he and Fazio reside6.

Down the ballot

The larger shift came in the General Assembly. Connecticut Public reported that Democrats won an overwhelming majority in the state legislature, leaving the GOP with as few as three seats in the state Senate13. Mark Pazniokas of the Connecticut Mirror called the result "a near extinction event"13 for the party. Several races still faced recounts. The losses included long-serving Sen. John Kissel of Enfield. Senate Minority Leader Stephen Harding of Brookfield conceded, saying he had no realistic path to victory.14 Hartford Business Journal reported that Democrat Jill Oberlander of Greenwich captured the 36th District seat held by Sen. Ryan Fazio, the GOP nominee for governor.15 Democrats swept all five House races in Connecticut.16

Republicans read the result differently. House Minority Leader Vincent Candelora blamed what he called the chaos in Washington. "Every candidate this cycle heard the same thing from voters: Connecticut is too expensive,"17 he said. Lamont said he would spend his third term focused on improving schools and lowering the cost of health care.10
