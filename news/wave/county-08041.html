El Paso County, long Colorado's Republican anchor, votes for Hickenlooper

Donald Trump carried the Colorado Springs county by nearly 10 points in 2024. Two years later, John Hickenlooper won it by about 4. Fewer people voted, and the county elected a Democrat to Congress for the first time.

For decades El Paso County was where Colorado Republicans made up for Denver. The Gazette described it as a place once known nationally as a stronghold of Republican votes, with its voters able to balance strong Democratic performance in Denver1. On Nov. 3 it voted for a Democrat for the U.S. Senate.

With 98.5% of the expected vote counted, Democratic Sen. John Hickenlooper leads Republican state Sen. Mark Baisley in the county, 155,453 votes to 143,607. That is a margin of 3.8 points. In 2024, Donald Trump beat Kamala Harris here by 9.8 points. A few ballots are still outstanding because election officials will be curing ballots and processing those received under the Uniformed and Overseas Citizens Absentee Voter Act for multiple days after the election2.

What the county did

The county moved 14 points toward the Democrats. That was slightly less than Colorado as a whole: statewide, Hickenlooper won by 25.3 points, a 14.3-point gain on Harris's 11.0-point win. All 64 Colorado counties moved left, and El Paso ranked 18th by size of move. Its shift was larger than the typical county's 11.5 points and close to the vote-weighted average of 14.1. Douglas County, the other big Colorado county Trump carried, moved 11 points and also flipped, from R+7.0 to D+4.1. Adams County moved the most of the big counties, 23 points.

Among big counties elsewhere that Trump won by similar margins, El Paso falls in the middle. Suffolk County, New York, moved 11 points and ended with a narrow Democratic edge. Macomb County, Michigan, moved 12 and stayed Republican. Collin and Denton counties in Texas moved 23 and 22 points.

Election-night reporting in the Gazette called this what appears to be the first time top-ticket Democratic nominees have carried the county1.

Who voted, and why

The Senate race drew 313,166 votes, 82% of the 2024 total, or about 68,000 fewer. County-level numbers can't separate voters who switched sides from voters who stayed home, and both probably played a part. In three Colorado Springs state House districts, the Colorado Sun found that turnout, based on ballots counted through Friday afternoon, dropped precipitously in 2026 from 20243. The Sun also found that the Republican candidates' totals fell faster than overall turnout. Its reporter noted that only voters in House Districts 15, 21 and 22 can definitively answer why they lurched so far to the left this year3.

There was also reporting of voters changing sides. Reuters reported that a volunteer canvassing in south Colorado Springs met conservatives willing to support Killin after they saw Crank back a war that has stoked energy prices4. Reuters also reported that Karl Schneider, a former US special forces officer and ex-chair of the El Paso Republican Party, backed Killin5. Democrat Jessica Killin, who won the House race, said her win was a repudiation of Republican incumbent Jeff Crank's support for President Donald Trump6. She also said it reflected frustration with the Iran war, tariffs and the Epstein scandal.

The county itself is changing. Reuters called Colorado Springs a military town and global center for Christian evangelism6. It also reported that the metro area's population more than doubled to around 782,000 in the last four decades, bringing greater ethnic diversity and a younger, more highly educated electorate5. Registration figures cited by Colorado Politics in June put the county at Republicans at 29%, Democrats at 17% and unaffiliated 54%7.

The Senate race itself was uneven. Hickenlooper raised a reported $11 million through the middle of October to the Republican's $138,0008. The Denver Gazette said Baisley ran a lackluster campaign9, and Axios reported that he supported the Iran war and mass deportations. The Gazette's editorial board offered its own explanation: many unaffiliated Coloradans vote against a party associated with a president who is unpopular in this state10.

What it changes

The biggest change is in Congress. Killin is the first Democrat to win the district in its 54-year history11, and the 5th District almost entirely overlaps El Paso County9. CBS Colorado reported that Democrats spent a record amount to boost Killin, who outraised Crank12. Reuters reported that outside spending for Crank brought the two campaigns' total resources close to even.

The shift also reached the state legislature. House District 15, which includes eastern Colorado Springs and Cimarron Hills, flipped from red to blue13. As of Friday, Republican Rep. Ken DeGraaf was narrowly ahead in House District 22.

Republicans did not lose everything. The Gazette's results show Republican Clerk and Recorder Steve Schleiker reelected and Republican Ryan Graham winning the District 1 commissioner seat. The Gazette's takeaway was that El Paso looks more like a suburban purple county after this year's election1. It traced the change back to 2023, when independent Yemi Mobolade beat Republican Wayne Williams for mayor. Sara Hagedorn, a political scientist at the University of Colorado Colorado Springs, told Reuters that Killin "has a real opportunity here to be a real moderate or independent voice within the Democratic Party"5.
