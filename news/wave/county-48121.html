Denton County backs Talarico by 8 points; its Republican county judge survives

The fast-growing North Texas suburb went from a 13-point Trump county to an 8-point Talarico county. That 22-point move was smaller than in most of Texas's other big counties, and voters kept most local Republicans in office.

Denton County covers the north side of the Dallas–Fort Worth area, from Carrollton and Lewisville up through the university town of Denton to the ranchland around Sanger. In 2024 Donald Trump carried it by 13.2 points. On Nov. 3 it chose Democrat James Talarico over Republican Ken Paxton for U.S. Senate by 8.4 points, 211,266 votes to 177,859, with 99% of the expected vote counted. That is a 22-point move.

The county keeps growing. It had 906,422 residents in the 2020 U.S. census1 and hit the 1 million mark1 in early 2023, according to regional planners. The Denton Record-Chronicle reported that it now has 695,512 registered voters — about 86,900 more than four years ago.2

What Denton County did

Across Texas, Talarico won by 12.1 points in a state Trump had carried by 13.7, a 25.8-point move. Democrat Gina Hinojosa won the governor's race by 7.1. All 254 Texas counties moved left. Denton ranked 85th. Its move was bigger than the typical Texas county's (18 points) but smaller than the statewide move weighted by votes (24).

Among Texas's seven biggest counties, only Travis moved less. Harris moved 27 points, Bexar 26, Tarrant 24, and Dallas and neighboring Collin 23 each. Collin, which the Dallas Morning News called Paxton's political home base3, went from R+11.2 to D+12.1. Denton moved further than similar counties elsewhere that Trump had won by about the same margin: Miami-Dade moved 19 points, El Paso County, Colorado, 14, Macomb in Michigan 12 and Suffolk in New York 11. The national typical move was 14.

The Dallas Morning News precinct map showed Talarico strongest in the center of the city of Denton4, with wins in much of Carrollton's Denton County region4, Lewisville and Little Elm. Paxton had a stronger showing on the county's western side, including cities like Justin, Argyle and Sanger.5

Why it moved

Denton cast 395,959 votes, 88% of its 2024 total, or about 53,000 fewer votes. County totals cannot tell voters who changed their minds apart from Trump voters who stayed home or new voters who showed up. Some arithmetic helps, though. Trump's 2024 lead here was roughly 59,000 votes, so even if every missing voter had been a Trump voter, he would still have come out ahead. The rest of the swing came from some mix of people switching sides and a different set of voters turning out, and the count cannot say how much of each.

Frank Phillips, the county elections administrator, credited the political atmosphere nationwide and an increase in involvement2. "I think they've come out to voice their opinion,"2 he told the Record-Chronicle. There were signs of this in March, too: Denton County Democrats nearly tripled their turnout6 in the primary compared with 2022 and 2024, though Republicans still cast more primary ballots.

No poll looked at Denton County on its own, so the evidence on why people voted as they did is statewide. The Associated Press's survey of Texas voters found about two-thirds of voters said Paxton did not have the right personal character to serve as a U.S. senator7. Talarico won most young voters and Hispanic voters7. The Dallas Morning News reported that the race became a battle over affordability, corruption and competing visions of Texas3, and that Paxton never agreed to debate. Strategists from both parties told The Texas Newsroom that economic pain exacerbated by President Donald Trump's tariffs and the war in Iran8 was likely the biggest factor. Brendan Steinhauser, a Republican strategist, said Republican voters were not as motivated and probably didn't show up in the numbers that they needed9.

Talarico campaigned in the county on the first day of early voting, filling a ballroom at the University of North Texas. There he framed big-money donors as "Goliaths," and specifically denounced Elon Musk's influence on his race.10

What it means at the courthouse

The Democratic wave thinned out further down the ballot. The Record-Chronicle found that local candidates from the left didn't perform as strongly as those higher on the ballot2. Republican County Judge Andy Eads barely edged past Democratic challenger Nick Pappas11. U.S. Rep. Brandon Gill and state Rep. Richard Hayes kept their seats red2, and so did state Reps. Andy Hopper and Mitch Little12.

Democrats did make two gains in the south of the county. Denise Wooten defeated state Rep. Ben Bumgarner in House District 63, and Democrat Candace Valenzuela ousted Precinct 2 Commissioner Kevin Falconer.12 The Cross Timbers Gazette summed up the night as one in which voters favored Democrats in the top statewide races while keeping County Judge Andy Eads and much of the area's Republican delegation in office.12

That leaves Denton County with Democrats ahead in the statewide races, Hinojosa included, and Republicans still holding most local offices.
