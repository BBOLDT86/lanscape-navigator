The wave · state by state · Maryland

Washington suburbs swing harder to Moore; the Eastern Shore moves the other way

Wes Moore beat Dan Cox again, and by more than Kamala Harris won Maryland. Most of the extra margin came from the big Washington suburbs and Frederick. A few small Eastern Shore counties moved toward Cox.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 11, 2026, from the count as of Nov. 11, 2026; the tables below refresh with the count (last Nov. 13, 2:59 PM ET, AP via The New York Times), and the piece is rewritten when a race is called or a margin moves more than a point. 14 sources. Gov. Wes Moore, a Democrat, won a second term on Nov. 3. It was a rematch of 2022 against Republican Dan Cox1, a former state lawmaker and lawyer from Frederick County2. With 88 percent of the expected vote counted, AP's tally has Moore ahead 1,337,884 to 657,111. That is a margin of 32.7 points, about 4 points wider than Kamala Harris's 28.8-point win over Donald Trump in 2024. Sixteen of Maryland's 24 counties moved toward the Democrats and eight moved toward the Republicans. That count treats Baltimore City as a county, separate from Baltimore County. No county changed sides. The typical Maryland county moved 2 points toward Moore. The typical county across the country moved 14 points toward Democrats, so Maryland's shift was a modest version of the national one.

The two campaigns talked about different things. WUSA9 reported that Moore's campaign focused on protecting Marylanders from President Donald Trump, while Dan Cox focused on affordability.3 One of Cox's main criticisms at the October debate was Moore's handling of the state's estimated $3 billion deficit4. Moore also declined to commit to serving a full four-year term amid speculation about a 2028 presidential run.5

Where the suburbs moved

Montgomery County moved more than any other, by 10 points. Harris won it by 53.3 points; Moore leads by 63.3 on 340,907 votes. Prince George's moved 7 points, to a margin of 82.6, and Charles County moved 8, to 48.4. These are the places where federal paychecks matter most. Before the election, Brookings counted federal workers at 10% of total employment in Montgomery, 11% in Prince George's and 18% in Charles6, and reported that Maryland lost 29,200 federal government jobs between January 2025 and April 20266. The county numbers can't show whether the job cuts moved votes. They only show that the biggest swings came where those jobs are.

The Montgomery figures are still moving. Only 82 percent of its expected vote is in. Bethesda Magazine reported that at least 35,000 mail-in ballots remain to be canvassed and counted in Montgomery County7, and provisional ballot canvassing is set to start on Thursday, Nov. 12.8

Frederick County, Cox's home, moved 9 points to Moore, from a margin of 8.8 to 18.1. WYPR reported that data centers, immigration and affordability stood as key issues9 in the county. It also reported that Democrats took almost all seats across the county9, and that the Republican sheriff, Chuck Jenkins, was also ousted by Democrat Karl Bickel9. Farther west, Washington County moved 9 points and Allegany 7, but both still voted for Cox. In Washington County, LocalNews1 found Republicans held the top five positions in the Washington County Board of Commissioners race10.

The Baltimore area barely moved. Baltimore County went from 24.5 to 25.3, and Baltimore City rose 2 points to 74.9. Anne Arundel rose 3, Harford rose 1, and Carroll moved 2 points toward Cox.

A shore apart

On the Eastern Shore, Somerset moved 6 points toward the Republicans, the most of any county in the state, going from R+17.4 to R+23.0. Dorchester moved 5 and Kent 4. Talbot, which split almost exactly even in 2024, went to Cox by 2.2 points. Wicomico, home to Salisbury, was the exception and moved 2 points toward Moore.

The Shore had a fight of its own on the ballot. Question 3 was a constitutional amendment that loosens the rules for drawing congressional maps. It could put Rep. Andy Harris's Republican seat in play. WMDT reported that Moore spent $4 million on ads supporting the ballot question.11 The amendment passed statewide. But a Capital News Service analysis found it did not get majority support in any of the 10 counties fully encompassed within Harris' Eastern Shore district12, and that opposition to Question 3 ran ahead of Cox's support in most counties there.13 Harris's Democratic opponent, Dan Schwartz, said the measure "turned out angry Republicans on the Eastern Shore"14. That is his reading of the result, not an established finding. The Star Democrat reported that Talbot voters turned out early at a higher rate than anywhere else in Maryland.5

Who moved

Counties are not voters, so the charts beside this section show where margins changed, not which people changed their minds. Still, the pattern is plain. The three counties with more than 250,000 votes each (Montgomery, Prince George's and Baltimore County) cast 43 percent of the state's vote and together moved 6.3 points toward Moore. The two smallest, Somerset and Kent, moved 4.5 points the other way.

The counties that were already most Democratic moved most. The five that Harris won by 30 points or more cast 46 percent of the vote and moved 7.1 points toward Moore. The nine that Trump won by 10 to 30 points moved little; the typical one went half a point toward Cox. The three Trump won by less than 10 (Kent, Talbot and Wicomico) moved a typical 2 points toward Cox. Frederick was the only county Harris won by less than 10, and it moved 9 points toward Moore.

Turnout fell sharply, as it usually does after a presidential year. About 2.08 million votes have been counted, 69 percent of the 2024 total. Twenty-three counties fell between 65 and 80 percent of their 2024 vote. Cecil was the only one below, at 60 percent, and it barely moved. Counties that kept more of their 2024 turnout tended to move less toward Moore, but the link is weak (r = -0.31). Talbot kept 79 percent of its 2024 vote and moved toward Cox. Montgomery and Prince George's kept about two-thirds and moved toward Moore, though Montgomery's share will rise as its ballots are counted. Frederick kept 73 percent and still moved 9 points. That suggests the Shore's Republican voters turned out better than others did. It does not rule out some voters changing sides, and county totals can't tell the two apart.
