The wave · state by state · Maryland

Moore's rematch win moved Montgomery and the mountains, not the Eastern Shore

Gov. Wes Moore beat Dan Cox again by about 34 points, five better than Kamala Harris's 2024 margin. The gains ran through Montgomery, Frederick and western Maryland; all five counties that moved right sit on the Eastern Shore.

Gov. Wes Moore won a second term on Nov. 3 in a rematch with Republican Dan Cox. On the votes counted so far, he leads 1,411,534 to 675,791, a margin of 33.8 points. That is 5.1 points better for the Democrats than Kamala Harris's 28.8-point win in Maryland in 2024. About 94 percent of the expected vote has been counted. Most of what remains is in Montgomery County (84 percent counted), Baltimore City (88 percent) and Prince George's County (90 percent).

Maryland moved left, but less than most of the country. Its typical county moved 3 points toward the Democrats, compared with 14 points for the typical county nationally. Nineteen of the state's 24 jurisdictions (23 counties plus the independent city of Baltimore) moved toward the Democrats. None changed hands in either direction.

A referendum Cox could not pay for

Maryland Matters reported that Cox repeatedly sought to make the election a referendum on Moore's first term, citing snags in the effort to rebuild the Francis Scott Key Bridge, repeated state budget deficits and rising costs1. In their debate, Cox pledged not to raise taxes or fees and criticized Moore over utility bills and projected budget deficits2. Moore, for his part, argued that Trump administration policies had made Marylanders' financial challenges worse.2 The money was lopsided: Cox reported raising $371,822 in his campaign finance report last month with the Maryland State Board of Elections, to just under $17 million reported by Moore.1

The governor did not go into the race especially popular. In UMBC's last pre-election poll, 49% reported approving of Moore's performance as governor3, and among unaffiliated voters, 37% approve of Moore's work and 49% disapprove.3 Moore spent much of his final push on Question 3, the redistricting amendment. The Maryland Board of Elections announced that 60.05% were for the amendment4.

Where the map moved

The largest moves came in the Washington suburbs and along the I-70 corridor. Montgomery County, which cast the most votes, went from D+53 to D+64, a 10-point move. Prince George's moved 7 points to D+83, and Charles moved 8. These are the counties where federal job cuts have landed hardest; The Banner reported that Maryland lost 29,200 federal jobs through April, one of the states hardest hit5. The county counts cannot show whether those cuts changed any votes.

Frederick County moved 10 points left, from D+9 to D+19, even though it is Cox's home county; Cox cast his ballot Tuesday morning at Sabillasville Environmental School in Frederick County6. WYPR reported that Democrats took almost all seats across the county7 and that data centers, immigration and affordability stood as key issues7. Farther west, Washington County moved from R+23 to R+13 and Allegany from R+40 to R+31. Both remain Republican, and in Washington County Republicans held leads in several major Washington County races Tuesday, including all five county commissioner seats and the sheriff's office8.

The Eastern Shore went the other way. All five counties that moved right are there: Dorchester and Kent by 2 to 3 points each, Talbot by 1, and Worcester and Somerset by less than 1. Talbot was dead even in 2024 and is now R+1. Caroline and Queen Anne's barely moved. Wicomico and Cecil were the exceptions, each moving about 4 points left. Question 3 won 60% support statewide Tuesday, but all nine Eastern Shore counties voted against it.9 Rep. Andy Harris said the measure "drove a lot of voters on the Eastern Shore"10, and his Democratic opponent, Dan Schwartz, agreed that it energized Republicans. Capital News Service found something different: turnout on the Shore stayed relatively high this year, but even with Question 3 on the ballot11, the region did not draw more voters than usual.

Who moved: turnout or persuasion

Counties are not voters, and a county's move cannot separate people who changed their minds from people who did or did not vote. With that caveat, the charts show a clear pattern by size. The three largest jurisdictions (Montgomery, Baltimore County and Prince George's) cast 43 percent of the vote and moved 7.2 points left. The eight mid-sized counties moved 4.8 points. The 11 counties with 10,000 to 50,000 votes moved 2.7. The two smallest, Kent and Somerset, moved 1.7 points right.

By 2024 partisanship, the five places Harris won by 30 or more points moved 7.5 points left, and Frederick, the one county she won by less than 10, moved 10. The most Republican counties moved too: the four Trump won by 30 or more moved 4.7 points left, weighted by votes. The counties that barely moved were in between. The typical county Trump won by 10 to 30 points moved less than 1 point, and the three close Trump counties on the Shore went slightly right in the typical case.

Turnout tells part of the story. The 2,176,350 votes counted so far equal 72 percent of the 2024 total. Counties that kept more of their 2024 turnout tended to move left less (r = -0.47). Talbot was the only county to keep more than 80 percent of its 2024 vote, and it moved right. Cecil kept the least, under 65 percent, and moved left. Montgomery's share will rise as its count finishes. The Daily Record reported that statewide turnout held nearly flat from the 2022 midterm12, with lower turnout in 13 counties, while turnout did rise in all three of Maryland's Black-majority jurisdictions.12 That fits a shift driven at least partly by who showed up: Democratic-leaning places held up better while Republican ones thinned out, except on the Shore. But persuasion cannot be ruled out from these counts.
