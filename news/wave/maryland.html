Moore widens Maryland margin in D.C. suburbs as Eastern Shore drifts right

Gov. Wes Moore ran 2.7 points ahead of Kamala Harris's 2024 margin. That is a small move by this year's national standard. His big gains around Washington and in Western Maryland were partly offset by losses on the Eastern Shore.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026, from the count as of Nov. 7, 2026; the tables below refresh with the count (last Nov. 10, 6:06 PM ET, AP via The New York Times), and the piece is rewritten when a race is called or a margin moves more than a point. 13 sources. Wes Moore, Maryland's Democratic governor, won a second term on Nov. 3. He beat former Del. Dan Cox in the gubernatorial election Tuesday, the Associated Press called1, in a rematch of their 2022 race. With 84 percent of the expected vote counted, Moore leads 1,278,118 to 647,110, a margin of 31.5 points. Kamala Harris carried the state by 28.8 points in 2024, so Maryland has moved 2.7 points toward the Democrats. That is a modest shift in a year when the typical county nationwide moved about 14 points left.

The race was lopsided from the start. Maryland Matters reported that Cox reported raising $371,822 in his campaign finance report last month with the Maryland State Board of Elections, to just under $17 million reported by Moore2. Maryland is a state where registered Democrats outnumber Republicans more than 2 to 12. In their only debate, Cox pledged not to raise taxes or fees and criticized Moore over utility bills and projected budget deficits3. Moore, for his part, at every turn tied Cox to President Donald Trump4. WUSA9 summed up the campaigns this way: Moore's campaign focused on protecting Marylanders from President Donald Trump, while Dan Cox focused on affordability.5

The statewide figure hides a split. Maryland has 24 voting jurisdictions: 23 counties plus the independent city of Baltimore. Fifteen moved toward the Democrats and nine toward the Republicans. None changed parties.

Gains around Washington, and in the west

Montgomery County moved the most. Harris won it by 53 points; Moore leads by 63, a shift of nearly 10 points. It also has the most votes, at 328,738 counted. In Prince George's County, Harris's 75-point margin is now 82, and Charles County went from 41 to 48. These are the counties most tied to the federal government. A Brookings analysis counted a loss of 29,200 federal government jobs between January 2025 and April 2026 in Maryland alone6. It found federal workers make up 10 percent of employment in Montgomery, 11 percent in Prince George's and 18 percent in Charles. WTOP reported that local Democrats plan to fight federal job cuts and relocations that hit the D.C. region, especially Prince George's County, very hard.7 The county count shows where the margins grew. It cannot show that the job cuts were the reason.

The bigger surprise is in the west. Washington County, home to Hagerstown, still went for Cox, but the gap shrank from Trump by 23 points to Cox by 14. Allegany County, around Cumberland, moved 7 points left and Garrett 3. The Cumberland Times-News covered Moore, Democrats talk turning red seats during Cumberland visit8, along with an endorsement from the city's mayor.

Frederick County moved 8 points, from Harris by 9 to Moore by 17. Cox once represented portions of Carroll and Frederick counties in the House of Delegates from 2019 to 20233. Carroll went the other way, from Trump by 25 to Cox by 27. WYPR reported that data centers were among the hottest topics this election, with residents critical of Fitzwater's support of a 2,600 acre data center overlay zone9. Cox ran on a statewide moratorium on data centers, yet Frederick voters reelected Jessica Fitzwater, the Democratic county executive, anyway.

The Eastern Shore went the other way

Seven of the nine counties that moved right are on the Eastern Shore. Somerset County moved the most, from Trump by 17 points to Cox by 23. Dorchester went from 15 to 19, and Kent from 3 to 6. Talbot, a dead heat in 2024, now favors Cox by 2. Wicomico and Cecil were the only Shore counties that moved left.

The Shore also had a fight of its own on the ballot. Question 3, a constitutional amendment that loosens the rules for drawing congressional maps, passed statewide. FOX45 reported that it was rejected in all nine Eastern Shore counties.10 Democrats promoted the amendment as a way to make it easier to target Harris11, meaning Rep. Andy Harris, the 1st District Republican. On election night, Harris said Question 3 "drove a lot of voters on the Eastern Shore"12. His Democratic opponent, Dan Schwartz, argued it hurt his campaign by angering Eastern Shore Republicans.12 Those are the candidates' own readings. No one has measured them.

The one big county that drifted right is Baltimore County, which slipped from Harris by 24.5 points to Moore by 23.5. Next door, Anne Arundel barely moved.

Who moved: the bluest places, and the biggest

The charts beside this section are about counties, not voters, but the pattern is clear. The three counties with more than 250,000 votes (Montgomery, Prince George's and Baltimore County) cast 42 percent of the vote and moved 5.6 points left as a group. The two smallest, Somerset and Kent, moved 4.5 points right.

By 2024 result, the five counties Harris won by 30 or more points cast nearly half the vote and moved 6.8 points left. The counties in the middle hardly moved. That includes the two Harris won by 10 to 30 points, Anne Arundel and Baltimore County, and the three Trump won by less than 10. The four most Republican counties moved 2.5 points left, mostly because of Allegany and Garrett.

So far, 2,006,126 votes have been counted, 67 percent of the 2024 total. The counties with the lowest turnout moved left the most: the five below 65 percent of their 2024 vote moved 4.8 points, and a county's turnout and its move are moderately correlated in that direction. But those five include Baltimore City, Prince George's, Montgomery and Baltimore County. These are also the places with the most votes still uncounted, between 71 and 82 percent of the expected vote. That makes the turnout comparison partly a product of the count. The county figures cannot tell people who changed their minds apart from changes in who voted. A turnout story fits both ends of the map at least as well as persuasion does, but these numbers cannot settle it.

The numbers will change. Maryland Matters reported 269,342 mail-in ballots left to count statewide as of Thursday night13, and noted that mail ballots lean heavily toward Democrats.13 County election officials have until next Friday, Nov. 13, to certify election results.13
