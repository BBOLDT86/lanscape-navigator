The wave · the biggest counties · Philadelphia County, Pennsylvania

Philadelphia gives Shapiro a 79-point margin, on far fewer votes than 2024

The city moved 20 points toward the Democrat from the 2024 presidential race. That was less than most of Pennsylvania moved, and about 231,000 fewer people voted, so the bigger margin is part turnout and part voters switching sides.

What Philadelphia did

Philadelphia is a city and a county with the same borders. Gov. Josh Shapiro won 436,266 votes there and Republican Stacy Garrity won 49,821. That is a 78.9-point margin, with 98.8 percent of the expected vote counted. Kamala Harris won the city by 58.8 points in 2024, so the Democratic margin grew by 20 points. The count is not finished: remaining mail and provisional ballots can still change the totals, and the county certifies results later in November.1

Philadelphia is the state's Black and Latino center. Census estimates put the county at 42.4%2 Black, 16.4 percent Hispanic and 33.8 percent non-Hispanic white. Its population is about 1.57 million, down 1.8 percent since 2020. In 2025 the Inquirer reported that Philly's poverty rate dropped to 19.7% in 2024, the first time it fell below 20% since at least 1979.3

A 20-point move is large for a place that started so far to one side, but it was small by Pennsylvania standards. All 67 counties moved toward the Democrat, and Philadelphia ranked 52nd. Statewide, a 1.7-point Trump win in 2024 became a 22.1-point Shapiro win, a 23.8-point move. Bucks County moved 26 points, Montgomery and Allegheny 23, and Delaware and Chester 22. Philadelphia did move more than other big Democratic counties around the country. DeKalb County, Georgia, moved 13 points, Alameda County, California, 8 and Multnomah County, Oregon, 5. Manhattan barely moved.

Turnout changed the most. Philadelphia cast 490,015 votes for governor, 68 percent of the 721,441 cast for president. Shapiro's lead in the city was about 386,000 votes. That is roughly a third of his statewide lead, but it is fewer net votes than Harris's lead of roughly 420,000, even though his percentage margin was much wider. A county total cannot tell voters who switched sides apart from voters who stayed home, so the move here is a mix of both, and the count cannot show the proportions.

Why: a quiet turnout and a weak challenger

The Inquirer reported that at least in Southeastern Pennsylvania, the blue wave was not fueled by a surge in voter turnout.4 It said one likely reason was a dearth of competitive races that could draw voters to the polls.5 City Commissioner Seth Bluestein told the paper that "In recent midterm elections, it was higher than the turnout that we just had, but we're not done counting."5 The commission's chairman, Omar Sabir, told 6abc that turnout remains slightly below turnout in the 2018 midterm election6.

Shapiro was already popular in the city. A Suffolk University/Inquirer CityView poll in June found that he also got positive reviews from almost half the city's independents and more than one-third of Republicans.7 Garrity ran a thin campaign. The Inquirer's post-election account said for the majority of the campaign, Garrity's television advertising footprint was barely existent8 and that national and state Republican groups never gave directly to her campaign. WHYY reported that Shapiro benefited from some GOP support and President Donald Trump's sagging favorability.9 In AP's voter survey, three-quarters of voters said they are either angry or dissatisfied with the direction of the country.10

The local press found some signs that 2024 Trump voters came back to the Democrats. Since 2016 the Inquirer had tracked Trump's steady gains in the city's majority-Latino precincts. In August it profiled Jorge Luis Burgos of North Philadelphia, who voted for Trump in 2024 and planned to vote for Shapiro. Burgos had said Trump's promises to bring the cost of living down to its pre-COVID rate11 won his vote in 2024. In the Northeast, the city's most Republican area, the Northeast Times found that every Northeast ward moved toward Shapiro compared with 202212. Its comparison was with 2022, not 2024. Many voters there split their tickets: in Republican state Rep. Martina White's district, Josh Shapiro carried the 170th Legislative District by 23 points and Republican state Rep. Martina White won it by 151. The Watch has not seen a published division-by-division comparison with 2024, so it is not yet known how much of the citywide move came from Latino, Black or white neighborhoods.

What it means for the city

Philadelphia's lopsided margin added to a statewide result that gave Democrats trifecta control of Pennsylvania Senate, House and governorship for the first time since 199313. The Inquirer said the new majority could mean more money for the city's schools and SEPTA, if Harrisburg can find the money14. The ballot also showed tension inside the party. State Rep. Chris Rabb won the 3rd District, becoming Philly's first democratic socialist in Congress14. Two of his supporters the Inquirer interviewed saw Gov. Josh Shapiro as too centrist, though they both voted for him.14

The turnout question is still open. Election officials told 6abc that they are exploring ways to increase voter participation, including follow-up systems for mail-in ballots and partnerships with community organizations6. Philadelphia gave a Democrat one of its widest margins in years, but it did so with about a third fewer voters than two years earlier.
