Black Hills tilt toward independent Bengs as Rounds holds South Dakota

Sen. Mike Rounds won a third term by 21 points, eight fewer than Donald Trump's 2024 margin. The shift was sharpest around Rapid City and Sioux Falls, while reservation counties and the Pierre area moved the other way.

Republican Sen. Mike Rounds won a third term on Nov. 3. He beat independent Brian Bengs 184,589 votes to 120,509, a margin of 21.0 points, with 99 percent of the expected vote counted. That margin is 8.2 points narrower than Donald Trump's 29.2-point win over Kamala Harris here in 2024. Of South Dakota's 66 counties, 55 moved toward Bengs and 11 moved toward Rounds, and none changed hands. The typical county moved 5.7 points. Nationally the typical county moved 14.1 points toward Democrats, so South Dakota moved well under half as far.

The ballot had no Democrat. Julian Beaudion, the Democratic nominee, withdrew in August after months of insisting he would stay in1, The Washington Post reported. Beaudion said he was pressured by Bengs and some Democrats to withdraw, an allegation Bengs denied.2 The Watch's map counts Bengs on the Democratic side. Bengs, a 56-year-old Hot Springs resident, served in the Navy and as an Air Force attorney, and ran as a Democrat four years ago, losing to Sen. John Thune by 44 points2.

An independent and an incumbent who would not debate

Bengs built his campaign around money in politics and the tech industry. South Dakota Searchlight reported that he criticized Rounds' ties to the tech industry, including senior adviser Rob Skjonsberg, who has served as a state-level lobbyist for Meta.3 Rounds co-chairs the Senate Artificial Intelligence Caucus.4 In October, Dakota News Now reported that Bengs said Rounds should meet with voters after holding a private forum with AI data center industry leaders in Rapid City5. Bengs challenged Rounds to debates, but Rounds declined.2 Outside groups ran ads tying Rounds to Trump policies, including higher tariffs and the war in Iran, which have helped raise fuel and consumer prices.2 Rounds' campaign focused on his opposition to abortion, his support of military spending and his A+ rating from the National Rifle Association.3

Total spending approached $10 million2. About 40% of it supported Bengs or opposed Rounds.6 The late polls disagreed. An Emerson College/KELO-TV poll had Bengs 8 points behind6, while Mason-Dixon and South Dakota News Watch showed Rounds ahead by 196. The final margin was closer to the second.

The governor's race on the same ballot barely moved. Republican Gov. Larry Rhoden beat Democrat Dan Ahlers by 27.0 points, 2.2 points narrower than Trump's 2024 margin. Rhoden is a rancher and welder from Meade County.7 He replaced Gov. Kristi Noem when she became Secretary of Homeland Security in January 2025.7

Where the map moved

The biggest moves were in the west. Pennington County, home to Rapid City, went from R+26.4 to R+14.3, a 12-point move on 38,216 votes. Nearby, Meade County moved 13 points, Lawrence 12 and Butte 14. Fall River County, which includes Bengs's hometown of Hot Springs, went from R+49.2 to R+35.7. The ranching counties of Perkins and Harding moved 14 points each, though Perkins is still R+55.7 and Harding R+72.6. The count cannot tell how much of this was home-region loyalty and how much was the campaign.

In the east, Minnehaha County (Sioux Falls) moved 10 points to R+3.1, the closest margin in any county Trump carried. Lincoln County, next door, moved 9. "In Sioux Falls, Democrats had more of an organized campaign effort,"8 one analyst told South Dakota Public Broadcasting. Searchlight reported that Democrats grew their legislative caucus from eight seats to 12, the first time in 18 years they gained ground in the 105-member Legislature.9

A few places moved the other way. Hughes County (Pierre) went from R+30.1 to R+32.2. Rounds grew up in Pierre10, and the counties around it, Stanley, Sully and Hyde, also moved slightly toward him. The reservation counties moved toward Rounds too: Buffalo and Dewey by 5 points, Ziebach and Mellette by 3, Oglala Lakota by 2 and Todd by 1. The reporting reviewed for this piece does not explain why. Of the five counties Harris carried, only Clay (Vermillion) moved toward Bengs, by 6 points to D+12.6.

Who moved: counties, not voters

These figures describe counties, not individual voters. By size, the 59 counties with fewer than 10,000 votes cast 42 percent of the ballots and moved 6.0 points, weighted by votes. The six mid-sized counties, from Pennington down to Meade, cast 36 percent and moved 10.1. Minnehaha alone cast 22 percent and moved 9.6. By 2024 result, the 48 counties Trump won by 30 points or more moved 7.4. The 12 he won by 10 to 30, which cast 56 percent of the vote, moved 9.2.

Turnout dropped sharply: 305,098 votes against 428,922 in 2024, or 71 percent. Searchlight reported that turnout fell to a historic low, with 49% of registered voters casting a ballot.11 A University of South Dakota political scientist suggested that Republicans who skipped the election may have been fatigued after a competitive June primary and a July runoff for governor.11 But turnout does not line up with the moves. In 59 counties, holding 96 percent of the vote, turnout came in between 65 and 80 percent of 2024's and the vote moved 8.3 points. The correlation between a county's turnout drop and its move is a weak −0.14. The reservation counties' votes fell roughly in line with the state's: Oglala Lakota cast 70 percent of its 2024 total. Even so, Oglala Lakota had the lowest turnout of registered voters, at 29%11.

The county count cannot separate voters who changed their minds from voters who stayed home. One comparison helps. The Senate and governor's races were on the same ballots and drew almost the same number of votes, 305,098 and 304,490. Even so, Rounds's margin was six points smaller than Rhoden's. Turnout cannot explain that gap, so at least part of the Senate move came from voters who split their tickets. The rest of the eight-point shift could be either. Results become official when the state canvass ends on Nov. 10.12
