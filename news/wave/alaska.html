Alaska moves 15 points left as Peltola leads Sullivan, count unfinished

Mary Peltola leads Sen. Dan Sullivan by 1.7 points with 84 percent of the vote counted, in a state Donald Trump carried by 13.8. A ranked-choice tally on Nov. 18 may decide the race.

Two years ago Alaska gave Donald Trump a 13.8-point margin. A week after the Nov. 3 election, Mary Peltola, the Democratic former congresswoman, leads Sen. Dan Sullivan, the Republican incumbent, by 118,926 votes to 114,676. That is 49.0 percent to 47.2, with about 84 percent of the expected vote counted. Her 1.7-point lead is a 15.5-point move toward the Democrats since 2024, a little more than the typical county's move of 14.1 points nationally. The Associated Press has not called the race.

Alaska has no counties. It reports by state House district, so The Watch's map has no local count here, and this piece deals only in statewide numbers.

Neither candidate has a majority, and in Alaska that matters. A candidate needs to receive more than 50% of first-choice votes to win outright. If no candidate reaches that threshold, then ranked choice tabulation occurs1: the last-place candidate is dropped and those ballots move to their next choice. The Anchorage Daily News reported that ranked choice tabulation is scheduled to take place Nov. 18, and election results are set to be certified Nov. 25.1

Fish, prices and a second Dan Sullivan

Peltola, who is Yup'ik, was the first Alaska Native to serve in Congress.2 She lost her 2024 re-election bid by 3 points, as President Donald Trump carried Alaska by 13 points.3 Alaska Public Media reported that she ran mostly on affordability, health care costs and accusing Republicans of flagrant corruption4. She also pitched herself as the candidate who would crack down on salmon bycatch by the Bering Sea pollock fleet. The Alaska Beacon described fish as central to the campaign, with critical salmon runs in Alaska rivers struggling5. The Daily News noted record spending, unprecedented fundraising by Peltola, and campaign rallies held in Alaska by Republican President Donald Trump and his vice president6 to help Sullivan.

The last weeks were taken up by a second candidate named Dan Sullivan, a retired teacher from Petersburg. The Alaska Supreme Court ruled this summer that the Petersburg man has a right to be on the ballot.7 Trump said he ordered a Justice Department lawsuit over him, and District Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed7 it before Election Day. Late in the race, The New York Times reported that the Peltola campaign and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee had worked to recruit him. Alaska Public Media said it has not independently confirmed the Times' reporting8, and noted that the Peltola campaign and the DSCC have previously denied involvement8. A Beacon analysis of the ranked ballots indicated there was little to no voter confusion between the senator and the spoiler candidate.9

Who moved, and what the count cannot say

Elsewhere, The Watch sorts counties into big and small, red and blue, and places where turnout fell or held. For Alaska there is no county or district count in our data, so there are no charts to read. We cannot say whether Anchorage, the Matanuska-Susitna Borough or the villages of the west moved most. Even where the data exist, counties are not voters, and a shift in a place's margin is not a shift in any one person's mind.

What the statewide count does show is the size of the electorate. So far 242,947 Senate votes have been counted. If AP's estimate of 84 percent is right, the final total would be about 288,000. Before the election, the Beacon expected between 260,000 and 300,000 Alaska voters10, based on the past two midterms. The 15.5-point move compares that midterm electorate with a presidential-year one, so the count alone cannot separate voters who changed their minds from changes in who voted.

The reporting on turnout points both ways. KYUK reported that tribal advocates saw strong Alaska Native turnout. Turnout in Elim, in the Bering Strait Region, was higher than 61%, and more than 71% turned out in Klukwan11, after only about 30% of Alaska Native voters showed up to the polls in 2024.11 Before Election Day, though, the Daily News found that early turnout is lagging in regions of the state that are rural and predominantly Alaska Native.12 The Beacon reported that early in-person voters included significantly more registered Republicans, which are currently 26.7% of early voters, than registered Democrats, at 18.4%.13 Neither signal settles the question of persuasion versus turnout.

A Democrat leads for governor, too

In the open race for governor, Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins, a Democrat, leads Bernadette Wilson, a Republican, by 119,860 votes to 69,199. That is 49.9 percent to 28.8, with about 84 percent counted. The 21.1-point margin overstates his position, because Wilson was one of three Republicans in the race. The Daily News reported that former Anchorage Mayor Dave Bronson14 and former Attorney General Treg Taylor split much of the rest. It added that if enough of Taylor's and Bronson's supporters ranked Wilson, she could overtake Kreiss-Tomkins to win. But preliminary ballot data shows that Wilson did not receive enough support to move ahead of the Democrat.14

Kreiss-Tomkins, a former lawmaker from Sitka, is on track to be Alaska's first Democratic governor in more than two decades14 if his lead holds. He promised to forward-fund K-12 schools, and he argued that a $4,000 payout this year would drain the fund within decades15. He called instead for a smaller dividend that the fund could sustain. He was also the only candidate in the governor's race who has committed to introducing a balanced budget16.

Neither race is over. The Division of Elections is scheduled to release more results Friday, and Alaska accepts ballots from overseas voters for up to 15 days after the election17. The ranked-choice count follows on Nov. 18.
