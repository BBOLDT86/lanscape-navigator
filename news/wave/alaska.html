Alaska swings 18 points as Peltola unseats Sullivan, Democrats take governorship

Mary Peltola won just over half the first-choice vote, enough to skip a ranked-choice count, in a state Donald Trump carried by nearly 14 points. Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins won the governorship against a divided Republican field.

Mary Peltola, a Democrat and former congresswoman, defeated two-term Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan in Alaska, overcoming allegations that members of her campaign helped recruit a candidate who shares the incumbent's name1, the Associated Press reported. AP made the call two weeks after Election Day. With 99 percent of the expected vote counted, Ms. Peltola had 144,302 votes to Mr. Sullivan's 131,844. That is a lead of 12,458 votes and 4.3 points, in a state Mr. Trump carried by 13.8 points in 2024. The 18.1-point move is larger than the national pattern: across the 2,775 counties The Watch tracks, the vote-weighted move was about 14 points toward Democrats.

Ms. Peltola took 50.1 percent of the 287,896 votes cast. Alaska Public Media noted that this cleared the majority line, meaning no ranked-choice calculation is needed.2 The two other candidates and the write-ins together drew 11,750 votes, fewer than her lead over Mr. Sullivan. NBC News reported that any confusion over the two Sullivans did not affect the result.3 She will be the first Alaska Native person to serve in the U.S. Senate.2 Mr. Sullivan had not conceded, and campaign spokesman Nate Adams said the senator would wait for the official process to finish.4

Fish, prices and a second Dan Sullivan

Ms. Peltola ran on her old slogan, "fish, family and freedom." Axios reported that she stressed affordability, protecting Alaska's fisheries and putting the state's interests ahead of party politics.5 Alaska Public Media found that the cost-of-living argument has special salience in rural Alaska, where groceries and gas can be double or triple the cost elsewhere.2 The Alaska Beacon reported that the two candidates fought bitterly over fisheries management, especially the hot-button issue of trawl bycatch6.

Mr. Sullivan ran with the White House behind him. CNN reported that Trump and Vice President JD Vance separately visited Alaska in the final weeks of the election campaign7. Mr. Sullivan sought to tie Peltola to national Democratic figures, including former President Joe Biden7. In a post-election account, Art Hackney, a Republican strategist who worked for super PACs backing Mr. Sullivan, said the senator's campaign assumed that voters knew about his legislative record8. In his view, that left Ms. Peltola room to say he was doing nothing about prices.

The last week of the race was taken up by the second Dan Sullivan. The New York Times reported that people aligned with Ms. Peltola's campaign helped recruit Daniel J. Sullivan, a retired teacher, onto the ballot. Gov. Mike Dunleavy then ordered an investigation into Democratic Senate candidate Mary Peltola's campaign9. Peltola's campaign and the Democrats' Senate campaign arm have strongly denied any hand in getting the duplicate Sullivan on the ballot.9 The Justice Department had also sued to block certification if the senator lost. NPR reported that a federal judge has thrown out10 that suit.

Who moved, and what the map cannot show

Alaska does not count its vote by county. It reports by state House district, so this state has no county shading on The Watch's map and no county-level voter analysis to draw on. Even where county figures exist, counties are not voters. A county's move cannot show whether people changed their minds or different people turned out.

Reporting on the district returns points to rural Alaska. Axios found that Ms. Peltola's path to Senate history ran through four Alaska Native strongholds11. Those districts cast a small share of the vote but supplied an outsized share of her lead. Axios also noted the limits of that finding: Alaska Native voters today don't vote as a bloc.11 And district returns can't establish how individual Native voters voted — or prove a statewide turnout surge.11 Get Out the Native Vote organizers saw gains in some rural communities, though definitive statewide turnout comparisons remain pending.11

Her support was not only rural. Newsweek described a coalition spanning Anchorage neighborhoods, Fairbanks, fishing communities, the Aleutian chain, and the Arctic.4 Late ballots from the Matanuska-Susitna Borough, the conservative area north of Anchorage, narrowed her lead only slightly. The Hill reported that the gain came largely from the conservative-leaning Mat-Su region12.

Whether turnout or persuasion better explains the 18-point move cannot be settled from The Watch's data. The data block gives the 287,896 votes cast this year but no 2024 vote total to compare it with.

A divided Republican field for governor

The governor's race moved even further on paper. Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins, a former state representative from Sitka, had 145,268 votes to 79,269 for Bernadette Wilson, the Republican endorsed by President Trump. That is a 23.2-point margin and a 37.0-point move from the 2024 presidential vote. The figure overstates the shift, because nearly 60,000 votes went to two other Republicans on the four-candidate ballot. Mr. Kreiss-Tomkins and a second Democrat, Tom Begich, both advanced to the general election out of the nonpartisan primary in August, with both candidates securing more votes than any Republican.13 When Mr. Begich withdrew, that move ensured that Republican support would be split among three candidates.13

Mr. Kreiss-Tomkins campaigned on boosting funding for public schools, lowering energy costs, expanding Medicaid and protecting Alaska's fisheries14. NBC News reported that he was endorsed by GOP Sen. Lisa Murkowski one week before Election Day.14 He will be the state's first Democratic governor since Tony Knowles, who served from 1994 to 200215.

The swing at the top of the ticket did not carry through the whole ballot. In the race for Alaska's only House seat, the AP has not called Alaska's U.S. House race. Congressman Nick Begich has 47.5%. Independent Bill Hill, endorsed by the Democratic Party, trails2. Those are Alaska Public Media's figures; The Watch's data block does not cover that race. Voters also rejected the latest attempt to repeal the state's system of open primaries and ranked choice voting in general elections.16
