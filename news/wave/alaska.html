Alaska moves 17.6 points left as Peltola leads uncalled Senate race

Mary Peltola leads Sen. Dan Sullivan by 3.8 points with 96 percent of the expected vote counted, in a state Donald Trump carried by 13.8. Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins won the governorship against three Republicans.

Two years ago Donald Trump carried Alaska by 13.8 points. In AP's count as of Saturday, Democrat Mary Peltola leads Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan 138,240 votes to 127,828, with 96 percent of the expected vote counted. That is a margin of 3.8 points and a move of 17.6 points toward the Democrats, larger than the typical 14-point move in counties across the country. AP has not called the race.

The governor's race has been called. Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins, a former state representative from the small island community of Sitka, won, according to a race call by the Associated Press.1 At 37, he will be the youngest governor in Alaska history2, and the state's last Democratic governor was Tony Knowles, who left office in 2002.3

A count that isn't finished

Peltola has 49.8 percent of the Senate vote in our data, just short of a majority. The other two candidates on the ballot have 11,300 votes between them. Under Alaska's system, if one candidate has more than 50% of the first-choice votes, they win; if no candidate does, a ranked choice runoff decides the winner.4 The Washington Post reported that officials aim to certify results by Nov. 25.5 Neither the Alaska Beacon nor the Anchorage Daily News has counted the full field. Still, the Beacon's partial run of the ranked-ballot data shows Peltola winning that matchup as well6, and the Daily News found that voters who backed the second Dan Sullivan were more likely to select the leading Democrat as their second preference over the Republican incumbent7.

That second candidate shaped the end of the campaign. Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. is a retired teacher who registered as a Republican, and the Justice Department argued he was deliberately trying to confuse voters.8 President Donald Trump took credit for the legal challenge.9 A federal judge threw the suit out two days before the election, writing that the Justice Department did not have proper legal standing10. The department appealed and is also criminally investigating an alleged plot to add Daniel J. Sullivan to the state's ballot10. Peltola has denied being involved.11

What the campaign was about

Much of the Senate race was about fish. The Alaska Beacon reported that fishing issues became a top campaign issue12, especially trawl bycatch. Peltola cast Sullivan as in the pocket of out-of-state industrial fishing corporations12 and criticized him for supporting "draconian" cuts to NOAA13. Sullivan pointed to his own bycatch bill.

The other theme was cost of living. The Anchorage Daily News reported that soaring energy prices and bills are voters' top concern, polls show14, and that critics point out that Sullivan has steadily backed the president's tariffs and the Iran war14. The same paper noted that the Republican budget package cut food aid and Medicaid through stricter eligibility rules, though Sullivan says it does not cut Medicaid funding for Alaskans.14 Trump and Vice President JD Vance both campaigned in Alaska for the senator.14

Kreiss-Tomkins campaigned on boosting funding for public schools, lowering energy costs, expanding Medicaid and protecting Alaska's fisheries2. He was endorsed by three former governors — a Democrat, a Republican and an independent3. The other Democrat in the race, Tom Begich, dropped out after the primary to consolidate Democratic support behind Kreiss-Tomkins2. The Republicans did not consolidate. Bronson and Taylor refused to suspend their campaigns even after Trump and Wilson called on them to do so.15 That makes the governor's 22.7-point margin, and its 36.5-point move, misleading. The margin measures Kreiss-Tomkins against Wilson alone. Kreiss-Tomkins got 139,293 votes and Wilson 77,110, and the other 57,744 went mostly to the two other Republicans. Kreiss-Tomkins's own share was 50.8 percent.

Who moved, and what the count can't say

The Watch's voter charts are built from county counts, and Alaska doesn't have them. It reports results by state House district, not by borough, so this state's map has no county shading. That means nothing here can say whether big places or small ones, the reddest areas or the bluest, moved most. Even where county counts exist, counties are not voters. The data block also has no 2024 vote total for Alaska, so it cannot show whether the 17.6-point move came from people changing their minds or from who turned out. The 277,368 Senate ballots counted so far cannot settle that.

The two races do offer one clue. The Democrats got nearly the same number of votes in both: 138,240 for Peltola and 139,293 for Kreiss-Tomkins. Everyone else got 139,128 votes in the Senate race and 134,854 in the governor's race. In both races, the people who voted split almost evenly between the Democrat and everyone else. The governor's lopsided margin came mostly from how the Republican vote split, not from a different set of voters.

Reporting suggests turnout favored Democrats, but it does not prove it. Before Election Day, Alaska Public Media found the state on pace to match 2024's record-high early voting numbers16. The Beacon reported that the number of Democratic early votes was disproportionately high17 compared with each party's share of registered voters. Ranked-ballot data from Alaska Public Media found the Republicans' "rank the red" message broke through, but it may not be enough18. A Beacon analysis, as described by The Hill, found little to no voter confusion between the senator and the spoiler candidate19. The Peltola campaign said late ballots from the Republican-leaning Matanuska-Susitna Borough cut into her lead less than expected. That is the campaign's claim, and the final count, not the campaign, will settle it.
