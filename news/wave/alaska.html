Alaska swings with the nation, leaving Peltola and Sullivan nearly tied

Mary Peltola leads Sen. Dan Sullivan by 637 votes with about three-quarters of the expected vote counted, a 14-point move from 2024. Roughly 75,000 uncounted ballots and a likely ranked-choice tabulation are still ahead.

Alaska moved almost exactly as far as the rest of the country. Donald Trump carried the state by 13.8 points in 2024. In AP's count as of Saturday, Democrat Mary Peltola led Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan by 0.3 points, 102,962 votes to 102,325. That is a move of 14.1 points toward the Democrats, the same as the typical move across the 2,775 counties The Watch has mapped nationally, 2,673 of which shifted left.

The race is not decided, and AP has not called it. The 213,733 votes counted are about 74 percent of what AP expects, which leaves roughly 75,000 ballots by that estimate. Neither candidate has a majority. Alaska does not have to report more votes until Nov. 10, when most remaining early and mail ballots will be counted1, and election officials will continue counting ballots until Nov. 18, with a target to certify results on Nov. 25.2 In the meantime, Peltola's campaign is suing the state to access records of rejected absentee ballots3, The Hill reported.

Prices, Medicaid and a second Dan Sullivan

The Anchorage Daily News reported that many Election Day voters expressed frustrations with the rising cost of living4. Peltola focused on issues such as inflation and record-high fuel prices caused by the unpopular war in Iran, income inequality, and cuts to Medicaid in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.5 Sullivan highlighted tax cuts in the One Big Beautiful Bill and other accomplishments, such as the state's military buildup and efforts to expand oil and gas opportunities on federal lands5. The race drew record spending5, and Trump and his vice president came to Alaska to campaign for Sullivan. Near Palmer, the Daily News met a small-business owner who voted for Trump in 2016 and said she voted "pretty much 'D' all the way."6 She was angry about tariffs and diesel prices. One voter is a story, not a trend.

A dispute over the ballot itself took over the final days. The New York Times reported efforts by the Peltola campaign and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee to recruit spoilers onto the ballot, including Dan J. Sullivan, a retired teacher from Petersburg7. The campaign for Peltola has repeatedly denied any involvement8. The Justice Department sued to block certification if the senator lost by fewer votes than his namesake received, and a judge dismissed the case Sunday, saying no such investigation is possible without violating ballot secrecy.9 Late Tuesday, the Alaska Beacon reported, Trump told the Times he would not challenge the results of the election.10

The two minor candidates and write-ins hold about 8,400 votes, roughly 4 percent. Those are the ballots that ranked-choice tabulation would pass on to voters' next choices, and early reads of the ballot data disagree about who gains. The Beacon's analysis of the state's ballot file found Sullivan will gain hundreds more redistributed votes than Peltola11 if the race goes to tabulation. That file leaves out ballots counted by hand and the early and absentee ballots that haven't been counted at all.11 Alaska Public Media found a strong overlap in support for Democratic U.S. Senate candidate Mary Peltola and Daniel J. Sullivan Jr.12; more of his backers ranked Peltola second than ranked the senator second.

Who moved, and what this count cannot show

Most states get a county-by-county reading here. Alaska does not. Alaska does not have counties, instead separating its votes by state house district.1 The map has no local count for the state, and there is no breakdown of big places against small or the reddest against the bluest. Counties are not voters even where they exist; in Alaska the map cannot even show places.

The statewide numbers also cannot separate persuasion from turnout. A 14-point move could come from voters changing their minds or from different people showing up, and the data block has no 2024 vote total to compare against. There is reporting on both possibilities. On the turnout side, the Daily News reported that early turnout is lagging in regions of the state that are rural and predominantly Alaska Native13, a long-running pattern that organizers tried to break this year. Indian Country Today reported a sharp rise in August primary turnout in tribally affiliated precincts compared with 2024. The Beacon noted that some votes from rural precincts believed to favor Peltola10 remained uncounted. Voters like the Palmer business owner point to persuasion. These counts cannot say which mattered more.

Governor: one Democrat against three Republicans

The governor's race shows a bigger move, 33.7 points, but most of it comes from how the field was set up. Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins has 103,837 votes, or 49.1 percent. Bernadette Wilson has 61,734, or 29.2 percent. The other 45,766 votes, 21.7 percent, went largely to Dave Bronson and Treg Taylor14, the two other Republicans. Kreiss-Tomkins's vote total is close to Peltola's. The difference is the split on the Republican side, which is why the map uses the Senate race.

Wilson, who was endorsed by President Donald Trump15, ran on a promise of massive cash payouts to eligible Alaskans, though she did not provide a clear explanation for how the state could afford them.2 Bronson and Taylor pitched themselves as more moderate Republican alternatives2 and asked their supporters to rank all Republicans on the ballot.2 The Daily News noted that if a high enough number of Taylor and Bronson voters ranked Wilson, she could defeat Kreiss-Tomkins.2 Kreiss-Tomkins, a former lawmaker from Sitka, pitched himself as a pragmatic leader who would appoint Republicans to his cabinet.2 AP had not called this race either as of our data. If he wins, he would be the first Democratic governor since Tony Knowles, who had back-to-back terms from 1994 to 2002.15

The system that may decide both races is on the ballot too. Ballot Measure 2, which would repeal ranked-choice voting and open primaries, is on track to narrowly fail, as of votes counted Wednesday afternoon16, the Daily News reported. It has not been called.
