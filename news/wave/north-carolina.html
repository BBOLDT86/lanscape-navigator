The wave · state by state · North Carolina

Every North Carolina county moved left as Roy Cooper won by nearly 20

The former governor turned a state Donald Trump carried by 3 points into a 19.6-point Senate win. The shift was almost the same size in mountain hollows, exurbs and cities, and largest where turnout fell furthest.

Roy Cooper, the Democrat who served two terms as governor, beat Michael Whatley, a former Republican National Committee chairman, by 19.6 points in North Carolina's open U.S. Senate race. The count was 2,467,972 votes to 1,643,114, with about 99 percent of the expected vote in. Donald Trump carried the state by 3.2 points in 2024, so the margin moved 22.8 points toward the Democrats. The former governor becomes the first Democrat elected to the Senate from North Carolina since 2008.1

The move reached every part of the state. All 100 counties shifted toward the Democrats. The typical county moved about 20 points, and the statewide shift, weighted by votes, was 21 points. Nationally, the typical county moved 14. Cooper carried 21 counties that Trump won in 2024, from Johnston and Cabarrus near Raleigh and Charlotte to Henderson and Haywood in the mountains. No county moved from the Democrats to the Republicans. Whatley still led in 57 counties, most of them small.

A lopsided campaign

Forty years after his first election, Cooper still hasn't lost a race in North Carolina2, Carolina Public Press noted. This was Whatley's first run for office. Whatley's campaign raised $13.7 million to Cooper's $49.6 million2, although outside groups spent more on Whatley's side. The money for him also thinned out. WRAL reported that the Senate Leadership Fund in April had pledged to spend $71 million to help Whatley win, much of which never materialized3.

NPR reported that Whatley's campaign centered around crime and immigration issues1, while Cooper's campaign painted him as a lobbyist for Big Oil who would become a rubber stamp for Trump in the Senate.1 Hurricane Helene was a running theme. Blue Ridge Public Radio reported that Whatley, who has been called the Helene "recovery czar,"4 faced accusations that he had not done enough to get the region federal aid. CBS News pointed to the president's unpopular war with Iran and high gas prices5. In the NBC News exit poll, Whatley was seen as too supportive of Trump (60%)6.

From the Blue Ridge to the Sandhills

The biggest move in the state came in Yancey County, in the mountains northeast of Asheville. Trump won it by 34 points; Whatley won it by 6. That is a 28-point move. Nearby Madison County flipped to Cooper, Jackson moved 27 points and Haywood flipped by half a point. These are counties where Helene killed more than 100 people in western North Carolina7. The farthest-west counties moved less: Cherokee shifted 15 points and still gave Whatley a 43-point margin.

Johnston County, southeast of Raleigh, went from a 22-point Trump win to a 6-point Cooper win. Neighboring Lee also moved 28 points, and Harnett flipped by less than a point. In Gaston County, which Trump carried by 25 points, the two candidates finished even when rounded to one decimal. Gastonia, the county seat, is where Whatley told supporters that he called Cooper to concede3.

The counties around the big military bases also moved sharply. Hoke moved 27 points, Cumberland 22 and Onslow 23. In Fayetteville, CityView spoke with a voter who said he backed Trump in 2024 but waited more than an hour to vote for Cooper. He cit[ed] high gasoline prices and the ongoing Iran war.8 Robeson County moved 27 points, from Trump +28 to Whatley by under a point. The Border Belt Independent noted that generations of Robeson County voters, including members of the Lumbee Tribe of North Carolina, reliably picked Democratic candidates.9

The smallest moves came in the northeast. Chowan moved 11 points, and Bertie, Warren, Northampton and Hertford each moved 12 to 13. The Associated Press has described this area as the northeastern counties with large proportions of Black residents10. Most of them were already Democratic. Durham County moved 14 points to Cooper +76, the smallest shift of any large county.

Who moved, and why turnout is only part of it

The charts beside this section compare counties, not voters, so they show where the vote shifted, not which people changed their minds. By that measure, the shift barely depended on a county's 2024 result. Counties Trump won by 30 points or more moved 21.5 points, weighted by votes. Those he won by 10 to 30 moved 22.1, and those Kamala Harris won by 10 to 30 moved 21.0. Only the three bluest counties, Durham, Orange and Mecklenburg, lagged, at 18.7. County size mattered more. The 25 counties with fewer than 10,000 votes moved 17.4 points. The 20 counties with 50,000 to 250,000 votes cast 44 percent of the ballots and moved 21.4 points.

Turnout was 74 percent of the 2024 total, and it lines up with the move. The seven counties that cast less than 65 percent of their 2024 vote moved 23.8 points. Robeson, Onslow and Surry are among them. The eight counties that cast 80 percent or more moved 19.9 points. The correlation is −0.39: counties where turnout fell further tended to move further left. That suggests part of the shift came from who stayed home.

Turnout does not explain all of it. Where turnout held up best, counties still moved by about 20 points. Henderson County cast 82 percent of its 2024 vote and still went from Trump +15 to Cooper +6. Reporting on the early vote fits both explanations. WBTV found that registered Republicans dropped five percentage points, making up 26% of early ballots compared to 31% four years ago11. NC Newsline, citing the Southern Coalition for Social Justice, reported that Black early voter turnout reached roughly 584,000, up 52% from the 2022 midterms12. A county count cannot separate voters who changed their minds from voters who stayed home. But a shift this even, across red and blue counties alike, is hard to explain by turnout alone.
