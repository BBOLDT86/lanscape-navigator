The wave · state by state · Georgia

All 159 Georgia counties moved toward Ossoff, from Dalton to DeKalb

Two years after Donald Trump carried Georgia, Jon Ossoff won a second Senate term by 18 points. The move reached every county, was largest in the north Georgia counties with many Hispanic residents and in the Atlanta suburbs, and was smallest in the southwest.

Donald Trump carried Georgia by 2.2 points in 2024. On Tuesday, Sen. Jon Ossoff beat Rep. Mike Collins by 18.0 points, 2,466,422 votes to 1,712,444, with 99 percent of the expected vote counted. That is a 20-point move in the margin, and it showed up everywhere. All 159 counties moved toward the Democrat. None moved toward the Republican. Twelve counties that Trump won voted for Ossoff. The race was called by the Associated Press less than an hour after polls closed Tuesday.1

The typical county moved 14.5 points. Weighted by votes cast, the move was 17.9. The governor's race on the same ballots moved less. Keisha Lance Bottoms, a former mayor of Atlanta, beat Rick Jackson by 8.7 points, a move of 10.9 from the 2024 baseline. The AP reported that she is the first Black woman to be elected governor of Georgia2. Republicans had maintained control of the governor's office since Sonny Perdue defeated Democratic incumbent Roy Barnes in 2002.3

A weakened nominee, and a split ticket

Republicans had targeted the seat. Ossoff was the only Democratic senator up for reelection in a state won by President Donald Trump in 2024.1 Gov. Brian Kemp did not run for it and instead backed Derek Dooley, a former football coach. Dooley emerged from a three-way contest for the nomination only to fall to Collins in a runoff.1 Collins then campaigned under a House Ethics Committee investigation. Reuters reported that he was accused of paying more than $10,000 to an intern who was dating his chief of staff and performed no work4. His office called the complaint bogus. The Georgia Recorder reported that another former staffer has posted Nazi and Ku Klux Klan imagery on social media.1 FOX 5 Atlanta reported that Ossoff campaigned on restoring Medicaid cuts and challenging President Donald Trump, while Collins emphasized his support for the president.5

The two statewide races drew almost the same number of votes: 4,181,147 for governor and 4,178,866 for Senate. Even so, Bottoms finished about 195,000 votes behind Ossoff, and Jackson finished about 197,000 ahead of Collins. Axios Atlanta noted that fewer people voted for Bottoms than they did for Ossoff and nearly all Democrats running for statewide office6. Jackson spent heavily from his own fortune. Axios, citing the Atlanta Journal-Constitution, put his spending at more than $220 million6. The Georgia Recorder reported that Bottoms ran a centrist campaign focused on pocketbook issues7.

From Dalton to the southwest corner

The largest move was in Whitfield County, home to the carpet-making city of Dalton. Trump won it by 44.5 points. Collins won it by 13.2, a 31-point move. In April, the Journal-Constitution reported that the county's heavily Hispanic precincts swung sharply left after immigration crackdowns8 in a special congressional election. The Georgia Recorder found that nearly a quarter of registered voters in Dalton's Whitfield County are Latino9. A Republican state lawmaker from Dalton, Kasey Carpenter, said he fears the increased immigration enforcement could turn away formerly Republican-voting Hispanics in the area.10 Hall County, around Gainesville, moved 23 points, from R+43.8 to R+21.1. The Journal-Constitution had listed it among the counties with the state's most Hispanic precincts11.

In the Atlanta suburbs, Gwinnett moved 26 points, from D+16.5 to D+42.6, on 343,835 votes. CNN had described Gwinnett's large Black, Asian and Latino population12. Paulding County, west of Atlanta, went from R+24.1 to R+0.4. Forsyth went from R+33.1 to R+11.3, and Cherokee from R+39.0 to R+20.6.

The counties that flipped were spread across the state. They included Fayette in the southern suburbs, Spalding around Griffin, Lowndes around Valdosta, and Houston, home to Warner Robins. Reporting from Central Georgia said a Democrat, Renita Duncan, flipped a Warner Robins-area state House seat. Duncan credited Keisha Lance Bottoms, the Democratic nominee for governor, for campaigning in the county.13

The smallest moves were in the southwest. Quitman, Randolph, Clay, Early, Terrell and Miller counties, along the Alabama line, each moved 7 or 8 points. Several of them already voted Democratic. Hancock County, in middle Georgia, moved 7 points, from D+35.4 to D+42.6.

Who moved: the voters behind the map

Counties are not voters, but the breakdowns show a move that was broad rather than concentrated. Counties of every size moved, though the smallest moved least. The 88 counties with under 10,000 votes moved 13.8 points, weighted by votes. Mid-sized and large counties moved between 17.9 and 18.9.

Partisanship made little difference. The 97 counties Trump won by 30 points or more cast a third of the vote and moved 18.1 points, about the same as the state. The 13 counties Harris won by 10 to 30 points moved the most, 21.2. The nine most Democratic counties, including Fulton, DeKalb and Clayton, moved 14.5. Their margins were already lopsided, so there was less room to grow.

Turnout fell everywhere. Georgians cast 4,178,866 votes for Senate, 80 percent of the 2024 presidential total. Republicans have pointed to that drop. Georgia Republican leader Josh McKoon said voter turnout south of I-20 did not keep pace with four years ago14. He also said that independents clearly appearing to break strongly for the Democratic ticket14 was part of the story. The county numbers give only partial support to the turnout explanation. The 134 counties where turnout fell to between 65 and 80 percent of 2024 moved 18.0 points. The 22 counties that held between 80 and 95 percent moved 17.7. The correlation between a county's turnout change and its move is weak (r = -0.16).

A county count cannot tell voters who changed their minds from voters who stayed home, and these data include no Census figures on the counties' populations. The clearest sign of voters making different choices is the gap between the two races on the same ballots, where Ossoff ran about nine points ahead of Bottoms. Georgia did not move alone. The Hill reported that Republicans nationally were weighed down by issues including the unpopular war in Iran, Trump's slumping approval numbers and concerns about the cost of living.15
