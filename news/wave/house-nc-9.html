Army veteran Richard Ojeda unseats NRCC chair Richard Hudson in North Carolina

Richard Ojeda, a retired Army major, beat the chairman of House Republicans' campaign arm by 2.2 points in a Fort Bragg-to-Greensboro district. Every county in the district swung at least 19 points toward Democrats in the Senate race.

Richard Ojeda is a retired major who served 24 years, with 15 at Fort Bragg1. He has unseated Rep. Richard Hudson, the chairman of the National Republican Congressional Committee, in North Carolina's 9th District. With 99 percent of the expected vote counted, Ojeda had 138,707 votes to Hudson's 132,692. That is a lead of 6,015 votes, or 2.2 points. The Associated Press has called the race. Hudson conceded his reelection bid Wednesday, his 55th birthday2, and quoted Ronald Reagan, NC Newsline reported: "we lost a skirmish, but the battle goes on."3

The race

Ojeda had been in politics before. He served in the West Virginia State Senate before unsuccessfully seeking the Democratic nomination in the U.S. Senate race in 20204, and he moved to Moore County in 20205. The Fayetteville Observer noted that Ojeda now lives in Aberdeen6, while Hudson, first elected to Congress in 2012, lives in Southern Pines6, a few miles away.

When the Observer asked Ojeda to name the state's biggest issue, he said affordability. He pointed to gas, groceries, health care, and prescription drugs6. Roll Call reported that throughout the campaign, he attacked the incumbent over his support for Trump's agenda, particularly the war in Iran.7 On election night, ABC11 found that some voters cited loyalty to Hudson, others said pocketbook issues and the war in Iran influenced their decision8. Hudson's campaign leaned on his record. His campaign manager, Cam Mills, told Carolina Journal that Hudson "wins because he delivers for the people he represents."9 One example CityView cited was obtaining 20 acres of federal property for North Carolina to build a new nursing home for veterans1.

For most of the cycle the seat sat outside the national contest. Democrats' House campaign arm added it to its target list in August. Republicans saw the move as Democrats baiting them into spending more money in the district, pulling resources away from closer races10. In October, the Washington Examiner reported that Hudson put $200,000 into his own first television ad campaign11. Ojeda's first ad called him Congress's "biggest recipient of big pharmaceutical dollars"11. A Democratic ad called "Corrupt" begins airing Friday in the Greensboro television market12 two days later. Before that buy, AdImpact counted $571,000 for Republicans and $351,000 for Democrats13 in ad spending. Ratings shifted late: Inside Elections moved its rating from "solid Republican" to "likely Republican"13 on Oct. 29.

The district

According to the Observer, the district includes Fort Bragg, most of Fayetteville and parts of Cumberland County, as well as all of Alamance, Hoke, Moore and Randolph counties and portions of Chatham and Guilford counties.14 It stretches from the Sandhills north to Asheboro, Burlington and part of Greensboro.

The Watch's map compares each county's 2024 presidential vote with this year's Senate race. In that race, Democrat Roy Cooper beat Republican Michael Whatley by 19.6 points statewide, a 22.8-point move from Donald Trump's 3.2-point win in 2024. The seven counties in the 9th moved a combined 21.3 points, slightly less than the state. The figures cover whole counties, so Guilford, Cumberland and Chatham include voters outside the district.

Every county moved at least 20 points. Hoke moved the most, from D+5.9 to D+33.1. Alamance was the only county to switch sides, going from R+8.2 to D+14.7. Randolph remained the most Republican county at R+33.9, down from R+57.2. Moore, where both candidates live, moved the least, from R+29.5 to R+9.8. Guilford went from D+21.8 to D+42.1, Cumberland from D+13.4 to D+35.1 and Chatham from D+12.5 to D+32.9.

These moves do not prove that voters changed their minds. Hoke cast 15,939 Senate votes and Randolph 47,603, and county totals cannot tell voters who switched parties apart from changes in who showed up. Turnout looked different this year. WUNC reported that Republicans made up 26% of early voters this year, compared with 31% four years ago.15

What it means

NBC News noted that Republicans have held the district since 196316, though the lines have been redrawn many times. Hudson began representing the 9th District in January 2023, having served the 8th District since 2013.17 Roll Call called him the second party campaign chief to lose his seat during a midterm election this decade.7 CityView reported that Ojeda will be the 9th District's first Hispanic congressman5. Ojeda told the Observer, "The people of NC-09 looked at the guy running the entire Republican money machine, and they fired him."18

The result was close. Ojeda won by 2.2 points, far less than Cooper's statewide margin. With 99 percent of the expected vote counted, about 1 percent remains, and the election results are unofficial until Nov. 13, when county boards of elections conduct their canvass.14 Another Republican lost nearby: NC Newsline reported that Rep. Addison McDowell also conceded on Wednesday, losing to Democratic High Point Mayor Cyril Jefferson.3 Nationally, 252 House seats have been called for Democrats and 161 for Republicans, with 22 still uncalled. A majority is 218.
