The wave · state by state · New Hampshire

New Hampshire moved left everywhere, but kept its Republican governor

All ten counties moved toward Democrat Chris Pappas, who won the open Senate seat by nearly 14 points and flipped Rockingham, Carroll and Sullivan counties. On the same ballots, Gov. Kelly Ayotte won a second term by about four.

Chris Pappas won New Hampshire's open U.S. Senate seat by 13.7 points. He had 355,720 votes to 268,340 for John E. Sununu, with 98.9 percent of the expected vote counted. Kamala Harris carried the state by 2.8 points in 2024, so the Democratic margin grew by about 11 points. All ten counties moved toward the Democrats. Three that Donald Trump carried two years ago, Rockingham, Carroll and Sullivan, went for Pappas.

The swing reached every county, but it was smaller than in much of the country. The typical New Hampshire county moved 11.8 points to the left. Across the 2,775 counties counted nationally, the typical move was 14.1.

The Boston Globe reported that the Associated Press called the Senate race at 8:22 p.m., with just 20 percent of precincts reporting.1 Sununu conceded the race to Pappas shortly after polls closed at 8 p.m.2, speaking at the McIntyre Ski Area in Manchester, according to NHPR. Pappas, 46, becomes the first openly gay man to be elected to the Senate in American history.1

A Senate race fought over Washington

Sununu was trying to win back a seat he had held before. Had he won, the Globe noted, he would have returned some 18 years after he lost this very seat in 2008 to now-retiring Senator Jeanne Shaheen.1 The New Hampshire Bulletin reported that both Pappas and Sununu tried to define the race by affordability issues3. Pappas blamed President Trump for rising prices, pointing to a war in Iran that has spiked fuel prices and a tariff campaign that hit New Hampshire companies doing business with Canadian partners particularly hard.3

Trump endorsed Sununu in the Republican primary, but Sununu kept his distance from the president. In the final debate he said he opposed Trump's "trade war with Canada"4, NHPR reported. Pappas kept tying him to the president anyway and asked voters to choose between "Team New Hampshire versus Team Trump."2 Outside groups spent heavily for Sununu, among them the Koch family-funded Americans for Prosperity Action, and entities tied to both Trump and Elon Musk.2

The move went well beyond the Senate race. The Bulletin reported a preliminary estimate by the House clerk counted 291 Democrats, 108 Republicans, and one independent representative, though those totals could change after recounts.5 Democrats also won all five seats on the Executive Council, wiping out Republicans' current 4-1 majority.5 House Speaker Sherman Packard narrowly lost his own seat. A political analyst quoted by the Concord Monitor called it "more of a Washington-based vote than a New Hampshire-based vote."6

Who moved

Counties are not voters, and the charts beside this section describe places, not people. Still, the pattern is clear. The four largest counties are Hillsborough, Rockingham, Strafford and Merrimack. Together they cast 76 percent of the vote and moved 12.3 points, weighted by votes. The six smaller counties moved 10.3.

The places that were already Democratic moved the most. Strafford and Grafton, which Harris won by 10 to 30 points, moved 13.3 points, weighted by votes. Hillsborough, Merrimack and Cheshire, which she won by less than 10, moved 12.9. Rockingham, Carroll and Sullivan, which Trump carried narrowly, moved 9.4. That was the smallest shift of any group, but it was enough to flip all three. Coos and Belknap, Trump's strongest counties, moved 10.7. They were the only two Sununu carried, by 1.4 points in Coos and 3.1 in Belknap.

Turnout explains part of this. Our county tallies show 696,987 votes, against 822,153 in 2024, or about 85 percent. The counties where the vote held up best moved furthest. Strafford, home to Dover, Rochester and Durham, was the only county where more votes were counted than in 2024: 85,442 against 76,323. It also had the largest move in the state, 15 points, going from D+12.1 to D+27.3. Across the counties, the correlation between turnout and the size of the move was +0.60.

That fits a story about which voters showed up. It does not settle it. The seven counties where the vote fell to between 65 and 80 percent of 2024 still moved 10.6 points. Carroll, where turnout held up better than in most counties, moved the least, 8 points. A county-level count cannot separate voters who changed their minds from voters who stayed home. The counts are also not finished. AP estimates only 73 percent of Belknap's vote and 75 percent of Merrimack's had been counted, so these turnout shares will rise.

Different answers on the same ballot

Ayotte won by 4.3 points, 324,410 to 297,339, over Cinde Warmington. That leaves an 18-point gap between the top two races on the same ballots. The Concord Monitor credited voters who split their ticket between Pappas and Ayotte6. It pointed to Bedford, a town Pappas carried and Ayotte won comfortably. The paper also noted that Warmington notably flipped the major cities of Manchester and Nashua, Ayotte's hometown6.

The split had shown up in polling before the election. In a Saint Anselm College poll for AARP, the Globe reported, thirteen percent of New Hampshire voters7 said they planned to vote for candidates from both parties. Most of them backed Pappas and Ayotte. NHPR reported that Ayotte worked to keep her distance from the Trump administration8 and stressed low taxes. InDepthNH wrote that Warmington couldn't shake Ayotte labeling her an opioid lobbyist.9 The Monitor reported that Ayotte out-fundraised her 5:1.6 AP declared Ayotte the winner at 12:42 a.m. EST.10

Rockingham shows the change most clearly. Trump carried the county by 2.4 points two years ago, and Pappas won it by 7.0. In Londonderry, a Republican stronghold in the county, the Bulletin reported that Democrats pushed out all Republicans in Londonderry except Lundgren, who hung on by fewer than 200 votes.5 Ayotte held her election night party in Salem, in the same county.
