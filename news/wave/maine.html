The wave · state by state · Maine

Jackson beat Collins, yet Maine's Senate map came out redder than 2024

Troy Jackson unseated Susan Collins by 6.3 points. Even so, 14 of Maine's 16 counties voted more Republican for Senate than they did for president in 2024. That shows how far Collins ran ahead of her party, not that Republicans gained ground.

Almost everywhere The Watch has mapped this year, counties swung toward Democrats. Across the 2,775 counties counted nationally, the typical one moved 14 points left of the 2024 presidential result. Maine is one of the few states where the Senate map leans the other way, and it got there in a race the Democrat won.

With 93.6 percent of the expected vote counted, Troy Jackson leads Sen. Susan Collins 375,516 to 330,994, a margin of 6.3 points. Kamala Harris carried Maine by 6.9 points in 2024, so the Senate result sits 0.6 points to the right of hers. Fourteen counties moved toward Collins. Only Waldo and York moved toward Jackson, each by less than a point, and no county changed sides. Still, the outcome is historic: the win makes Jackson the first Democrat to represent Maine in the Senate since 19951, and Collins conceded the race before the AP made the call.2

Collins ran ahead of her party to the end

The governor's race on the same ballot shows what the Senate map hides. Hannah Pingree, a former Maine House speaker, beat the Republican Bobby Charles 387,226 to 265,982. Her 17.1-point margin is 10 points to the left of Harris's. Collins drew 65,012 more votes than Charles did, and Jackson drew 11,710 fewer than Pingree. Part of that gap comes from the independent Rick Bennett, who was also on the governor's ballot3. But much of it is ticket-splitting. Before the election, a CBS News poll found that Collins peels off one in five voters who disapprove of Trump4. The Maine Monitor spoke with a Belfast voter who voted for Democrat Hannah Pingree for governor and Collins for U.S. Senate.5

The map compares the Senate race with Donald Trump, who has never won Maine6, not with Collins's own past. Measured against her, the picture changes. A Maine Monitor analysis published by the Bangor Daily News found that Lewiston, Bangor and Auburn, which Collins carried in 2020, switched to support Jackson.7 The Portland Press Herald reported that Collins tried to localize the race by making it about delivering federal resources to Maine, while Jackson sought to nationalize it8. In NBC News' voter survey, 57 percent named Collins as better at getting federal funding for Maine, against 28 percent for Jackson. Maine Public reported that the split-ticket voters Collins once relied on did not show up in enough numbers for her to overcome Jackson.2

Who moved: the interior more than the coast

Counties are not voters, but the county pattern is clear. Rural, Republican Maine moved most. Piscataquis, the only county with fewer than 10,000 votes, moved 7 points right, from R+29.2 to R+36.2. The six counties Trump won by 10 to 30 points cast a quarter of the vote and moved 3.1 points right when weighted by votes. The three he won by less than 10 (Kennebec, Androscoggin and Franklin) moved 3.2. The five coastal counties Harris won by 10 to 30 points, 30 percent of the vote, moved 0.7. Cumberland, home to Portland and a quarter of all ballots, slipped 1.5 points but still went D+34.8. The five largest counties, two-thirds of the vote, moved 1.6 points right. The 10 mid-sized counties moved 2.3.

Turnout fits the same pattern, with a catch. These counties cast 702,729 votes, 85 percent of their 2024 total. The four counties below 80 percent (Aroostook, Piscataquis, Somerset and Washington) moved 3.8 points right, against 1.7 for the rest. The correlation between holding turnout and moving less to the right is +0.43. But those four are also the least fully counted, with Washington at 54 percent of its expected vote. Part of their apparent drop is ballots not yet counted, and their margins could still change. Statewide, Secretary of State Shenna Bellows said more voters turned out to vote than ever before in a midterm election9.

Turnout differences alone cannot explain the map. County counts cannot separate voters who changed their minds from voters who stayed home. But the gap between Pingree and Jackson comes from the same voters filling out the same ballots, so it reflects their choices, not who turned out.

The County stood with its own

Both candidates are from Aroostook, and Jackson was the first challenger from Collins' home county to take her on.6 The Bangor Daily News reported that Collins closed out her campaign Monday in the Aroostook towns that elected Troy Jackson to the Maine Senate10. The paper also noted that Republicans had grown from 29 percent to 37 percent of active voters in his old district since she last ran. Aroostook went from R+26.2 to R+30.1, with 80 percent counted. The County, a local news site, reported that every part of the county swung red for Collins — including Jackson's native Allagash11.

The interior's lean toward Republicans also showed up down the ballot. Paul LePage won the vast majority of rural towns in northern, central and downeast Maine12 in the 2nd Congressional District. Matt Dunlap, the Democrat, conceded13 on Thursday. Maine Public noted that this may make Maine perhaps the only state — where Republicans flipped a district12 this year.

The main exception is York County, which is fully counted and moved half a point toward Jackson. The Boston Globe reported that Jackson won Biddeford after a year of immigration enforcement there. Charlie Dingman, the state Democratic chair, called ICE enforcement and the fatal violence that took place in Biddeford just months ago14 decisive. Lance Dutson, a Republican strategist who worked on past Collins campaigns, didn't think immigration was a "top shelf" issue for Maine voters.14 The county numbers cannot settle that disagreement.
