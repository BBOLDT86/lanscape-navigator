Austin's Travis County, Talarico's home base, moves 19 points left

What Travis County did

Travis County is home to Austin, the state capital, and the University of Texas. It was already the most Democratic of Texas's big counties, and on Nov. 3 it went further. James Talarico, the Democratic state representative, beat Ken Paxton, the Republican attorney general, 430,707 votes to 109,791 in the county's U.S. Senate race. That is a margin of 58.5 points, with 99% of the expected vote counted. Kamala Harris carried the county by 39.3 points over Donald Trump in 2024, so the county moved 19 points toward the Democrats.

The race had been seen as one that would turn on this county. On the eve of the vote, the Texas Tribune wrote that Talarico must earn epic returns in Travis County, the bluest part of the state.1 He closed his campaign at one of Austin's iconic live music venues.1

Statewide, Talarico won by 12.1 points in a state Trump carried by 13.7, a 26-point move. All 254 Texas counties moved left. Travis ranked 112th by the size of its move, and it moved less than any of the state's other big counties: Harris (27 points), Bexar (26), Tarrant (24), Dallas (23), Collin (23) and Denton (22). Part of the reason is arithmetic. Paxton won only about a fifth of the county's vote, so there was little Republican vote left to lose. Measured against Democratic counties of similar size elsewhere, though, the move was large. Cook County, Illinois, moved 18 points and Milwaukee County, Wisconsin, 14. Middlesex County, Massachusetts, and Contra Costa County, California, moved 5 each, and Santa Clara County, California, 1.

Who turned out, and why

Travis County cast 548,521 votes, 95% of its 2024 presidential total, which is unusual for a midterm. KUT reported before Election Day that the county's early vote was the highest it has seen for a midterm election since 1998, which is the earliest available data2. It also was higher than the turnout for the 2012 presidential election2. County Clerk Dyana Limón-Mercado said her office set out to make this "the most accessible election in our community's history" by increasing the number of polling locations and expanding voting hours.3 The Austin American-Statesman wrote that the record early vote might have contributed to Talarico's success.4

Because nearly as many people voted as in 2024, the move can't be explained only by Republicans staying home. But a county's count also can't separate voters who changed sides from changes in who showed up, and this result mixes both. Statewide reporting suggests both played a part. The Tribune wrote that Talarico needed a surge in Democratic enthusiasm, enough independents and swing voters, and enough Republicans — from demoralized base voters to frustrated Cornyn supporters — to either stay home, leave the Senate race blank or vote third-party.5 The Associated Press reported that Paxton's primary win over Sen. John Cornyn bolstered Talarico's appeal to Republican and independent voters.6 According to the Tribune, Paxton's favorability stayed deeply underwater, and even many of his supporters told pollsters they were voting more against Talarico than for Paxton.5

Inside the county, the Statesman's precinct map found Talarico's strongest support came from central and South Austin7. His support weakened toward the edges of the city, particularly in precincts north of Travis County near Leander, Georgetown and Hutto.7

What it means in Austin

In the county's own races, Democratic Congressman Greg Casar has won the seat for U.S. House District 37, defeating first-time Republican challenger Lauren Peña8. Casar ran for the Austin seat Lloyd Doggett is leaving after his own district was scrambled9. Austin voters also rejected Proposition I, a citywide audit measure backed by the PAC Save Austin Now. The Statesman reported that it lost overall but passed in the parts of Austin that lie in Williamson and Hays counties. Mayor Kirk Watson said the broader election results signaled voter concerns over affordability.10

The county Republican Party has little local presence. When the primaries were held in March, the Statesman noted that no Republicans filed11 for two Travis County Commissioners Court seats. The bigger change is in the Capitol downtown. Democrats seized control of the Texas House in Tuesday's midterms for the first time in more than two decades12, including a suburban district north of Austin13. The Tribune reported that both parties are already considering whether Democrats could retain their new seats in 202814 without another wave like this one.
