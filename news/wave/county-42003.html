In Allegheny County, Shapiro doubles Harris's margin as turnout falls

Pennsylvania's governor won the Pittsburgh-area county by nearly 44 points on about seven-tenths of the 2024 vote. Its 23-point swing toward Democrats almost exactly matched the statewide swing, and local mapping found the biggest gains in the suburbs.

Gov. Josh Shapiro, a Democrat, beat Republican state Treasurer Stacy Garrity in Allegheny County by 368,577 votes to 142,682, a margin of 43.7 points. With 98.66 percent of the expected vote counted, that is 23.4 points better than Kamala Harris's 20.3-point margin over Donald Trump in 2024. Some ballots are still being counted, so the final figures may change slightly.

What Allegheny did

This year Allegheny cast more votes than any other county in Pennsylvania: 516,778, against Philadelphia's 490,015. Its swing was close to the state's. Shapiro won Pennsylvania by 22.1 points, 23.8 points better than Harris did when she lost the state by 1.7. Every one of the 67 counties moved left, and the typical county moved 23 points. Allegheny ranked 27th by size of move, so it went along with the statewide shift rather than leading it. Among the other big counties, Philadelphia moved 20 points and Montgomery 23. Bucks moved 26 and went from a near-tie to Democratic, and Lancaster flipped from Republican to Democratic.

Outside the state, the comparison is less even. Dallas County, Texas, moved by the same 23 points. Sacramento moved 10 and San Diego 5. Across the 2,775 counties counted nationwide, the typical move was 14 points.

Allegheny is an educated, middle-income county that is slowly losing people. The Census Bureau puts its 2025 population at 1,225,0351, -2.0%1 since 2020. 45.8%2 of adults hold a bachelor's degree, against 35.7%2 nationally, and median household income is $78,5482, close to the national figure.

Why it moved

Turnout fell. About 72 percent as many people voted as in 2024. Even so, Shapiro's lead in raw votes grew to about 226,000, from roughly 146,000 for Harris. A county total can't tell voters who switched sides apart from voters who stayed home. This move is a mix of both.

Mapping by Pittsburgh's Public Source helps show where it happened. Shapiro improved on Democratic presidential nominee Kamala Harris' 2024 margin in all of Allegheny County's 130 municipalities3, and he beat his own 2022 margins in 112 of them. The outlet found the swings were more pronounced in the suburbs where Democrats were hunting for gains in state House seats, and less pronounced where turnout was less energetic in urban and more racially diverse areas.3 That fits a pattern from 2024, when Public Source found that low-turnout precincts were clustered in liberal areas: Pittsburgh, McKeesport, Penn Hills, Wilkinsburg and other nearby suburbs4.

Data centers were a major issue across the state. City & State reported that the race centered on Shapiro's record in office, coupled with a heavy dose of data center politics5. Spotlight PA reported that both adjusted their positions on data center development as public opinion has shifted against the massive projects this year.6 In the county, a registered Republican told Public Source that data centers worried him more than any other issue and that he was considering voting for Shapiro.

National politics mattered too. WHYY reported that Shapiro benefited from some GOP support and President Donald Trump's sagging favorability.7 In October, a Quinnipiac poll of likely voters statewide found Shapiro winning 17% of Republicans8 and leading among independents 62% to 23%8. In the suburbs, Democratic legislative candidates were counting on Trump's negative approval ratings, and anger over gas prices and the war in Iran9. The ticket also had a local tie: lieutenant governor Austin Davis is Shapiro's running mate10 and lives in the county.

What it means locally

Shapiro's large margin helped Democrats lower on the ballot only in places. The party flipped the 44th state House District in the airport suburbs, where Hadley Haas defeated Republican Rep. Valerie Gaydos. That seat has never been held by a Democrat before.11 But Allegheny County Republicans, though, managed to defend four of their five seats despite historically adverse political winds.3 Before the vote, Republican Rep. Jeremy Shaffer, whose district covers the county's northernmost suburbs, told WESA that voters are responding to messages that avoid the polarizing rhetoric of national politics.12 His seat was not among those that changed hands.

The county's results came in a good year for the party statewide: Pennsylvania Democrats expanded their majority in the state House and took control of the state Senate11, according to the AP's race calls. On the same ballot, county voters showed a different mood toward local government. They voted down a measure that would have allowed County Council to expand its powers in the future.13
