Franklin County's margin nearly matches Sherrod Brown's whole Ohio lead

Columbus and its suburbs voted D+42 for Brown over Jon Husted. That was 14 points to the left of their 2024 presidential vote, on 15 percent fewer ballots so far. The move was smaller than Ohio's typical county but worth about 213,000 votes.

What Franklin County did

Franklin County, home to Columbus, had Ohio's largest vote. It gave Democrat Sherrod Brown 349,983 votes to 136,999 for Republican Jon Husted in the special election for U.S. Senate. That is a D+42.1 margin with 96.55 percent of the expected vote counted. In 2024 the county voted D+28.4 for Kamala Harris over Donald Trump, so this is a move of 14 points toward the Democrats.

Brown's lead in the county is about 213,000 votes, close to his whole statewide margin of about 223,000. He won Ohio 2,301,042 to 2,077,748, or D+4.9. Franklin cast 505,352 votes for senator, about 11 percent of the state's total and more than Cuyahoga County's 475,488.

By Ohio standards, though, the county's shift was modest. All 88 counties moved left. The typical county moved about 17 points, and the state as a whole moved 16.1, from R+11.2 for Trump to D+4.9 for Brown. Franklin ranked 72nd of 88. Ohio's other big urban counties also moved less than the typical county: Cuyahoga (Cleveland) moved 12 points, Hamilton (Cincinnati) 10 and Lucas (Toledo) 13. Summit (Akron) moved 16 points, Montgomery (Dayton) 15 and Butler, north of Cincinnati, 16. Outside Ohio, Franklin's shift matched that of Wayne County, Michigan, home to Detroit, which went from D+29.0 to D+42.9. Wake County, North Carolina, which started at a similar margin, moved 22 points.

Franklin is younger and more diverse than the rest of Ohio. The Census Bureau estimates 1,361,536 residents in 2025, 938,396 of them in Columbus1. 25.6 percent of county residents are Black2, and 13.7 percent are 65 or older, against 19.1 percent statewide1.

Who turned out, and why

Local election officials expected a big turnout. In October, the director of the county board of elections, Antone White, told NBC4 that he thought turnout would track closer to presidential elections3. 10TV reported that 93,166 voters cast early ballots at the county's early vote center on Morse Road4, and NBC4 said that number shattered early-voting records in Franklin County5.

Even so, the count so far is 85 percent of the county's 2024 total, about 92,000 fewer votes, and some ballots are still uncounted. Brown's lead is still about 43,000 votes larger than Harris's lead of roughly 170,000. County totals cannot show how much of that came from Trump voters switching sides and how much from Republicans staying home or from Democrats turning out at higher rates. This move mixes all of those.

Local reporters saw both forces at work. Analyzing the state result, NBC4 said Brown's comeback came from some combination of a larger advantage in Democratic Franklin County, smaller losses in Republican territory, and victories in closely divided counties6. In a House race that split the county, WOSU reported that Democrat Don Leonard's defeat of Republican Mike Carey was largely due to higher than usual turnout in the southern and western portions of Franklin County7.

On the issues, the Associated Press reported that Brown hammered Husted relentlessly8 over his support for artificial intelligence data centers, his ties to the FirstEnergy bribery scandal at the statehouse, and donations from the retail billionaire Les Wexner. CBS News said Brown cast Husted as "the face of data centers."9 The issue was also on local ballots. NBC4 reported that in each of central Ohio's five races, communities voted to reject data centers10. One of those was in Franklin County: Axios Columbus reported that Grove City's Issue 28 will require a referendum on any large-scale developments using large amounts of water or land11.

The exit polls cover the whole state, not individual counties. CBS News found that Brown led big against GOP Sen. Jon Husted with younger voters, those who rated the economy badly and independent voters12, and that those who opposed the construction of data centers in their community voted for Brown12. Brown also had far more money. NBC4 reported that his campaign reported $68.4 million in receipts through Oct. 14, compared with $19.9 million reported by Husted's campaign13.

What it means for Franklin County

The result puts Franklin County at the center of Ohio Democrats' statewide wins. Its margin alone covered almost all of Brown's lead. Democrat Amy Acton also won the governor's race, 10TV reported, becoming the first woman elected governor of Ohio and the first Democrat to win the office in 20 years14.

The win may not last long. NPR noted that Brown will have to run for reelection in 2028 to keep the seat15, because this election only filled the rest of JD Vance's term. Before then, final counts and a turnout breakdown by precinct will show more clearly whether Franklin's 14-point move came from new voters, from voters changing sides, or from Republicans who did not vote.
