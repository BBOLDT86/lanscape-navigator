Every Mississippi county moved left as Colom edges ahead of Hyde-Smith

Scott Colom leads Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by 2,574 votes, and the race has not been called. The biggest moves came in the Memphis suburbs, Hattiesburg and the Gulf Coast. The smallest came in Delta counties that were already heavily Democratic.

Two years after Donald Trump carried Mississippi by 22.9 points, Democrat Scott Colom leads Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by 467,930 votes to 465,356. That is a lead of 2,574 votes, or 0.3 points, with 96.92 percent of the expected vote counted. The Associated Press has not called the race. The state moved 23.2 points toward the Democrats, and it moved everywhere: all 82 counties shifted left, none shifted right, and 18 counties that Trump won now show Colom ahead.

Few people expected a close race. Mississippi Today reported that most forecasters had rated the Mississippi Senate race as safely Republican.1 Colom is a state district attorney covering Clay, Lowndes, Noxubee and Oktibbeha counties.2 If his lead holds, he would be the first Democrat to win a U.S. Senate race in Mississippi since John Stennis in 19823, and the first Black senator from Mississippi since the post-Civil War Reconstruction Era.4

Most of the ballots still out are affidavit ballots, Mississippi's name for provisional ballots. In a letter sent to the election commissioners in all 82 Mississippi counties, attorneys for Hyde-Smith5 asked officials to keep those ballots separate and preserve them. Colom's lawyer argued that it is against the law for election commissioners to separate any processed ballots6. The secretary of state's office confirmed to ABC News that the state has no formal recount process.7 Counties must send in certified results by Nov. 13, Secretary of State Michael Watson said5.

Prices, hospitals and a senator who skipped the debates

Colom ran on household costs and health care. He opened his campaign by criticizing Hyde-Smith's vote for Trump's 'big beautiful bill', a sprawling piece of legislation that included Medicaid cuts, tax breaks and new money for national defense and deportations.8 He warned that eight of Mississippi's rural hospitals will risk closing forever.9 He also used a remark Hyde-Smith made in a TV interview about beef prices, when she suggested that Mississippians upset over the high cost of beef remember that there are "so many proteins to choose from."10 His campaign turned that into a viral ad, Mississippi Today reported. In the final days, he asked Republicans directly for their votes, arguing that voters have been betrayed by a president who pledged to stay out of foreign wars, lower gasoline prices and tame the rising cost of goods.10

At Trump's Oct. 26 rally in Corinth, Hyde-Smith also labeled Colom as "the transgender defender"11. CNBC reported that Hyde-Smith refused to debate Colom several times.4 CNN pointed out that she has run behind Trump before: In 2020, she won her first full term by 10 points, while Trump won Mississippi by 16 points that year.12 Ty Pinkins, an independent, was also on the ballot. He said he stayed in the race after realizing he could not win because he believed it would boost Colom's candidacy.4

From the Memphis suburbs to the Coast

DeSoto County, south of Memphis, made the largest move in the state. It went from a 24.1-point Trump win to a 6.1-point Colom lead, a shift of 30 points. The Hill reported that a consultant blamed a facility in DeSoto that has drawn significant backlash from locals about persistent noise issues13. The county's Democratic chair gave more credit to organizers knocking on nearly 70,000 doors and working to increase absentee ballots.13 Forrest County, home to Hattiesburg, moved 28 points, from R+17.9 to D+10.3. Panola moved 27. On the Gulf Coast, Harrison County went from R+28.9 to R+3.7 and Jackson County from R+39.9 to R+14.7, a 25-point move in each.

Colom's home ground moved strongly too. Lowndes County went from R+8.2 to D+15.9, Clay moved 24 points, and Oktibbeha, home to Starkville, went from R+0.3 to D+22.4. Lafayette, home to Oxford, flipped from R+21.8 to D+1.2. Madison County, the suburbs north of Jackson, moved 18 points and flipped by under a point. Rankin County, the suburbs east of Jackson, stayed Republican at R+25.6.

The smallest moves came in counties that were already heavily Democratic. Sharkey did not move at all; it was D+36.8 in 2024 and is D+36.8 now. Claiborne moved 8 points, Jefferson 9 and Holmes 11. Hinds County, which includes Jackson, cast the most votes in the state (77,465, with 93 percent counted). It moved 15 points, to D+60.8. Even Itawamba, Trump's strongest county at R+80.0, moved 14 points.

Who moved: turnout, persuasion or both

These numbers describe counties, not voters. Size made some difference. The four counties that cast more than 50,000 votes moved 22.2 points (weighted by votes). The 22 mid-sized counties moved 21.4, and the 56 counties with fewer than 10,000 votes moved 18.5. The 2024 result mattered more. Counties Trump won by 10 to 30 points moved 23.9. The 37 counties he won by 30 or more, which cast 40 percent of the vote, still moved 20.4. The 13 counties Kamala Harris won by 30 or more moved 14.7, partly because they had less room to move.

Turnout fell everywhere. The 953,314 votes counted so far are 78 percent of the 2024 total. The places where turnout fell furthest tended to move the most toward Colom (correlation −0.30). The 49 counties where turnout came in at 65 to 80 percent of 2024 cast 65 percent of the vote and moved 22.2 points. The five counties where turnout held at 95 percent or more (Bolivar, Clay, Holmes, Humphreys and Noxubee) moved 17.3. DeSoto cast 69 percent of its 2024 vote.

That pattern could mean Republicans stayed home, or that some voters switched sides. County counts cannot tell the two apart, and reporting points to both. The Hill reported that preliminary returns show about 10 percent more votes were cast in majority-Black counties than in the 2023 governor's race. The New York Times estimated that 91 percent of Black voters back Colom.13 CNN's voter poll found that Black voters made up about 30% of voters in the race12. Brannon Miller, whose firm ran a super PAC effort to reach Republican voters, pointed to Colom's gains in red counties like Pearl River and DeSoto as a sign that some voters who typically back Republicans crossed over13. Pearl River moved 19 points, and it cast 67 percent of its 2024 vote. Which explanation matters more will depend on voter-file studies, and the outcome itself depends on the affidavit ballots still being counted.
