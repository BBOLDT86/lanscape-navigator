In Mississippi, DeSoto and the Coast moved most as Colom edged Hyde-Smith

All 82 Mississippi counties moved toward the Democrats. The biggest swings came in Republican suburbs and Gulf Coast counties where turnout fell. In the Black-majority Delta, turnout held but margins grew the least.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 11, 2026, from the count as of Nov. 11, 2026; the tables below refresh with the count (last Nov. 14, 5:52 AM ET, AP via The New York Times), and the piece is rewritten when a race is called or a margin moves more than a point. 14 sources. Scott Colom, the Democratic district attorney from Columbus, won Mississippi's U.S. Senate seat by 4,669 votes. He had 473,336 to 468,667 for Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith, a margin of 0.5 points with 98.73 percent of the expected vote counted. Donald Trump carried the state by 22.9 points two years ago, so the race moved 23.4 points. Hyde-Smith congratulated Colom in a video message Wednesday evening.1 The results are not official until the counties certify them, and the state's December 3 deadline for certifying the results2 is still weeks away.

Every one of the 82 counties moved toward the Democrats, and none moved the other way. In the typical county the swing was 19 points. Eighteen counties that Trump won in 2024 voted for Colom, among them DeSoto, Madison, Forrest, Lowndes, Lafayette and Oktibbeha. No county went the other way.

Where the ground shifted most

DeSoto County, the Memphis suburb, had the largest swing in the state. It went from Trump by 24 to Colom by 6, a 30-point move. Mississippi Today called DeSoto not the only reason for Colom's stunningly strong performance, but it is the biggest factor.3 Turnout there also fell sharply: 54,184 votes were cast, against 79,082 in 2024.

On the Gulf Coast, Harrison County (Gulfport and Biloxi) moved 25 points and Jackson County (Pascagoula) moved 26. Hyde-Smith still carried both, but her edge in Harrison shrank to under four points. Harrison is the county that, Mississippi Today wrote, had become a red firewall for the state GOP4 over the past decade. Further inland, Forrest County (Hattiesburg) went from Trump by 18 to Colom by 11, a 28-point move.

Colom's home region moved in step with these places, and more of its voters came back to the polls. He is a state district attorney covering Clay, Lowndes, Noxubee and Oktibbeha counties.5 Lowndes, which includes Columbus, went from Trump by 8 to Colom by 16. Oktibbeha (Starkville), which Trump won by less than a point, went for Colom by 22. In both counties the 2026 vote reached 87 percent of the 2024 total, and in Clay it reached 96 percent, against 78 percent statewide. The Commercial Dispatch's editorial page wrote that a big factor in the turnout was the appearance of Columbus' Scott Colom6 on the ballot.

Around Jackson, Hinds County widened its Democratic margin from 46 points to 61. Madison County flipped by about a point. Rankin County moved 22 points but stayed Republican by 25.

The campaign

Colom sharply criticized Hyde-Smith's vote for Trump's "big, beautiful bill," which cut Medicaid funds.1 He also attacked tariffs and the war with Iran, labeling his opponent "Cindy High Prices."1 Hyde-Smith campaigned as a reliable conservative and attempted to portray Colom as a "radical" progressive.1 The two had met before: in 2023, Hyde-Smith blocked Colom's nomination by former US president Joe Biden to serve as a federal district judge in northern Mississippi.7

CNN described a noticeably quiet eight years in the Senate, capped off by a campaign in which she was rarely seen8. Trump came to the state for her in the final week. NPR reported that Mississippi did not see much national money or attention until the closing days of the election.9 The Cook Political Report had the race rated as Likely Republican going into election night.9

Who moved: turnout or persuasion

Counties are not voters, and a county's move cannot tell people changing their minds apart from people staying home. With that caveat, the county numbers show a clear pattern. The 16 counties Trump won by 10 to 30 points cast 30 percent of the vote and moved 24.1 points, weighted by votes. The 13 counties Harris won by 30 or more moved 14.9. By size, the four biggest counties moved 22.4 points and the 55 counties with fewer than 10,000 votes moved 18.6.

Turnout fell most in the places that moved most. Statewide, 2026 votes came to 78 percent of the 2024 total. In 47 counties the vote fell to between 65 and 80 percent of 2024; together they cast 62 percent of the vote and moved 22.1 points. In five counties the vote held at 95 percent or more: Humphreys, Noxubee, Bolivar, Holmes and Clay. All but Clay are heavily Democratic, and together they moved 17.5. The correlation between a county's turnout change and its move is modest, at -0.28.

That pattern fits Republican voters staying home at higher rates than Democrats. It also reflects a limit on how far some margins can move: Claiborne County, already Harris's by 68 points, moved only 8. And the pattern has exceptions. George and Greene counties had the steepest turnout drops in the state but moved less than average, about 16 and 19 points. Sharkey County did not move at all.

Reporting on who voted points the same way as the turnout figures. The AP found strong turnout in the Mississippi Delta and its concentration of majority Black counties.10 Black voters made up about 30% of voters in the race, according to the CNN Voter Poll, with nearly all of them breaking for Colom.11 In those counties, high turnout added votes to Colom's statewide total without moving local margins much. CNN's analysis also credited Democrats with winning over a slice of White working-class voters with a populist economic message12. The county count cannot confirm that kind of crossover, and it cannot rule it out.

The last ballots counted were affidavit ballots, Mississippi's name for provisional ballots. In Hinds County, officials rejected about two-thirds of them, a share that is much higher than in other Mississippi counties.13 Earlier, Hyde-Smith had sent a letter on Thursday to all 82 Mississippi county election commissioners demanding that their offices preserve all affidavit, or provisional, ballots14. Colom's campaign called that request unlawful. Mississippi is the only US state without a legal provision for vote recounts in statewide elections.14
