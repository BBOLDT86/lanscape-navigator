Every Rhode Island county swings left; Reed wins sixth term by 37

Sen. Jack Reed beat a little-known Republican by 37.5 points, 23.6 points better than Kamala Harris did in 2024. The biggest moves came in Providence County and in Kent County, which was the state's closest county two years ago.

Jack Reed was never in much danger. He secured a sixth term after easily beating Republican Ray McKay and independent first-time candidate Michael Bahry1, and the AP called the race at 8:011, a minute after polls closed. The county map is interesting for a different reason: it shows how far the margin moved.

With 98% of the expected vote counted, Reed leads Raymond McKay 240,069 to 105,346, a margin of 37.5 points. Kamala Harris won Rhode Island by 13.9 points in 2024, so the state moved 23.6 points toward the Democrats. Across the country, the typical county moved about 14 points. All five Rhode Island counties moved left. None changed hands, because Harris had already won all five. Reed's lead of about 135,000 votes is nearly twice Harris's 2024 edge, even though roughly 152,000 fewer ballots were cast.

A safe seat and a one-sided campaign

Reed, a West Point graduate and Army veteran first elected to the Senate in 19962, is the senior Democrat on the Senate Armed Services Committee and served as its chairman between 2021 and 2025.2 McKay, 65, of Warwick, a former city administrator and network and telecommunications manager, lost the 2024 Republican primary to Patricia Morgan1. WPRI reported that Reed spent about $1 million on broadcast TV ads, while McKay aired none3.

The national mood also worked against the Republican. In the University of New Hampshire's election-eve poll, only 29% of Rhode Islanders approved of President Trump's job performance, while 69% disapproved3. In the same survey, 66% of Reed voters said their vote was meant to oppose Trump4. When voters were asked about issues, the Rhode Island Current reported, affordability and inflation came first, followed by economic health or financial security.5

Who moved: the big counties, and the places where turnout fell most

The charts beside this section cover only five counties, and counties are not voters. Still, a pattern shows. Larger counties moved more. The three counties that cast between 50,000 and 250,000 votes (Providence, Kent and Washington) make up 85% of the vote and moved 24.2 points left, weighted by votes. Bristol and Newport, the two smaller counties, moved 18.8. That gap is why the vote-weighted move (+23.4) is larger than the median county's (+20.6).

Providence County had the biggest move, from D+14.4 to D+40.5, or 26 points. It cast 184,130 votes, about half the state's total. Kent County, south of the capital, is the most telling case. Harris won it by just 1.9 points in 2024. Reed won it by 25.5, a 24-point move, in the county that includes McKay's hometown of Warwick. It is the only county in the "Harris by under 10" group. The four counties Harris won by 10 to 30 points moved by about the same amount when weighted (+23.4). The coastal counties moved least: Washington moved 19 points and Newport 18, though both still gave Reed margins above 33 points.

The county counts cannot tell voters who changed their minds apart from voters who simply stayed home. About 359,000 ballots were cast in the five counties, 70% of the 2024 total. All five fall in the 65-to-80% turnout band. Within that band, though, the order is suggestive. Providence, where turnout fell to about 68% of 2024's, moved most. Washington and Newport, at about 75% and 74%, moved least. A smaller midterm electorate that leaves out less regular voters could explain much of the move. Polling suggests some persuasion as well. In September, UNH found that 79% of Republicans and 52% of independents would vote for McKay6, so about one in five Republicans was not with their party's nominee. The data here cannot divide the move between those two explanations.

On turnout overall, the Boston Globe reported on election night that it was on track to be nearly on par with the 2022 midterms7. Early voting changed a lot: in-person early voting was more than double the turnout from two years ago8, the Rhode Island Current reported.

A governor's race the map does not show

The governor's race is the reason this map uses the Senate contest. Helena Foulkes, a former CVS executive, had already beaten the sitting governor, Dan McKee, in September. She won in all 39 cities and towns, with 62.5 percent of the vote to his 37.5 percent.7 In November she defeated independent Ken Block and Republican Aaron Guckian9. The AP count puts Foulkes at 207,216 votes and Guckian at 47,361. That works out to D+44.5, but it overstates the contest: about 105,000 more votes went to other candidates, most of them to Block, who finished second. Block ran as a Republican in 201410. Guckian faced public pressure to drop out, especially after his GOP primary rival Elaine Pelino endorsed Block.10 WPRI reported that Block won some of the western Rhode Island communities like Scituate and West Greenwich.11

Democrats also won further down the ballot. Ocean State Media reported that the party appeared to sweep Rhode Island's statewide elections12, and that voters kept the state's congressional delegation entirely blue.12 The Globe described a political tidal wave that started with the September primaries7.

Counting is not finished. Statewide, 98% of the expected vote is in. AP's estimates for the counties run from about 84% in Washington and Newport to about 94% in Providence and Kent, so the county figures may still move a little as the last ballots are added. Every race above has been called.
