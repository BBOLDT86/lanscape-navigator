The wave · state by state · Kansas

Every Kansas county moved left; the southwest moved furthest

Adam Hamilton beat Sen. Roger Marshall by 5.1 points in a state Donald Trump carried by 16.1, a 21-point move. Meatpacking counties hit by September immigration raids moved most; the governor's race is still uncalled.

Kansas is sending a Democrat to the U.S. Senate for the first time since 1932.1 Adam Hamilton, founding pastor of the United Methodist Church of the Resurrection in Leawood2, led Sen. Roger Marshall 561,143 votes to 505,558 with 98.9 percent of the expected vote counted. That is a margin of 5.1 points in a state Donald Trump carried by 16.1 two years ago. About 33,000 more ballots went to other candidates, including Libertarian candidate David Graham3.

The 21.2-point move toward the Democrats showed up in every one of Kansas's 105 counties. Not one county moved toward the Republicans. Six counties that voted for Trump in 2024 voted for Hamilton: Sedgwick (Wichita), Lyon (Emporia), Geary (Junction City) and three in the southwest, Finney, Ford and Seward. Three more came close: Leavenworth ended at R+0.9, Crawford at R+1.3 and Harvey at R+3.9.

The governor's race is closer still. Democrat Cindy Holscher leads Republican Ty Masterson, the state Senate president, 551,250 to 543,612, or 0.7 points, and the AP has not called it. Kansas law calls for county election officers to audit 10% of a county's precincts when the margin between candidates is within 1%. Masterson also could request a full recount if county commissions certify the results.4

Hamilton, a pastor and former Republican5, ran on an affordability-focused agenda6. He blamed tariffs and foreign wars for higher prices and hit Marshall over a New York Times report7 about Marshall's old medical practice. According to that report, the practice sued more than 700 patients during his decades-long medical career. In 81 cases, patients were arrested for missing court dates8. Marshall called the lawsuits a "common business practice."9 He told voters that supporting him meant supporting the president's agenda10, and Trump rallied twice in Kansas3 for him.

The southwest, six weeks after the raids

The biggest moves in Kansas were in the southwest, and they are among the biggest in the country. Seward County, home to Liberal, went from Trump by 39.0 to Hamilton by 13.5, a 52-point move. Ford County (Dodge City) moved 39 points and Finney County (Garden City) 37. Both went from Trump by more than 34 to narrow Hamilton wins.

In late September, Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents arrested 104 people in Dodge City, Garden City, Liberal and surrounding11 areas. The Kansas Reflector reported that around half or more of registered voters in Finney, Ford and Seward counties are Latino12. In CBS News's reporting, one farm family complained the raids left ranchers short-staffed during harvest season13.

Turnout held up better in these three counties than in the state overall. Seward cast 92 percent as many votes as in 2024, Ford 88 percent and Finney 86 percent, against 83 percent statewide. Still, Wichita State political scientist Neal Allen told KAKE that "a shift as big as we saw in Southwest Kansas has to be a combination of both mobilization and persuasion."14 Their mostly white rural neighbors moved too, even though turnout there fell more: Grant County moved 28 points, Greeley 27 and Stevens 25, each casting about three-quarters of its 2024 vote.

The southwest's net Democratic margin comes to under 900 votes. Washburn University's Bob Beatty told the Topeka Capital-Journal that "Hamilton still wins if he doesn't win those three counties."15

Wichita, the suburbs and the farm counties

Most of Hamilton's margin came from the Kansas City suburbs. Johnson County cast 297,051 votes, 27 percent of the state total, and went from Harris by 8.5 to Hamilton by 28.0. That works out to a net of about 83,000 votes, more than his statewide margin of about 55,600. Wyandotte County (Kansas City, Kan.) moved 25 points, to D+48.7.

Sedgwick County moved 22 points, from Trump by 13.8 to Hamilton by 7.7. Last month Trump visited Park City and encouraged voters to pretend he was on the ballot when voting for Republican candidates.16

In the countryside, many Kansas farmers, already squeezed by fuel costs driven up by the Iran war, have been hit by retaliatory tariffs on exports such as soybeans and wheat10. CBS News found the pressure has split farmers13, and some are voting for Marshall because they don't want Republicans to lose the Senate13. The smallest moves were in the north and west. Wallace County moved 9 points and is still R+76.0. Sheridan and Lincoln moved 10 points, and Washington and Chautauqua 11. Barton County, where Marshall's Great Bend practice was, moved 19 points, from R+56.8 to R+38.1. That is close to a typical Kansas county, and the count alone cannot show what the debt story did there.

Who moved: turnout or persuasion

The charts beside this section describe counties, not voters. What stands out is how even the move was. The 87 counties that cast fewer than 10,000 votes made up 22 percent of the vote and moved 19.9 points, weighted by votes. The 14 mid-sized counties moved 20.4, the three large ones (Sedgwick, Shawnee and Douglas) moved 20.0, and Johnson moved 19.5. The typical small county moved less (16.8) because the far northwest and north-central counties pulled the median down.

By 2024 vote, the 94 counties Trump won by 30 or more cast a third of the vote and moved 19.4. The six he won by 10 to 30 moved most, 21.5, and four of them flipped. Douglas County (Lawrence), the bluest in the state, moved least, 16.7.

Turnout fell everywhere: no county cast 95 percent of its 2024 vote, and none fell below 65 percent. If the result were simply Republicans staying home, the counties with the biggest drops would show the biggest moves. They don't. The 73 counties that cast 65 to 80 percent of their 2024 vote moved 19.2 points. The 32 that cast 80 to 95 percent, with 77 percent of the ballots, moved 20.2. The correlation between holding turnout and moving left is weak and positive (r = +0.27).

The county count cannot tell voters who changed their minds from voters who stayed home. Both readings have support. Republicans interviewed by KCTV said Republicans must figure out why their own voters did not show up17. The Kansas Reflector reported early indications show Latino and Black voters across the state boosted Democratic candidates' vote totals12, though official data is still pending. Turnout was not low for a midterm: state officials told KCUR they think the total is in the state's top three for voter turnout in a nonpresidential election year.18
