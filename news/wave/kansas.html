The wave · state by state · Kansas

Kansas moved left in all 105 counties, led by its meatpacking towns

Adam Hamilton beat Sen. Roger Marshall by five points in a state Donald Trump won by 16. Seward County, home to Liberal, moved 53 points six weeks after federal immigration raids.

Before this year, the last time Kansas elected a Democrat to the U.S. Senate was 1932. Hamilton's victory marks the first time in 94 years that Kansas voters have elected a Democrat to the U.S. Senate1. Adam Hamilton, the Methodist pastor who leads the Church of the Resurrection in the Kansas City area2, unseated Sen. Roger Marshall. In AP's count, with 98.7 percent of the expected vote in, Hamilton has 561,978 votes to Marshall's 506,588. That is a margin of 5.0 points in a state Donald Trump carried by 16.1 in 2024, a move of 21 points.

The move reached every county. All 105 shifted toward the Democrats, the typical one by 18 points, and none moved the other way. Six counties Trump carried two years ago went for Hamilton: Sedgwick (Wichita), Lyon (Emporia), Geary (Junction City), and Finney, Ford and Seward in the southwest. In the governor's race, Cindy Holscher beat Ty Masterson by 0.7 points, 552,076 to 544,791. Holscher, a state senator, promised to halt new data centers and overcame a flood of money that Republicans poured behind Masterson3.

Three packing towns lead the map

The largest moves in Kansas, and some of the largest in the country, came in the southwest's meatpacking belt. Seward County went from a 39.0-point Trump win to a 14.1-point Hamilton win, a 53-point move. Ford County (Dodge City) moved 39 points to D+1.1. Finney County (Garden City) moved 37 to D+2.5.

ICE agents arrived in the area Sept. 22, focusing immigration checks in Dodge City, Liberal and Garden City. The federal government reported making more than 100 arrests.4 The Kansas Reflector reported that the raids led to children not showing up to school, delayed start times at meatpacking plants and instigated fear across the communities5. It also reported that Latino voters make up 49% of registered voters in Ford County, 48% in Finney County and 60% in Seward County5, while registered Democrats make up only 23 to 24 percent in each. In Liberal, some residents believe immigration enforcement shaped voters' choices6, KWCH reported. The town's vice mayor, Janeth Vazquez, said Democrats would have to follow through on their promises to retain the community's trust in future elections6.

The move also reached nearby counties. Grant (+28), Greeley (+27) and Stevens (+25) were next on the list, although each stayed Republican by 35 points or more. Neal Allen, a Wichita State political scientist, said "southwest Kansas is now voting more like Emporia or Pittsburg and less like rural western Kansas"7. Emporia's Lyon County flipped, and Pittsburg's Crawford County came within 1.3 points of flipping.

Debts, tariffs and the suburbs

Marshall went into the fall facing questions about his medical practice. The New York Times reported in September that Marshall filed the suits against more than 700 patients8 over unpaid bills. The Kansas City Star found that his 18 percent interest rate was by far the highest of the roughly 150 medical practices whose public records were reviewed9. Marshall called the lawsuits a "common business practice."10 Two former Republican governors, Bill Graves and Mike Hayden, endorsed Hamilton11, and NPR reported that the contest became the most expensive in Kansas history12.

Farm country gave Hamilton another opening. Many Kansas farmers, already squeezed by fuel costs driven up by the Iran war, have been hit by retaliatory tariffs on exports such as soybeans and wheat. Cattle ranchers were angered by a President Trump plan to ease tariffs on beef imports.13 The Kansas Livestock Association's political action committee declined to endorse Marshall this year.14

Most of Hamilton's votes still came from the metro areas. Johnson County cast 297,051 votes, 27 percent of the state's total, and went from Harris by 8.5 to Hamilton by 28.0. Sedgwick moved 21 points, from R+13.8 to D+7.7. A political analyst told KMBC that "Sedgwick County goes for Hamilton even more than Laura Kelly four years ago"15. Wyandotte (Kansas City, Kan.) moved 25 points to D+48.7. Leavenworth (R+0.9), Crawford (R+1.3) and Harvey (R+3.7) were close to flipping.

The smallest moves came in some of the reddest, least-populated counties. Wallace County, on the Colorado line, moved 9 points, from R+84.8 to R+76.0. Sheridan and Lincoln moved 10, and Washington, Elk, Chautauqua, Smith and Jewell about 11.

Who moved: big and small, by about the same amount

Counties are not voters, but the county pattern is unusually even. Weighted by votes, the 87 counties that cast fewer than 10,000 ballots, 22 percent of the vote, moved 19.9 points. The 14 mid-sized counties moved 20.4; Sedgwick, Shawnee and Douglas together moved 20.0; and Johnson moved 19.5. The typical small county moved less (16.8), which suggests the larger packing counties pulled up that group's weighted figure.

By 2024 result, the six counties Trump won by 10 to 30 points moved most: 21.5 weighted and 22.3 for the typical one. Three of the six (Sedgwick, Lyon and Geary) flipped. The 94 counties Trump won by 30 or more, a third of the vote, moved 19.4. Johnson, Shawnee and Riley, which Harris won narrowly, moved 19.3. Wyandotte moved 24.9, and Douglas, the bluest county, moved 16.7.

Turnout fell everywhere. Kansas cast 1,101,877 Senate votes, 83 percent of its 2024 total. The 34 counties that kept 80 to 95 percent of their 2024 vote, which hold 77 percent of the total, moved 20.2 points. The 71 counties that fell to 65 to 80 percent moved 19.3. The link between holding turnout and moving left is weak (r = +0.29). Seward stands out: it cast 94 percent of its 2024 vote, a higher share than any other county, and moved the most. PBS NewsHour reported that turnout also surged 27% over 2022 in the three counties16.

Elsewhere, the county count cannot show whether Trump voters switched sides or stayed home. Two Republicans who spoke to KCTV said Republicans must figure out why their own voters did not show up17. A move this even, both where turnout fell steeply and where it held, could also mean some voters crossed over, but the county figures cannot settle the question.
