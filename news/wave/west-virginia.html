Capito wins a third term as all 55 West Virginia counties move left

Sen. Shelley Moore Capito won by about 20 points, half of Donald Trump's 2024 margin. Her Democratic challenger carried Charleston, Morgantown and Jefferson County, and the biggest shift came in Berkeley County in the Eastern Panhandle.

By Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, with web search) for The Watch, Nov. 7, 2026, from the count as of Nov. 7, 2026; the tables below refresh with the count (last Nov. 10, 3:44 PM ET, AP via The New York Times), and the piece is rewritten when a race is called or a margin moves more than a point. 13 sources. Shelley Moore Capito will serve a third term in the U.S. Senate. The Associated Press declared Capito the winner at 8:52 p.m. EST.1 As of Saturday morning, with 99 percent of the expected vote counted, the Republican had 266,067 votes and Democrat Rachel Fetty Anderson had 172,922. That is a margin of 20.4 points. Two years ago Donald Trump carried West Virginia over Kamala Harris by 41.9 points.

The 21.5-point move toward the Democrats reached every one of the state's 55 counties. The typical county moved 20 points, compared with 14 for the typical county nationally. Three counties that Trump carried in 2024 voted for Fetty Anderson: Kanawha, Monongalia and Jefferson. The count is unofficial.

Where the map moved

Kanawha County, which includes Charleston, cast the most votes in the state: 47,778. It went from a 17.4-point Trump win to a 0.6-point edge for Fetty Anderson. It is also Capito's home base. Her public service in West Virginia began earlier as a member of the House of Delegates representing Kanawha County starting in 1996.2 Monongalia County, around Morgantown, is where Fetty Anderson served as a Morgantown City Councilor and Deputy Mayor3. It went from Trump by 4.4 to Fetty Anderson by 17.4, a 22-point move. Jefferson County, on the Maryland and Virginia line, went from Trump by 15.8 to Fetty Anderson by 4.9.

Berkeley County, next to Jefferson, had the biggest move in the state: 28 points. Trump won it by 35.8. Capito won it by 8.1. Marion County, around Fairmont, moved 23 points to the same 8.1-point Republican margin. Cabell County, which includes Huntington, ended at R+2.6.

The southern coalfield counties moved more than the state as a whole, although they remain heavily Republican. Lincoln and Wayne each moved 25 points and Mingo moved 24, but Capito still won Mingo by 50.0. The smallest moves were in the Northern Panhandle and the Potomac Highlands. Ohio County, home to Wheeling, moved 12 points. Neighboring Brooke and Hancock moved 15 each. Pendleton moved 12, and Grant moved 14. Grant is still the state's reddest county at R+65.0. The count does not say why these places moved less.

Who moved: big counties and small, red and less red

The charts beside this section show a shift that was almost the same everywhere. The 43 counties that cast fewer than 10,000 votes moved 19.8 points, weighted by votes. The 12 larger counties moved 20.5. The 50 counties Trump won by 30 points or more cast 71 percent of the vote, and together they moved 20.7 points. That is slightly more than the four counties he won by 10 to 30, which moved 18.1. The deepest-red parts of West Virginia did not hold back.

Turnout fell sharply. The 456,128 votes cast are 60 percent of the 762,390 cast in 2024. No county reached even 80 percent of its 2024 vote. Wyoming and McDowell counties cast fewer than half as many votes as they did two years ago. Wirt and Jefferson came closest to their 2024 totals, at a little over 70 percent. But how much a county's turnout fell barely tracks how far it moved. The 49 counties below 65 percent of their 2024 vote moved 20.3 points. The six above it moved 19.4. The correlation between the two is a weak -0.12.

Counties are not voters, and a county-level count cannot tell people changing their minds apart from people staying home. When four in 10 of the 2024 voters did not vote, a shift of this size could come from Trump voters skipping the race as easily as from people switching sides. Some of it also went to neither major party: about 17,100 votes went to other candidates, roughly 3.8 percent. Most of those went to Constitution Party nominee S. Marshall Wilson4. The evenness of the move points to a statewide mood more than to a turnout gap between particular places. The data cannot settle the question.

What the campaign and local reporting point to

The race was not close in money. Capito's campaign reported $7,314,563 in cumulative receipts through Oct. 14, compared with $164,558 for Fetty Anderson's campaign5, according to a Legis1 summary of campaign finance reports. Capito was endorsed by President Donald Trump1. Fetty Anderson had pulled an upset in the May primary, defeating former state Senate President Jeff Kessler, who entered the race as the best-known candidate6, The Mountaineer Journal reported.

In an interview with WVVA, Fetty Anderson criticized the Republican tax law that Capito backed. She said people who lost Medicaid or Affordable Care Act subsidies "lost their health insurance completely."7 In the same interview, Capito said addressing the situation in Iran and increasing domestic energy production could lower prices at the pump.7

West Virginia Public Broadcasting reported that the cost of living is a key issue motivating voters this year.8 Robert Bolton, a political science professor at Fairmont State University, told MetroNews that the sweep in the House and Senate is due to the increase in gas prices due to the conflict in Iran.9 Data centers were a state-level issue. MultiState reported that Democratic legislative candidates said voters were most interested in their positions on data centers.10 In Mason County, which moved 23 points, WOWK described a House district sandwiched between two planned high-impact data centers11.

The shift reached further down the ballot. WCHS reported that in the House of Delegates, Democrats held nine seats going into the night and ended with 21.12 State Republican chairman Josh Holstein said "We knew going into this election that the national environment was going to be difficult"12. West Virginia Public Broadcasting also reported that a smaller share of registered voters took part than in the 2022 midterm, despite more West Virginians being registered to vote13.
