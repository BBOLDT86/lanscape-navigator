The wave · state by state · Colorado

Every Colorado county moved left; El Paso and Douglas went Democratic

John Hickenlooper won a second Senate term by 25 points, more than twice Kamala Harris's 2024 margin. The biggest moves came in Adams County, Weld County and the San Luis Valley. The smallest came in tiny, deeply Republican counties.

No Colorado county moved toward the Republicans this year. Democratic Sen. John Hickenlooper beat Republican state Sen. Mark Baisley 1,600,935 votes to 930,787, with about 97 percent of the expected vote counted. That works out to a 25.3-point margin. Kamala Harris carried the state by 11.0 points two years ago, so the move toward the Democrats was 14.3 points.

Eight counties that Donald Trump carried in 2024 voted for Hickenlooper: El Paso, Douglas, Pueblo, Grand, Archuleta, Las Animas, Alamosa and Huerfano. None went the other way. In the governor's race, Phil Weiser beat Victor Marx by 27.7 points, a 16.7-point move from 2024. According to Colorado Politics, it was the sixth election in a row in which Democrats swept every statewide race1.

An uneven contest at the top

The Senate race was lopsided from the start. The Denver Gazette reported that Hickenlooper raised about $11 million through mid-October to the Republican's $138,0002. At their debate in Fort Collins, The Colorado Sun reported, Hickenlooper presented himself as the candidate who could push back against President Donald Trump3 if Democrats won Congress. Baisley, meanwhile, downplayed Trump's importance, noting the president has two years left while the senator serves six3. Colorado Public Radio reported that the two clashed over the war with Iran. Baisley said Trump was standing up to a bully nation4.

The Republican nominee for governor ran a quieter campaign. Colorado Newsline reported that Marx failed to pick up endorsements from many prominent Colorado Republicans, some of whom backed Weiser instead5, and declined to debate Weiser6. CNN described the national results as a statement of frustration with the state of the country and the presidency of Donald Trump7.

Where the map moved

The largest move came in Adams County, in Denver's northern suburbs. It went from a 9-point Harris win to a 32-point Hickenlooper win, a 23-point swing, on 195,886 votes. Adams anchors the 8th Congressional District. That district has the highest share of Latino voters of any of the state's congressional districts, at nearly 40%8, KUNC reported. There, Democrat Manny Rutinel unseated Republican Rep. Gabe Evans by a 15-point margin8. Next door, Weld County stayed Republican, but its margin shrank from 21 points to 3.

El Paso County cast the most votes in the state, 313,166. It went from a 10-point Trump win to a 4-point Hickenlooper win. According to Colorado Politics, it appeared to be the first time top-ticket Democratic nominees have carried the county1. The Gazette reported that Democrat Jessica Killin won the Colorado Springs congressional seat. She is the first Democrat to win the district in its 54-year history.9 Douglas County, south of Denver, moved from a 7-point Trump win to a 4-point Democratic win. The Colorado Sun reported that Democrat Kevin Leung unseated Republican state Sen. John Carson10 in Highlands Ranch, even though Republican groups spent about $4.3 million to help Carson10.

Pueblo County moved 16 points, from a 5-point Trump win to an 11-point Democratic win. The Pueblo Chieftain had noted that the county, long a Democratic stronghold, went red for Donald Trump in 2016 and 202411. The San Luis Valley moved sharply too. Costilla moved 21 points, Alamosa 20, Saguache 18 and Rio Grande 15. Alamosa went from an 11-point Trump win to a 9-point Democratic one.

The smallest moves came in thinly populated, deeply Republican counties. Kiowa County, with 700 votes, moved 2 points and still went Republican by 72. Custer, Rio Blanco and Dolores each moved about 5. Boulder County moved 9 points, but it had counted only 92 percent of its expected vote.

Who moved

The page's charts sort the counties several ways. Counties are not voters, so these patterns describe places, not people. The county with the most voters moving does not prove that individual voters changed their minds.

By size, the big counties moved more. The 39 counties that cast fewer than 10,000 votes moved 12 points, weighted by votes. Together they cast 6 percent of the vote. The seven counties with 50,000 to 250,000 votes moved 15 points, and the four largest moved 14. Colorado's typical county moved 11.5 points, below the national median of 14.1. Weighted by votes, the state moved 14.1, the same as the nation. The populous Front Range counties made up the difference.

By the 2024 result, the movement was in the middle. The 24 counties Trump won by 30 points or more moved 11 points, weighted. The three counties Harris won by less than 10 points (Adams, Garfield and Saguache) moved 22. Counties Trump won by 10 to 30 points moved 16, and the six bluest counties moved 12.

Turnout explains little at the county level. Colorado's counties cast 2,651,294 votes, 83 percent of the 2024 total. That share will rise as the last ballots are counted. Every county cast fewer votes than two years ago. But the correlation between a county's turnout drop and its move is +0.08, close to none. The 21 counties at 65 to 80 percent of their 2024 vote moved 15 points, weighted. The 42 counties at 80 to 95 percent moved 14.

Turnout among registered voters may still matter. Before Election Day, the secretary of state reported that turnout was highest among Democrats, with 49.4% of registered Democrats having returned their ballots12. But swings of 15 to 20 points in Republican places like Weld and Morgan counties suggest more than a lopsided turnout. The county totals alone cannot say how much of the move came from who voted and how much from voters changing sides.
