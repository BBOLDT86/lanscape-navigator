The wave · the biggest counties · Williamson County, Texas

Talarico's home county north of Austin swings 23 points toward Democrats

Donald Trump carried Williamson County by 2.5 points in 2024. Two years later, James Talarico, who started his political career in Round Rock, won it by 20 points, and Democrats took control of the county commissioners court.

Williamson County runs north from the Austin city line through Round Rock, Cedar Park, Leander and Georgetown and out to Taylor and Hutto. Senator-elect James Talarico, who is from Round Rock, began his political career representing Williamson County in the state Legislature after being elected to Texas House District 52 in 2018.1 On Nov. 3 the county voted for him over Ken Paxton, the Republican state attorney general, by 164,626 votes to 108,074, with 99% of the expected vote counted.

It is a fast-growing suburban county. County government puts its population at 752,827, according to 2025 Census figures.2 KXAN reported that Williamson County added almost 24,000 residents3 between mid-2024 and mid-2025, and that migration accounted for 81%3 of that growth.

What Williamson County did

The county went from a 2.5-point Trump win over Kamala Harris to a 20.3-point Talarico win, a move of about 23 points toward Democrats. That was a little smaller than the move across the state. Talarico won Texas by 12.1 points in a state Trump had carried by 13.7, a move of about 26 points. Williamson ranks 73rd of Texas's 254 counties by size of move. Every county in the state moved left. The typical Texas county moved about 18 points, and the average weighted by votes was 24.

Among the state's biggest counties, Collin, north of Dallas, moved 23 points, from Trump +11.2 to Talarico +12.1. Tarrant moved 24 and Harris 27. Travis, next door, started out far more Democratic and moved 19. Williamson moved more than the big counties elsewhere that Trump won narrowly in 2024: Maricopa in Arizona moved 20 points, San Bernardino 19, Riverside 17, and Hillsborough and Duval in Florida about 12 each. Across the country, the typical county moved 14.

Fewer people voted than two years ago. There were 278,482 votes in the Senate race, 90% of the 308,163 cast for president in 2024, or about 29,700 fewer. Trump's 2024 margin here was about 7,700 votes. Talarico's was 56,552. A county total can't show how much of that change came from Trump voters staying home, how much from Democrats turning out in larger numbers and how much from people who switched parties. It mixes all three.

Why it moved

The reasons given so far come from statewide polling and from local party figures. No poll has measured Williamson County voters on their own. In the AP Voter Poll, about two-thirds of voters said Paxton did not have the right personal character to serve as a U.S. senator4. The AP also found that about 2 in 10 Texas voters said inflation had imposed "severe" hardship on them and their family — and about three-quarters of this group backed5 Talarico. The Texas Tribune reported that Paxton was weighed down by his legal and ethical baggage, a sluggish economy marked by high gas and grocery prices6. The Tribune also wrote that Talarico needed a surge in Democratic enthusiasm, enough independents and swing voters, and enough Republicans — from demoralized base voters to frustrated Cornyn supporters — to either stay home, leave the Senate race blank or vote third-party6.

In Williamson, both parties pointed to turnout and organizing. Heather Jefts, the Democrat who won the race for county judge, told KUT that her party's work to turn out the base voters and the solid Democrats allowed us the freedom to use our money to go after all of those other voters1. She also cited crossover votes and strong turnout among Latino and South Asian voters. Michelle Evans, the county Republican chair, told KUT that despite outspending Democrats, Republicans were just not as energized or organized as they were this election cycle.1 Those are the parties' own accounts, not measurements. The Austin American-Statesman's precinct map found Talarico's support weakened toward the edges of Austin, particularly in precincts north of Travis County near Leander, Georgetown and Hutto7, and Paxton still led in some of those precincts.

What changes in Georgetown

The shift reached county government. Democrats won all four countywide contested races1, and KUT reported that Jefts will be working with a Democratic majority court.1 She defeated Steve Snell, who was appointed to the position in March 2025 after former County Judge Bill Gravell resigned to take a job with the Trump administration.8 The Statesman reported that she is the first woman to hold the office and the first Democrat to be elected county judge since 1990.9 KUT gave a slightly different count, saying it was the first time in 40 years that a Democrat would hold the post. Democrat Erin Block will be county clerk, replacing Nancy Rister, a Republican who has held the position since 1998.8

Democrat Chris Jimenez also won House District 52, Talarico's old seat. In 2021, KUT noted, state lawmakers redrew the district to favor Republicans, largely by lopping off two-thirds of Round Rock and adding rural areas and smaller communities.10

The new majority will take on local disputes. Jefts has said her priorities include establishing a medical examiner's office for the county, protecting water resources through aquifer protection, addressing traffic congestion and delivering property tax relief11. She has opposed the site the current court bought for a new jail and justice center in southeast Georgetown. Evans told KUT she has serious concerns about such a seismic shift in county leadership.1 These results came from a midterm electorate smaller than 2024's. The count can't say how the same county would vote in a bigger presidential-year turnout.
