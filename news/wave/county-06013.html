The wave · the biggest counties · Contra Costa County, California

In Contra Costa, Becerra widens Democrats' edge, but less than California does

Xavier Becerra carried the East Bay county by 42.5 points with four-fifths of the expected vote counted. That is about 5 points better than Kamala Harris did in 2024, and less than half of California's statewide shift.

Contra Costa County stretches inland from the refinery towns on San Pablo Bay, through the suburbs along Interstate 680, to the newer subdivisions around Antioch and Brentwood. About 1.17 million people live there, according to Census Bureau estimates for July 20251. No racial or ethnic group was a majority in 2020. White and Hispanic residents were the largest groups, followed by Asian and Black residents.2

What the county did

Democrat Xavier Becerra had 258,015 votes to Republican Steve Hilton's 104,033, a margin of 42.5 points, with 80% of the expected vote counted. Kamala Harris carried the county by 38.0 points in 2024, so the move is about 5 points toward Democrats.

That is a smaller move than California's. Statewide, Becerra leads by 30.4 points, against Harris's 20.1, a shift of 10.3. Contra Costa ranks 34th of 58 counties by move; 54 of the 58 moved left, and the typical county moved about 6 points. Next door, Alameda moved 8 points and Santa Clara barely 1. The biggest swings were inland in Southern California: Riverside moved 17 points and San Bernardino 19, both going from narrow Trump wins to double-digit Becerra leads. Los Angeles moved 12.

Big counties elsewhere that started near Contra Costa's 2024 margin mostly moved much further: Milwaukee 13 points, Fairfax 17, Travis 19. Only Middlesex, Massachusetts, at 5 points, and Santa Clara moved as little. Nationally, the typical county moved 14 points.

Who voted, and why

The ballots counted so far equal 68% of the county's 2024 total. If AP's estimate of the expected vote holds, the final count would be roughly 450,000, about 85% of 2024. So this electorate is not the same as the presidential one. A county's move mixes who turned out with who changed sides, and the count alone cannot separate them.

Before Election Day, the county's clerk-recorder-registrar, Kristin Connelly, told Richmond Confidential she was seeing "a high degree of interest in this election."3 She also told ABC7 that fewer voters were returning ballots early by mail and more were waiting to vote in person, which could slow the count.4 The county's own summary report, which matches AP's tally, shows Hilton doing noticeably better among the smaller group of in-person voters than among mail voters. Ballots that arrived after Election Day and provisional ballots are still being processed, so the margin may change.

The campaign gave voters a clear choice. The Associated Press reported that Becerra repeatedly tied Hilton to the president and promised to defend California against Trump's policies5. Hilton argued one-party rule was to blame for the state's high cost of living and homelessness crisis.5 Becerra ran a cautious race: Axios reported that he debated Hilton once and did a few media interviews and very little campaigning.6 CalMatters wrote that inflation, much of it attributable to Trump's war in Iran, tariffs and immigration crackdown,7 had raised costs further.

No poll has been published for Contra Costa alone, so it is not known which groups in the county moved. Statewide, most voters who said the economy was the top issue supported Becerra, according to The AP Voter Poll.8 Mike Madrid, a Republican consultant, told Axios that Becerra's win is largely because of anti-Republican, anti-Trump feelings in California6.

What it means locally

Further down the ballot, Richmond chose a new mayor. Richmondside reported that Claudia Jiménez won the Richmond Progressive Alliance's endorsement over reelection hopeful Mayor Eduardo Martinez in April9. Her apparent win continues the progressives' run in city elections and makes her the first woman, immigrant mayor in the city's history.9

Contra Costa remains solidly Democratic at the top of the ticket, but its modest move puts it with a few Bay Area neighbors rather than with the state as a whole. Its starting point does not explain the difference: Milwaukee, Fairfax and Travis began from similar Democratic margins and moved three to four times as far. Whether Contra Costa's smaller move reflects who voted, who switched or the ballots not yet counted will be clearer when the count is finished and certified.
