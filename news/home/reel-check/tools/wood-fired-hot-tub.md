**The short answer: a fire built directly under a stock tank on a ring of cinder blocks will heat the water, but it also turns the floor you sit on into a griddle and holds up three tons on a sheet of thin steel.** Move the fire to the side, put the tank on a full pad and add a thermometer, and it becomes one of the better backyard builds going.

The wood-fired tub is a reel staple because it looks like a reward: a cold night, a crackling fire, a steaming tank and a person sinking in up to the chin. AI video tools have made the clips cheaper still, and many now show builds no one ever lit. So we took the typical version at its word and worked out what the fire, the water and the steel would actually do.

## What the video shows

The clip usually opens on a galvanized round stock tank, about 8 feet across and 2 feet tall. Cinder blocks are stacked two courses high in a ring, with a gap left on one side as a fire door. The tank is lifted onto the ring, filled from a garden hose and a fire is lit underneath. A second version skips the block ring and runs a coil of copper pipe through a fire pit beside the tank, with two hoses into the water. At dusk the builder climbs in and the steam rolls.

What never appears: a thermometer, a pad under the floor of the tank, a flue, a fire extinguisher, a lid, or any water treatment. Generated versions add their own tells. Flames lick the steel floor while the water stays perfectly glassy. The block ring changes from one course to two between cuts. The bather sits right above the fire with no board in between. We can't prove how a given clip was made, but most of these read as made to be watched, not copied.

For the numbers below we assume an 8-foot tank filled 20 inches deep, which is close to what the clips show.

## The physics: 272,000 BTU and a hot seat

An 8-foot round tank filled to 20 inches holds about 627 gallons. That is roughly 5,200 pounds of water, or 2.6 tons, before anyone gets in. A common tank of this size is rated at 706 gallons brim-full, has a 20-gauge bottom and weighs about 153 pounds empty [8]. Add four adults and the load is just over 6,000 pounds.

Heating that water from 50°F tap water to a 102°F soak takes about 272,000 BTU. Dry firewood carries roughly 6,900 to 7,000 BTU per pound across most species [5]. So the heat itself is in about 40 pounds of wood. A homemade fire box loses half or more of its heat up and out the sides, which puts the real figure near 80 pounds, a big wheelbarrow load. That part of the reel is plausible.

How fast is the problem. A purpose-built heater with a water jacket, such as the Japanese Chofu stove, brought a 250-gallon tank close to 104°F in about four hours [3]. That works out to roughly 25,000 to 30,000 BTU an hour reaching the water. At a similar rate, 627 gallons needs most of a day. **The 8-foot tank in the reel is about twice too big to heat in an evening.** A 6-foot tank, 18 inches deep, is the size that matches what real heaters can do.

The bigger issue is where the heat goes in. Steel that is wet on the inside stays near the temperature of the water touching it. But the water touching a floor directly over a fire is the hottest water in the tub, and close to boiling right at the steel. A person sitting on that spot is sitting on the burner.

## Six ways it fails

1. **The floor is not built to bridge a hole.** Stock tank floors are thin sheet crimped to the wall and meant to rest on level ground. On a ring of blocks, the middle spans an open fire box under about 100 pounds per square foot of water, plus a person's weight on one spot. It sags, the seam at the wall strains, and it starts to leak.
2. **Burns from the floor and the rim.** Anyone sitting or standing over the fire is in contact with steel near 200°F. Japanese iron baths heated from below solve this with a wooden board floating on the water. The reels never show one. The dry rim of the tank, above the water line, can get far hotter.
3. **No thermostat on a wood fire.** The Consumer Product Safety Commission says hot tub water should never go above 104°F. Water at 106°F can drive the body toward heat stroke, and hot water causes drowsiness that can end in drowning [1]. The agency counted alcohol-related drownings in tubs near 110°F and warned that even electric thermostats can be off by four degrees. A fire stoked by someone already in the water, and used to the heat, climbs past that line easily.
4. **The zinc and the blocks do not like flame.** Galvanizing is rated for steady service up to about 390°F; above that, the outer zinc layer can peel [2]. Flame on the dry parts of the tank is far hotter. Ordinary concrete blocks hold water, and above 212°F that moisture turns to steam and can pop flakes off the surface; a firebrick liner is the usual fix [7]. Blocks rained on and then fired can shed fragments.
5. **A copper coil can flash to steam.** The coil version works only if water fills the coil and hoses before the fire starts and keeps moving. One stock-tank guide calls that step critical [9]. If a hose lifts out of the water, or kinks, the coil boils dry and can blow scalding steam out of the hose end.
6. **Dirty warm water.** Warm, still water grows bacteria. The CDC links hot tub rash to *Pseudomonas* and calls for at least 3 parts per million of chlorine or 4 to 8 of bromine, with pH between 7.0 and 7.8 [6]. A tank refilled from a hose and left uncovered for a week fails that test fast.

Smoke and carbon monoxide are minor outdoors but real under a roof. Never put a wood-fired tub inside a gazebo or screened porch without a proper flue.

## The verdict

As filmed, this is a cooking pot with a person in it: two out of ten. The heat sums work, but the floor, the seat and the temperature control do not. Moved to a full pad with a side-mounted heater, a thermometer and a lid, it becomes a seven: a real hot tub for the price of a used appliance, with no electric bill. Owners of such setups describe going from near-freezing water to a soak with an armful of wood [4].

## How to build a version that works

**Move the fire beside the tank, not under it.** A jacketed heater draws cool water from the bottom of the tub, warms it and sends it back in near the top. The hot water rises on its own, so no pump is needed [3]. The flame never touches the tub, and no bather sits over it.

1. **Check the rules.** Some towns ban open burning on bad air days or need a fire permit. Pick level ground at least 10 feet from buildings, fences and low branches.
2. **Build a full pad.** Four inches of compacted crushed stone topped with concrete pavers, a foot wider than the tank all round. The floor of the tank must touch the pad everywhere, with no gaps.
3. **Pick the right size.** A 6-foot tank filled 18 inches deep holds about 317 gallons and heats in four to five hours. Bigger tanks take most of a day.
4. **Set the heater on firebrick.** Use a wood-fired tub heater with a water jacket, connected through stainless bulkhead fittings. Run the flue well above head height. Fill the jacket with water before every fire.
5. **Test without people.** On the first firing, log the water temperature every hour and confirm it circulates.
6. **Control the heat.** Use a floating thermometer and stir the water before getting in. Stop feeding the fire at 100°F, and get out or add cold water if it passes 104°F. No alcohol in the tub, and adults only near the fire.
7. **Add a seat and a lid.** A wooden floor grate keeps feet off the steel near the heater inlet. A foam lid cuts heating time and keeps the water clean.
8. **Treat the water.** Bromine tablets in a floater, tested with strips before each soak, and drain and refill every few weeks.
