**The short answer: the pool in the reel would not hold water for long, and when it let go it would let go all at once.** The idea underneath it is not crazy, though. Changed in three ways, a version of it can be built.

## What the video shows

The build runs in four beats on an open lawn in a fenced backyard. Thousands of identical, label-free water bottles are stacked into a rectangle roughly 12 by 24 feet and about 3 feet high. The stacks are boxed between panels of welded-wire fencing, one on each face. Cement mortar is troweled onto the outer mesh in patches until the faces are covered. The finished box is shown as a pool.

What never appears is just as telling: no excavation, no footing, no floor, no liner, no mixer and no bags of cement. Several frames also carry the usual marks of generated video. The builder's head and arms smear while the fence behind him stays crisp, bottles shift between loose stacks and caged rows from one cut to the next, and in a close-up the plaster seems to sit on mesh with nothing solid behind it. We can't prove how a given clip was made, but this one reads as made to be watched, not followed.

For the numbers below we assume the build is real and filled to 30 inches, leaving 6 inches of freeboard.

## The physics: 22 tons pushing outward

A 12 by 24 foot box filled 30 inches deep holds about 5,400 gallons, roughly 22 tons of water. Water pushes sideways on every wall, harder the deeper it goes, so the load piles up at the bottom edge. The outward push on one foot of wall is half the water's weight per cubic foot (62.4 pounds) times the depth squared: about 195 pounds on every foot of wall, all the time. Along one 24-foot wall that is roughly 4,700 pounds, about the weight of a pickup truck, pushing sideways. At the base of each foot of wall, the water also works to tip it over with a moment of about 160 pound-feet.

Now the wall. Two skins of mortar three-quarters of an inch thick, plus empty bottles that weigh almost nothing, come to about 55 pounds per foot of wall. Resting on grass, that weight can muster perhaps 28 pounds of friction against sliding and about 18 pound-feet against tipping. The water asks for 195 and 160. **The wall is short by a factor of seven to nine before anything cracks.**

Oddly, the wire is not the weak link. If the mesh is ordinary 2 by 4 inch, 14-gauge fence and the two skins worked together, they could carry several times the bending load on paper. The failure is at the bottom: nothing holds the wall to the ground.

## Five ways it fails

1. **No footing, no floor tie.** Real above-ground pools hold their walls with a round shape, buttresses, or straps that run under the floor to the opposite wall. This one sits loose on turf, so the long walls slide and roll outward as it fills. Concrete pool walls are typically 6 to 8 inches of reinforced concrete on a footing [5].
2. **The bottles are not structure.** Empty plastic bottles crush easily, and cement does not bond to smooth plastic. Bottle bricks have no building standard, and their strength depends on how densely they are packed [2]. Lab work on bottle-filled blocks finds them suited to walls that carry no load [3]. Here they are just a squishy core between two thin skins.
3. **Mortar is not waterproof.** Patchy hand-troweled cement over a soft backing cannot be packed tight, leaves voids, and cracks. A pool needs a liner or properly applied pool plaster, and a bumpy bottle wall would puncture a liner.
4. **Sun and heat.** Each capped bottle is a sealed pocket of air. Heated from 70°F to 120°F in summer sun, that air tries to expand about 10 percent, pushing on the skins from inside, while the plastic itself softens near 160°F.
5. **Code and liability.** Most U.S. jurisdictions treat water deeper than 24 inches as a pool that needs a permit and a barrier, typically a 48-inch fence with self-latching gates. A sudden wall failure would dump 5,400 gallons in seconds: a wave that can knock down a child and flood a house or a neighbor's yard.

The technique itself is real, just not for this job. Builders do stand bottles between mesh and plaster over them, but for interior walls, and it uses a lot of cement [7].

## The verdict

As filmed, this is a demonstration of a wall, not a pool: two out of ten. Redesigned around a round shape, a footing and a liner, it becomes a real project of a few weekends spread over two months: six out of ten. The honest comparison is that a galvanized stock-tank pool or a framed kit pool gets you swimming sooner, with less risk, for similar money. The bottle pool is worth building only if the build itself, and the bottles you keep out of the trash, are the point.

## How to build a version that works

**Make it round.** Ferrocement water tanks are cylinders for one reason: a round wall turns water pressure into tension running around the ring, which steel wire handles well, instead of bending, which thin walls handle badly [4]. A 12-foot round pool, 30 inches deep, holds about 2,100 gallons and puts roughly 940 pounds of ring tension on each foot of wall height near the base. Fence wire alone barely covers that, so add steel hoops. Home builders have finished ferrocement pools this way, doing the steel work themselves [6].

1. **Permit and site.** Confirm the local rules, pick level ground, and call 811 before digging.
2. **Footing.** A ring trench about 12 inches wide and 8 deep, filled with concrete around a rebar ring, with short rebar dowels standing up every foot.
3. **Cage.** Vertical rebar tied to the dowels, #3 rebar hoops every 6 inches in the bottom foot and every 12 above, mesh on both faces.
4. **Core.** Bottles packed solid with sand or clean plastic, never empty, with wire ties through the core every 8 inches to lock the two skins together.
5. **Plaster.** A 1:3 cement-to-sand mortar, the standard ferrocement mix [1], pushed through the mesh and built to at least an inch on each face, then kept damp for a week.
6. **Floor and liner.** A smooth sand bed, a padded vinyl or EPDM liner, and a coping cap over the wall top.
7. **Fill test.** Fill about 6 inches a day, check for cracks and bulging each morning, and drain if anything moves.
8. **Finish.** Barrier fence, filter pump and water chemistry before anyone swims.
