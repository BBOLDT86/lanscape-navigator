**The short answer: the waterfall in the reel would work, in the sense that water falls down. It would also soak the floor, fog the windows and pour more than 17 gallons of water a day into the air of the house.** Scaled down to a thin film running down glass, it becomes a real and fairly modest project.

The clip type is a staple of home-design feeds. A plain living-room wall turns into a sheet of falling water over stone in under a minute, and the closing shot always has someone reading a book beside it. The sound is the sell. We took a typical version at face value and ran the numbers.

## What the video shows

A feature wall about 8 feet wide and 9 feet tall is clad in stacked stone. A trough is hidden at the ceiling line. When the water turns on, a thick, glassy curtain falls the full height into a narrow pebble trench cut into the floor. Lights in the trench glow up through the water. The room around it has hardwood floors, a fabric sofa a few feet away, and a TV on the next wall.

What never appears: a pump, pipe, filter, drain, splash guard, vent or outlet. Nobody tops up the water or cleans anything. The usual signs of generated video show up too. The curtain stays perfectly smooth all the way down a rough stone face, which real water won't do. The falling water makes no splash where it lands. Reflections in the sheet don't match the room. We can't prove how a given clip was made, but this one shows a mood, not a build.

For the numbers below we take it at its word: 8 feet wide, a 9-foot drop, and the kind of solid sheet the clip shows.

## The physics: 200 gallons a minute

Pond builders size a waterfall by flow per foot of width. A trickle takes about 750 gallons an hour per foot, and a full sheet about 1,500 [1]. The sheet in the reel, 8 feet wide, needs about **12,000 gallons an hour, or 200 gallons a minute.** That is a swimming-pool pump, about 1,100 watts running around the clock, pushing through pipe about 3 inches across. As a check, a pool builder planning a 10-foot water wall paired it with a 2.5-horsepower pool pump and still warned it might only dribble [9].

Then gravity. Water falling 9 feet hits the floor at about 24 feet per second, roughly 16 miles an hour. A sheet that heavy landing in a 6-inch pebble trench will splash well beyond it, onto hardwood and fabric.

The bigger cost is in the air. A wet surface gives off water vapor at a rate set by its area, the air moving over it, and how dry the room is [6]. We used the standard pool formula for a room at 72°F and 45 percent humidity. A 72-square-foot curtain of falling water, which drags air along with it, gives off about 15 gallons a day. The trench adds about 2 more. **That is about 17 gallons of water a day added to the house,** about what a whole-house humidifier puts out. The evaporation also pulls about 6,000 BTU an hour of heat out of the room.

## Five ways it fails

1. **The house gets wet.** Seventeen gallons a day has to go somewhere. In winter it condenses on windows and cold exterior walls. Builders who have lived with indoor water features describe mildew in the room and walls streaked with drips [8]. The stone wall itself stays wet behind the sheet, which is where mold starts.
2. **The spray can make people sick.** Falling water throws off a fine mist. The CDC notes that every decorative fountain makes aerosols, and that Legionella grows best in water from 77 to 113°F [2]. A room-temperature basin with a heated pump sits close to that band. In 2012, a lobby fountain at a Chicago hotel was linked to 114 cases of Legionnaires' disease and Pontiac fever, and three people died [3]. Investigators found no record of regular cleaning or disinfection.
3. **Stone breaks up the sheet.** A real sheet of water needs a perfectly level, sharp lip and a smooth face. On stacked stone it tears into ropes and splatter. Fixing that takes more flow, which means more splash and more evaporation.
4. **Power meets water.** The clip shows lights in the water and a pump somewhere. Code requires fountain equipment that sits in the water to be on GFCI protection, metal piping to be bonded, and pumps to have protection against running dry [4]. A hidden pump with a cord buried in stone has none of that.
5. **Upkeep never ends.** Water evaporates and leaves its minerals behind, so the stone scales white and the pump clogs. Without an automatic fill, the basin runs low in a day or two and the pump burns out.

The safety issues are real ones: a shock from failed lights or a pump, and a lung infection that can kill, from water nobody treats. Neither is rare in the record. Both are cheap to prevent.

## The verdict

As filmed, this is a rainstorm in the living room: three out of ten. The look itself is not hard to get. Change the sheet to a film that clings to glass, keep the water cool and sanitized, and put it on a GFCI circuit: seven out of ten. Commercial water walls work exactly this way, with a sealed stainless basin, an automatic fill, a drain overflow, a separate fused pump circuit and a built-in water treatment system [5].

## How to build a version that works

**Make the water cling.** A thin film of water running down a glass panel stays on the glass, so it barely splashes and moves far less air. One maker's glass water wall runs up to 8,000 liters an hour per meter of width [7], about 640 gallons an hour per foot. We sized the kit's pump to 600 and expect most builders to turn it down. A 4 by 7 foot wall needs a pump of about 2,400 gallons an hour, about a sixth of what the reel's sheet needs. It gives off about 2.6 gallons a day of water vapor. That is the output of a small humidifier, welcome in a dry winter and manageable in summer.

1. **Plan the humidity.** If the room already runs above 50 percent in summer, plan on a dehumidifier or a bigger wall-mounted HVAC return. Keep the wall off exterior walls and away from windows.
2. **Permit the power.** Have an electrician run a GFCI-protected circuit and a wall switch beside the feature [4][5]. Use 12-volt lights rated for wet locations, and bond any metal frame or pipe.
3. **Fabricate the parts.** A local metal shop can weld a stainless basin about 15 inches wide and 8 deep, plus a top trough with a sharp, level lip. Order the glass tempered, half an inch thick, from a glass shop.
4. **Waterproof the floor.** Lay a membrane and tile tray under the basin, a few inches wider on every side. Flood-test it for a day.
5. **Mount and level.** Anchor the frame into studs or blocking. Level the trough to within a thirty-second of an inch, or the film will run to one side.
6. **Treat the water.** Add a UV sterilizer and a cartridge filter. Keep the water under 77°F, hold a free-chlorine level per the CDC's fountain guide, and test it every week [2]. Fit a float valve that tops up the basin, an overflow to a drain, and a float switch that shuts the pump off if the water runs low.
7. **Clean on a schedule.** Drain, scrub and refill every month. Clean right away if the water turns cloudy, smells, or grows a film [2].
