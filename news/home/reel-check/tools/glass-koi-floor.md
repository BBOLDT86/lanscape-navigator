**The short answer: a living-room floor of glass over a koi pond, laid on ordinary floor joists the way the reel shows, would put about five times the design load on the floor and fog over within a day.** A smaller version, a glass window into a concrete pond set into a ground-floor slab, can be built. It is a job for an engineer, a concrete crew and a structural glazier, with the homeowner running the pond.

The clip type is one of the most shared in home-design feeds. A family walks across what looks like open water, and fat orange koi drift between their feet. It reads as luxury and calm at once. We took a typical version at face value and ran the numbers.

## What the video shows

The clip opens on a plain wood-floored living room about 10 by 16 feet. The flooring comes up, and the joists below are shown briefly. Then the space between them becomes a shallow pond lined with river stones and plants. Large glass panels drop onto a thin metal grid. Water goes in, koi go in, and the last shot is a toddler lying on the glass, nose to nose with a fish.

What never appears: a concrete shell, a drain, a filter, a pump room, a fan, an access hatch or an electrician. There is no headspace between the water and the glass, and the glass never fogs. The generated-video signs are the usual ones. The joists vanish from one cut to the next, and the water is somehow deeper than the floor framing. Koi pass through each other at the edges. Reflections on the glass show a different room. We can't prove how a given clip was made, but this one never shows where the water's weight goes.

For the numbers below we take it at its word: a pond the size of the room, 10 by 16 feet, with water 3 feet deep, the shallowest depth koi keepers accept.

## The physics: 15 tons on a floor built for three

A 10 by 16 foot pond, 3 feet deep, holds about 3,600 gallons, roughly **30,000 pounds or 15 tons of water.** Water 3 feet deep presses down with about 187 pounds on every square foot. Add three plies of half-inch glass at about 20 pounds per square foot, plus the frame and stones, and the floor carries well over 200 pounds per square foot.

The code designs living-room floors for 40 pounds per square foot of live load [7]. Over the full 160 square feet, that is a design load of 6,400 pounds, a bit over 3 tons. **The pond asks for about five times that,** and asks for it every hour of every day, not just at a party. Wood under that kind of constant load also creeps, sagging more each year. As a check from the other direction, a builder in one forum thread put a smaller 12 by 16 foot pond, only 2 feet deep, at about 12 tons, or 120 pounds per square foot [6].

Then the glass. Code requires glass you walk on to be laminated, meeting either the industry standard for glass walkways or the full structural load rules [2]. Glass floor makers design so the floor still carries its load with one layer broken [1]. A common layup is three or more layers of toughened glass bonded with structural interlayers, with the top one treated as sacrificial [4]. The reel's single panes on a thin grid don't qualify.

## Five ways it fails

1. **The floor fails.** Joists sized for 40 pounds per square foot carrying 200 or more deflect, crack drywall below, and in time can fail. There is also no waterproof shell. The water sits on whatever is between the joists.
2. **The glass fogs and drips.** The air in the gap under the glass sits right over the water, so it is close to saturated. Any time the glass is a degree cooler than that air, water condenses on its underside, and the view is gone. An open pond this size, at 72°F in a 70°F room, would also put about 25 gallons of water a day into the house [8]. Builders who lived with indoor ponds report mildew, dripping walls and warped hardwood [6].
3. **The koi suffocate or sicken.** Koi keepers want 3 feet of depth at minimum, about 200 gallons per adult fish, filtration of 5 to 10 percent of the pond's volume, and turnover one to two times an hour [5]. A sealed shallow pond with no drain or filter fouls in days. The reel shows about 20 koi, enough for 4,000 gallons.
4. **No one can reach the water.** Koi need netting for treatment, the drain needs flushing, and plants need trimming. With panels set down on the frame and no hatch, every visit means lifting several hundred pounds of glass.
5. **Slips and shocks.** Smooth glass is slick, and texturing it to stop slips can cut its strength by up to half [1]. Pumps, UV units and lights in the water need GFCI protection and bonding.

The danger is real. A panel that breaks under someone drops them into 3 feet of water with sharp edges, and a toddler can drown in far less. The fixed version has to make breakage a non-event.

## The verdict

As filmed, this is a weight problem with fish in it: one out of ten. Done the way professionals build it, with a concrete pond set into a ground-floor slab, a small walk-on glass window over part of it, a vented gap under the glass and a filter room next door, it works: five out of ten. It scores low on cost and on how much of it a homeowner can do. Structural glass alone runs about $150 to $200 per square foot, with the steel or timber framing under it adding $100 to $250 more [3].

## How to build a version that works

**Put the water in the ground and the glass over part of it.** A pond set into the soil under a slab carries its weight down to the earth, not into joists. A glass window of 6 by 6 feet over a pond 8 feet square still gives the walk-over-the-koi moment. Solid floor around the glass covers a foot of pond on every side, which hides the edges and gives the fish a shady place to retreat.

1. **Start on a slab.** Choose a ground floor on a slab, or a basement floor. Never a framed floor.
2. **Hire the design.** Get an engineer to design the shell, the cut through the slab and the frame for the glass. Get a structural glazier to supply laminated panels rated to carry the load with one ply broken [1][2]. The glass should have an anti-slip top in the glazier's tested finish.
3. **Cut and dig.** A contractor saw-cuts the slab, digs out the hole, and shores or slopes it as the engineer says. Keep the hole well clear of the house footings.
4. **Plumb before you pour.** Set a bottom drain and a skimmer line before the concrete goes in [5]. A 4-inch gravity drain handles about 3,600 gallons an hour.
5. **Pour, seal and test.** Pour the reinforced shell, seal it with a fish-safe coating, then fill it and watch the level for a week.
6. **Vent the gap.** Leave about a foot of air between the water and the glass. Run a small fan that pulls that air outside all the time, and keep the water a few degrees cooler than the room so the glass stays above the dew point. This is the part most likely to need tuning.
7. **Build the filter room.** Next door, in a closet or utility room, put the filter, pump, UV and air pump on GFCI circuits. Add a flush access hatch in the solid floor over the pond.
8. **Set the glass and stock slowly.** Seat the panels on setting blocks so glass never touches steel. Cycle the filter for several weeks, then add koi a few at a time.
