**The short answer: the pool in the reel sits on a hillside with nothing holding it there. Its 60 tons of water want to go downhill.** The vanishing edge itself is a solved problem. What makes or breaks a hillside pool is the ground under it, and that is a job for a soils report, piers and a pool contractor.

The appeal is obvious. A slope that seemed like a problem becomes the best seat in the yard, and the pool's far edge melts into the view. These clips draw long comment threads from people with sloped lots asking whether they could do the same. The answer is yes, but not the way the clip shows.

## What the video shows

The clip opens on a lawn falling away toward trees or a valley. A crew sets plywood forms on the grass and ties a light rebar cage inside. Concrete goes in from a pump truck. The uphill side is cut a little into the slope. The downhill wall stands several feet clear of the ground. The forms come off, the shell is painted blue, and the pool fills until water slides over the low edge. The last shot is from the deck, with the water seeming to run straight into the sky.

What never appears is most of a hillside pool. No soil borings, no piers or caissons, no retaining wall uphill, no drain under the shell. The water pouring over the edge falls onto the slope itself, with no catch basin, pump or pipe to return it. There is no fence, and a person on the downhill side would face a drop of several feet. Clips like this often carry the marks of generated video. The edge water is perfectly smooth and endless, the downhill wall has no stain from the overflow, and the slope below stays dry. We can't prove how a given clip was made. As a guide, it shows the finish and skips the structure.

For the numbers we assume a 14 by 28 foot pool, 5 feet deep on average, on a yard that falls 20 percent. The edge runs along the long downhill side.

## The physics: 61 tons on a hill

The pool holds about 14,700 gallons, which weigh about 61 tons. Set that weight on a 20 percent slope with nothing anchoring it, and the part of it pulling downhill comes to about 24,000 pounds. **That is twelve tons of steady downhill pull. Only friction with whatever soil lies under the shell resists it.**

It gets worse because the soil is not uniform. On a slope, the uphill end usually sits on cut ground while the downhill end sits on fill pushed out to make a level pad. The two settle at different rates. Engineering geologists say that if any part of a pool reaches bedrock, the whole pool should be founded in it [1]. Uneven wedges of fill under one pool are a classic cause of settlement and cracking [1]. One homeowner's two-year-old hillside pool, its shell built on dirt fill over a single rebar mat, began cracking and leaking. Three structural engineers then gave three different fixes [6].

Water adds a second hazard. Every crack that leaks soaks the slope below, and wet soil is weaker soil. Cracks near a slope have to be repaired at once, because a leaking shell can destabilize the hill [1]. The edge itself needs water. A quarter-inch sheet over a square edge takes about 7 to 10 gallons a minute per foot [4]. Along 28 feet, that is about 280 gallons a minute pouring downhill, unless something catches it.

## Five ways it fails

1. **No foundation for a slope.** Hillside pools are usually free-standing shells on caissons or friction piles tied with grade beams, reaching into competent ground [1]. Borings can be about 24 inches across and up to 50 feet deep [1]. A slab poured on grass has none of that. It can creep, settle at one end, or crack across the middle.
2. **No catch basin.** The vanishing edge is a waterfall into a hidden trough that a pump returns to the pool. Without it, the pool empties itself over the hill and erodes the slope it stands on [2]. Builders size the basin to hold at least the top 2 inches of the pool [4]. They add room for swimmers' surge, about 18.5 gallons per 155-pound person [2], and keep at least a foot of water over the drain [4]. For our pool that is about 1,450 gallons.
3. **The edge is not level.** Builders aim for a weir within a sixteenth of an inch from end to end [3]. A form set by eye on a slope will be off by far more, so water pours from one corner while the rest stays dry.
4. **Leaks through a freestanding wall.** The weir wall is wet on both sides. It needs waterproofing on both faces and the top, extending past the wall on every side [3]. It should be shot full thickness from one side in one pour. Shooting from both sides makes two thin walls that can split apart [3].
5. **Falls and drowning.** A downhill wall standing 8 or 9 feet above the ground is a fall hazard as well as an edge. The catch basin is a second body of water and needs its own protection: stairs, a fence or a grate [3]. Codes call for a barrier at least 48 inches high around the pool [7].

## The verdict

As filmed, one out of ten: a heavy box set on a slope with no foundation and no plan for the water that leaves it. Built properly, five out of ten. The physics is well understood, and builders have done these for decades. But almost all the work is professional, and the cost is steep. Infinity pools typically run $60,000 to $150,000, and a vanishing edge adds about 20 to 25 percent over a standard pool of the same size [5]. Our 14 by 28 foot version on a 20 percent slope comes to roughly $105,000 to $265,000 once piers, a retaining wall and the basin are counted. A cheaper route to the view: a standard pool set into a terrace with a level patio, with the money saved spent on the patio.

## How to build a version that works

**Found it on the ground, not on the slope, and catch every drop.** The plan below puts the pool on drilled piers and holds back the hill uphill. It returns the edge water through a sized basin. The homeowner's part is choosing the team, checking the work at each gate, and the fence and planting.

1. **Get a soils and slope report first.** A geologist or geotechnical engineer drills borings and says where competent ground is. Without that report, a good design is close to impossible [1]. Budget $1,000 to $5,000 [5].
2. **Hire the engineer and the right contractor.** A structural engineer designs the shell, piers, grade beams and basin. Pick a pool contractor whose vanishing-edge work you have seen in person.
3. **Drill piers.** Piers go to the depth the engineer sets, tied by grade beams under the shell. Under the shell goes gravel with perforated drain pipe or relief valves, so groundwater cannot pop the shell [1].
4. **Hold the hill.** An engineered retaining wall uphill, drained behind, so the slope cannot push into the pool.
5. **Shoot the shell and build the weir.** Full thickness, one side, one pour. Screed the weir with a laser level accurate to better than an eighth of an inch per 100 feet [3].
6. **Size the basin and give it its own pump.** An electronic leveler, not a float valve, controls the water [2]. Run the edge only as long as you need the look. Constant overflow builds calcium scale on the wall.
7. **Waterproof everything.** Both faces and the top of the weir wall, the basin, and ideally the whole shell [3].
8. **Fence, drain and plant.** A barrier around the pool and the basin, deck drains piped off the slope, never onto it [1], and deep-rooted planting below.
