**The short answer: the meadow in the reel weighs about three times what a typical shed roof is framed to carry, and it sits on a tarp that will leak within a season.** Green roofs on sheds are real, though. A shallow, light sedum roof on stronger rafters is a weekend or two of work and will outlast the shed.

Living-roof clips are some of the most soothing on the feed. A plain shed turns into a wildflower meadow in a few seconds, and someone kneels on top to pull a weed. Some are real projects. Many lately are generated: faceless, golden-lit, and finished in a single cut. We took a typical one at its word and weighed the roof.

## What the video shows

A standard backyard shed with a nearly flat, single-slope roof sits in a garden. Someone lays a blue tarp across the roof and nails a board along the low edge. Wheelbarrow after wheelbarrow of dark garden soil goes up a ladder and is raked out about 6 inches deep. Wildflower seed and grass plugs go in. A time-lapse fills the roof with tall flowers and grasses, and in the last shot a person kneels on top, weeding.

What never appears is telling. Nobody looks at the rafters or adds any. Water never runs off; there is no gutter, no outlet and no gravel strip. Nothing holds the soil at the sides. In generated versions the shed's roof thickens between shots, the board along the edge comes and goes, and the person on top leaves no dent in the soil. We can't prove how a given clip was made, but its roof carries a load nobody planned for.

For the numbers below we assume a common 8 by 10 foot lean-to shed with 2x4 rafters every 24 inches spanning 8 feet, and 6 inches of garden soil after a soaking rain.

## The physics: 68 pounds on a 22-pound roof

Wet garden soil is heavy, about 100 to 125 pounds per cubic foot [6]. Six inches of it, soaked, comes to roughly 58 pounds on every square foot of roof. Add about 10 for the roof itself and the load is **about 68 pounds per square foot**.

Now the rafters. A No. 2 2x4 every 24 inches, spanning 8 feet, can carry about 22 pounds per square foot before it sags past normal limits. Its strength in bending is only a little higher. **The soaked roof is about three times what the rafters can hold, before any snow.** In a snowy region, add 20 to 40 pounds more on top. Wood under a constant overload creeps: the roof sags over weeks, and a heavy rain or snowfall can then split a rafter.

For comparison, a real extensive green roof with 3 to 4 inches of engineered media weighs about 17 to 18 pounds per square foot when saturated [1]. That is a third of the reel's roof. The media is made light on purpose, mostly from expanded shale, pumice or similar mineral grit with a little compost. Even that load needs checking. Green roof experts note that many retrofit plans die when the building turns out too weak [4].

As a check on our rafter math, a city span table rates a stronger species, Douglas fir No. 2 2x6 at 24 inches, to about 10 feet for a 40 psf load [5]. Our method gives the weaker spruce 2x6 about 37 psf at the same span, close to that table.

## Five ways it fails

1. **The roof is overloaded.** Wet garden soil at 6 inches loads 2x4 rafters about three times past their limit, and much more with snow. Every green roof guide begins with a structural check by an engineer [1][2][3].
2. **A tarp is not a roof membrane.** Green roofs hold water against the roof for days. That makes a real waterproof layer essential, and roots will find their way through anything less without a root barrier [1]. A polyethylene tarp cracks in the sun and splits along folds within a season or two. Then water and roots go straight into the decking.
3. **No drainage.** On a nearly flat roof, soaked soil and ponded water just sit. Real systems lay a drainage layer between a quarter-inch and an inch and a half thick [2] under a filter fabric. They also keep a clear strip at the outlet so water can escape [3].
4. **Nothing holds the edges.** Soil slides on a slope, even a gentle one. Green roof guides call for edge restraints that let water pass [3]. Slopes steeper than about 30 percent need grids or baffles to stop the media from slipping [2].
5. **The wrong plants, the wrong soil, and a person on top.** Meadow flowers need deep soil and steady water. A thin roof bakes and drains fast. Sedums and similar succulents are the standard picks for shallow roofs [2]. And a person kneeling on a shed roof that is already overloaded is the moment a rafter is most likely to break. A fall from even a shed roof can break bones.

## The verdict

As filmed, the roof would sag, leak and could fail under a person or a storm: two out of ten. The fix is well understood, though. Reframe the roof for the soaked weight plus snow, waterproof it with a single sheet of EPDM, add a root barrier, drainage mat and kerb, and plant sedums in 3 inches of light media. That is a seven: a living roof that cools the shed, slows stormwater and looks good for decades.

## How to build a version that works

The standard layers, from the deck up, are a waterproof membrane, a root barrier, a drainage layer, filter fabric, growing media and plants [3]. Extensive media is usually laid 3 to 6 inches deep [2]; with a shed's small size, 3 inches is a good place to start. The kit below sizes the rafters for your span and media depth.

1. **Measure the shed.** Rafter size, spacing and span, and the condition of the walls, floor and base. Rot anywhere means repair first.
2. **Have an engineer check it.** The soaked green roof is a permanent load; snow and people come on top [4]. A short review of the rafters, walls and base costs a few hundred dollars and is worth it.
3. **Reframe the roof.** Sister or replace rafters at 16 inches to the size the kit shows, with hangers and hurricane ties. Give the roof at least a quarter-inch per foot of fall toward one outlet.
4. **Deck and waterproof.** Lay 3/4-inch exterior plywood, then one unbroken sheet of 45 mil EPDM turned up the kerb on all sides. Hose it for 30 minutes and check the ceiling for drips.
5. **Lay the layers.** Root barrier, drainage mat and filter fabric, in that order. Fix a perforated aluminum or cedar kerb around the edge, and keep a strip of gravel clear at the outlet.
6. **Add media, not soil.** Use a commercial extensive green roof mix, not garden soil. Spread it from a ladder at the edge; do not stand on the roof.
7. **Plant sedums.** Plugs or cuttings are cheapest; pre-grown mats give instant cover [3]. Water weekly until they root.
8. **Catch the runoff.** A gutter and downspout to a rain barrel. Check the ceiling after every heavy rain for the first season.
