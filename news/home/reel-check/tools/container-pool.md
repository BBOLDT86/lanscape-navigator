**The short answer: with its roof cut off, the box in the reel would bulge at the top. Buried in wet ground, it would be crushed when drained or float up out of its hole.** Factory-converted container pools do exist, though. Set above ground on a braced frame, the idea can be made sound.

Container pools are one of the most-shared backyard ideas online, and for good reason. The box is cheap, it is the right shape for laps, and the industrial look photographs well. The reels make it look like a weekend: buy a box, dig a hole, add water. The companies that build these for a living tell a different story [3].

## What the video shows

A used 20-foot container arrives on a tilt-bed truck. A small excavator digs a pit about 9 by 22 feet and most of the box's height deep. The container is lowered in. A worker runs an angle grinder around the roof and lifts it off in one sheet. A blue liner is draped over the top edge, a hose goes in, and the box fills almost to the brim. The last shot is a sleek steel lap pool set flush with a new wood deck.

What never appears is everything between the box and the water. No slab or gravel bed, no drain around the pit, no welding, no added steel, no sandblasting or coating, no pump or skimmer, no electrician and no fence. The wooden floor, soaked in pesticide for shipping, stays in. The tells of generated video are common in this genre too. The grinder cuts the roof in one smooth pass with no sparks, the liner fits without a fold, and the corrugations stay perfectly straight as the water rises. We can't prove how a given clip was made. As instructions, it leaves out the parts that hold it together.

For the numbers we take a standard 20-foot box, about 19.3 by 7.7 feet inside, filled 4.5 feet deep.

## The physics: 12,000 pounds on a wall built for cargo

At 4.5 feet deep the box holds about 5,000 gallons, around 21 tons of water. Water pushes out on each long wall with about 630 pounds per foot of length. **Along one 19-foot side that is about 12,000 pounds, pressing outward all the time.**

A container is strong in a specific way. Its load runs down the frame and the eight corner castings, which is how it stacks nine high on a ship [2]. The side walls are corrugated steel sheet, typically 1.6 to 2.0 millimeters thick, there for rigidity and to keep the cargo in [2]. Each wall hangs between a bottom rail and a top rail.

That corrugated sheet is not the weak link. Treat it as a vertical span between the two rails. At 4.5 feet of water, its bending stress works out to roughly 19,000 pounds per square inch, under what the steel can take. The trouble is at the top. About a fifth of the wall's load, roughly 120 pounds per foot, goes into the top rail. The roof normally ties the two top rails together. Cut it off and each rail must span 19 feet on its own. That puts a bending moment of about 5,700 pound-feet at its middle. A top rail is a light steel tube, only a couple of inches across. On our rough sizing the stress runs well past yield. The sides would bow out at mid-length and the liner would follow.

Bury it and two new problems arrive. Wet soil pushes inward on an 8-foot wall with roughly 1,900 pounds per foot when the pool is drained. That is three times what the water pushed outward. Container makers say the roof and side walls are not built to hold back earth or water pressure, and that a standard box should not be fully buried [1]. And groundwater 4 feet up the sides would lift an empty box with about 19 tons of buoyancy. The box itself weighs about 2.3 tons.

## Six ways it fails

1. **The cut roof.** Removing the roof takes away the tie between the top rails. Factory converters weld in extra steel. A rival pool maker says container pools need a second layer of steel welded inside the walls, adding a lot of weight [5]. The reel adds nothing.
2. **Buried and drained.** In-ground means drainage, an engineered foundation and usually a pool builder [3]. Without them, the walls cave inward the first time the pool is emptied, and a high water table can pop the box up out of the ground.
3. **Rust.** Corten steel resists weather, not chlorinated water held against it. Without a proper coating, holes can appear within a few years [3]. Even coated, the steel can rust over time, and salt systems speed it up [4]. A pinhole under a liner is invisible until the wall is gone.
4. **The floor.** Container floors are marine plywood, 28 to 36 millimeters thick, treated against pests [2]. Pesticides used on them include phoxim, chlorfenapyr, cypermethrin and imidacloprid [6]. That floor should come out, carefully, and be replaced with welded steel plate.
5. **Electrical.** A steel shell full of water next to a pump is exactly what pool bonding rules exist for. Bonding keeps every metal part at the same voltage so a fault cannot drive current through a swimmer; it calls for at least 8 AWG solid copper [7]. The reel's extension cord to a pump is an electrocution risk.
6. **The barrier.** A pool this deep needs a barrier at least 48 inches high around it, with small gaps at the bottom [8]. A box flush with a deck has no barrier at all. An above-ground box with locked steps can sometimes serve as its own, depending on the town [4].

## The verdict

As filmed, two out of ten: the box would bulge as it filled, rust from inside, and fight its hole every time it was drained. Built above ground on a slab, with welded buttresses, a steel floor, a real coating, a liner and bonding, it is a sound lap pool: five out of ten. The score stays modest because of money. A factory-converted pool costs about $40,000 to over $50,000 before site work [3]. Fully installed projects commonly run $50,000 to $85,000 [4]. Our self-managed version lands nearer $27,000 to $61,000 for a 20-foot box. That is not cheap, and a framed kit pool swims just as well for less. The container is worth it if you want that look and will do the coating and deck work yourself.

## How to build a version that works

**Keep it above ground, and brace it.** Above ground, soil and groundwater never push on the walls, and you can see every weld and every drop of rust. Steel buttresses take over the job the roof used to do.

1. **Engineer and permit first.** Get a stamped design for the slab, the buttresses and how they anchor. Buy a container you have walked inside, checking for holes and floor rot.
2. **Pour a slab.** Reinforced concrete, level within a quarter inch, with anchor plates where the buttresses land. The filled pool weighs about 23 tons.
3. **Set it and strip it.** Crane the box onto the slab. Pull out the treated plywood floor wearing a mask and gloves, and bag it.
4. **Weld.** Plate the floor in steel over the cross members. Weld steel posts to the outside of each long wall about every 4 feet, braced back to the slab. Only then cut down the roof, leaving a flange along the top. Hire a certified welder.
5. **Blast and coat.** Sandblast to bare metal and apply two coats of a two-part epoxy, inside and out.
6. **Bond, plumb and line.** An electrician bonds the steel and installs a GFCI circuit. Fit a skimmer and returns. Line the walls with closed-cell foam, then a custom vinyl liner.
7. **Fill in stages.** Measure the wall at mid-length as each foot of water goes in. If it moves more than the engineer allows, stop and drain.
8. **Deck and barrier.** Build the deck and a 48-inch barrier with a self-latching gate before anyone swims.
