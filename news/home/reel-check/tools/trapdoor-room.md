## The physics: 100 tons through a 3-foot hole

A finished room 12 by 14 feet with 8 feet of headroom needs a hole about 14 by 16 feet and 9.5 feet deep. That leaves room for walls, a footing and a slab with gravel under it. **That is about 79 cubic yards of soil, or roughly 106 tons.** Hauled out in 5-gallon buckets, it is about 3,200 trips up a ladder through the hatch. A single cubic yard of soil weighs about as much as a car [1].

The danger is in the walls of the hole. Workplace rules require a protective system, such as shoring or a trench box, in any excavation 5 feet deep or more, and cave-ins are the excavation hazard most likely to kill [1]. The reel's hole is nearly twice that deep. Its sides are the soil holding up the home's own footings. Dig within a few feet of a footing and the soil under it can slide into the hole, taking part of the foundation with it.

Then there is the air. A sealed underground room is a textbook confined space: a space not designed for people to stay in, with one restricted way in or out [2]. Workplace rules call the air in such a space hazardous once oxygen falls below 19.5 percent [2]. Even with good oxygen, people breathe out carbon dioxide. Four people resting in a sealed 12 by 14 by 8 foot room push it from outdoor levels to 5,000 parts per million in **about two and a half hours.** That is the federal limit for a worker's average exposure over a full shift. And soil gas brings radon, which the EPA links to about 21,000 lung cancer deaths a year in the U.S. [6].

## Six ways it fails

1. **The hole caves in.** Unshored soil walls 9 feet high can collapse with no warning, onto the person digging. This is the main way the build kills [1].
2. **The house drops.** Removing the soil next to and under footings lets them settle or rotate. Professionals dig out basements under houses only after they underpin the footings or jack up the house, and they call it no job for a homeowner [3].
3. **The floor loses its joists.** Cutting a hatch through the floor severs joists. Code requires headers and trimmers around floor openings: doubled once the header spans more than 4 feet, and on metal hangers past 6 feet [10]. The reel's hinged panel simply floats.
4. **One way in is the only way out.** A room below grade that people use needs an emergency escape opening, such as a window big enough to climb out of, with a sill no higher than 44 inches [5]. A single ladder under a heavy lid is a trap in a fire.
5. **The air goes bad.** No vent means stale air, rising CO2, and radon with nowhere to go [6]. Damp soil walls without drainage add mold.
6. **The lid hurts someone.** A finished floor panel weighs 70 pounds or more. Dropped or thrown shut, it puts huge bending forces on the frame and hinges [7], and on any fingers in the gap. An open hatch in a living room is also a hole to fall into.

## The verdict

As filmed, this is a hazard dressed as a hobby: one out of ten. Even done properly, a professional dig-out under an existing house runs from about $50,000 to $150,000 [3][4], with an engineer, underpinning, waterproofing and a crew. The good version starts from space you already have. Frame a room in one corner of an existing basement, give it a hidden hatch from the floor above and a hidden door into the rest of the basement, and you get the same reveal with none of the digging: seven out of ten.

## How to build a version that works

**Borrow the basement.** The basement already has a footing, a slab, a code stair and, if it is finished, an egress window. Your secret room is a partition and a hatch, not a hole.

1. **Test for radon first.** Test the basement, and retest once the room is in use [6]. Fix the house if it reads 4 pCi/L or higher before you spend anything on the room.
2. **Pick the corner.** Choose one beside the basement stair or the egress window, so the hidden room's normal door opens a few steps from a way out [5]. Find a spot above where the hatch can fall between the joists or cut as few as possible.
3. **Engineer and permit.** Have an engineer size the headers for the floor opening, and pull building and electrical permits. A code stair needs risers no taller than 7¾ inches and 6 feet 8 inches of headroom [9]. A ladder through a hatch can't meet that, so it must be an extra way in, never the only one. Ask your building department how it treats one before you buy.
4. **Frame the opening.** Install doubled headers and trimmers in joist hangers around a clear opening about 30 by 44 inches [10].
5. **Frame the room.** Build two stud walls off the foundation and hang a prehung bookcase door. It must open from inside without a key.
6. **Air and alarms.** Run a small exhaust fan ducted outside, all the time, and put an interconnected smoke and CO alarm in the room.
7. **Build the hatch.** Frame the lid stiff, skin it in plywood, and lay the matching floor on top. Hang it on through-bolted hinges into the header. Use a pair of gas struts sized so the lid still needs a slight push to close [7], plus a hold-open arm that locks it open the way commercial floor doors do [8]. Keep a guard rail that drops into sockets around the open hatch.
8. **Ladder and inspect.** Bolt the ship's ladder top and bottom, with handrails on both sides. Pass the final inspection, then retest the radon.
