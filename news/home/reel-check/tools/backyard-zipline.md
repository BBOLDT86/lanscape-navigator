**The short answer: a cable between two trees really can carry a rider across a yard, but the drum-tight, steep, brakeless line in the reel is the version that sends people to the emergency room.** A slack, gentle line with a brake, healthy trees and soft ground is a reasonable backyard toy. It still needs a weekly look and an adult at the launch.

The zipline reel is short and joyful. Someone wraps a cable around two trees, cranks it tight, clips on a pulley and a kid flies across the lawn, sometimes over a pool, sometimes off a deck. Plenty of those clips are real, and more are now AI-generated with flights that ignore gravity. Doctors have watched the trend from the other end. A study of U.S. emergency rooms counted about 16,850 zipline injuries over 16 years, with almost 70 percent in the last four [1]. Children under 10 made up 45 percent. The researchers advise against homemade backyard ziplines. We took the typical clip at its word and ran the numbers.

## What the video shows

The clip opens on two big trees about 100 feet apart. A steel cable is looped around each trunk and clamped. A truck or a hand winch cranks it until it barely droops. A child grabs a pulley handle at the high end, maybe 15 feet up a ladder, and rides it steeply down, fast. At the far end the rider lets go into the grass, or into a pool, or swings into the trunk and laughs.

What never appears: a brake, a helmet, a seat or harness, any sag, padding on the trees, or an adult checking the hardware. Generated clips add their own tells. The cable stays perfectly straight under the rider. The pulley has no wheels you can see. The rider hangs one-handed at highway speed. The anchor tree never flexes. We can't prove how any one clip was made. Most read as made to be watched, not copied.

For the numbers below we assume a 100-foot line and a 175-pound rider, an adult or a big teenager.

## The physics: a cable that multiplies weight

A rider hanging in the middle of a cable pulls down. The cable can only push back along its own length, so the flatter it runs, the harder it has to pull to hold the rider up. For a load at mid-span the tension is the weight times the span, divided by four times the sag.

Crank a 100-foot line until it sags only 6 inches under a 175-pound rider. **The cable then pulls on each tree with about 8,750 pounds, 50 times the rider's weight.** Let it sag 2 feet, the 2 percent that kit makers recommend [2], and the pull falls to about 2,190 pounds, about 12 times the rider. Either way, the cable pulls far harder than the rider weighs. Bouncing, jumping on at the start or two riders at once can double the load.

The cable itself is usually fine. Common 3/8-inch galvanized aircraft cable breaks at about 14,400 pounds [4]. The weak points are the ends. Wire rope clips installed correctly keep up to about 85 percent of the cable's strength, and installed wrong they lose much more [5]. At the drum-tight line, bounce alone takes the ends past their limit. At 2 percent sag, the line keeps a margin of about 2.6 even after allowing for bounce.

Then the speed. Every foot of drop turns into speed. A line that falls 15 feet over 100 feet, a 15 percent slope, could reach about 21 miles an hour at the bottom with no friction. At the 3 percent slope kit makers suggest for lines without an engineered brake [2], the limit is under 10 miles an hour. Drag and the trolley shave off more.

## Six ways it fails

1. **Too tight.** The tighter the line, the more a rider's weight multiplies at the anchors, and higher tension makes that worse [3]. A drum-tight cable also gives the rider a stiff ride and a fast finish.
2. **No brake.** Without one, the rider stops by letting go or by hitting the end tree. Falls caused 77 percent of zipline injuries in the hospital study and collisions 13 percent. Fractures were the most common injury, at 46 percent [1]. Kit makers call for a spring or bungee brake on anything steeper than 3 percent [2].
3. **Too low, or over hard things.** Kit makers ask for at least 7 feet of clearance under the heaviest rider along the whole run [2]. A guide for builders adds 5 feet of clear space on each side and a padded stop block at the end [7]. A line over a pool, a fence, rocks or a patio turns a slip into a real fall.
4. **Bare cable on bark.** A cable looped around a trunk crushes the living layer just under the bark, which carries sugar from the leaves to the roots. The tree declines, and a partly girdled tree is more likely to fail [6]. That tree is also your anchor.
5. **The wrong trees.** A dead limb over the run or a trunk with hidden rot can fail under a pull of a ton or more. Builders' guides call for healthy, mature trees at least 12 inches thick where the cable attaches [7].
6. **Clips on backward.** The saddle of each clip goes on the live, load-bearing side of the cable, and the U-bolt on the dead tail. Riggers sum it up as never saddle a dead horse [5]. Two loose clips put on backward are a common way homemade lines let go.

## The verdict

As filmed: two out of ten. The idea is sound, but the cable is too tight, the slope too steep, and there is no brake, helmet, seat or tree protection. Built from a braked kit at 2 percent sag and 3 percent slope, on arborist-checked trees, it earns a cautious five. It is a fun, slow ride that costs about $1,200 to $3,200 with the arborist's visit. It still sits in a category of injury doctors would rather you avoided, and it only stays safe if someone inspects it.

## How to build a version that works

**Go slack, slow and braked.** A good backyard line feels gentle. The rider glides to a soft stop at the end and walks back.

1. **Pick the run.** Choose a clear path at least 10 feet wide. Nothing but grass under it: no pool, fence, rock, patio or road, and nothing past the end.
2. **Have an arborist check both trees.** Look for healthy trunks at least 12 inches thick at anchor height, with no decay and no dead limbs over the run.
3. **Protect the trees.** Use tree-saver blocks or wide slings, never bare cable. Move or loosen them each season as the trunk grows.
4. **Set the heights.** The end anchor sits at 7 feet plus the sag; the start anchor sits higher by 3 percent of the span, adjusted for slope in the ground [2]. On flat ground, a 100-foot line runs from about 12 feet down to 9.
5. **Hang and clip it right.** Use three forged clips at each end, saddles on the live side, torqued and re-checked after the first rides.
6. **Tension to 2 percent sag.** With the heaviest rider hanging mid-span, the cable should dip at least 2 feet on a 100-foot line. Tighter is worse, not better.
7. **Brake and test.** Fit a spring or bungee brake with a stop block. Run a sandbag at full rider weight several times before anyone rides.
8. **Ride by the rules.** Helmets, a seat or harness, one rider at a time, and an adult at the launch. Check anchors weekly and the cable monthly.
