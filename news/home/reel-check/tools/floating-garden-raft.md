**The short answer: the raft in the reel would sink as soon as the soil got wet, and the person kneeling on it would go into the pond with it.** Floating gardens are real, though. Farmers have grown food on them for centuries, and a light, tethered foam raft of wetland plants is a well-tested way to clean a backyard pond.

Floating-garden clips are pure feed magic: a pallet, some bottles, a pile of dirt, and a vegetable patch drifting on still water. Many look generated lately, faceless and impossibly calm. Even real ones skip the part where the raft goes under. We took a typical clip at its word and ran the buoyancy.

## What the video shows

The clip opens beside a small backyard pond. Hands lash rows of capped 2-liter bottles under a wooden pallet, about 4 by 4 feet. Garden soil goes on top, about 4 inches deep, then lettuce, basil and a tomato plant. The raft is pushed out onto the water, riding high. A time-lapse shows the plants growing lush. In the last shot someone kneels on the raft to pick a ripe tomato.

What never appears is telling. The raft never settles as the soil soaks up water. No line holds it, so it should drift into the bank on the first breeze. Muddy water never seeps through the pallet. In generated versions the number of bottles changes between shots, and the raft does not dip when the person kneels on it. We can't prove how a given clip was made, but its raft defies the one law that matters here.

For the numbers below we assume the clip's raft: a 4 by 4 foot pallet on 24 sealed bottles, carrying 4 inches of garden soil after a soaking.

## The physics: 660 pounds on 125 pounds of lift

A floating thing holds up exactly the weight of water it pushes aside. A sealed 2-liter bottle pressed fully under water lifts about 4.4 pounds. Twenty-four of them lift about 105 pounds, and a soaked pallet adds perhaps 20 more. **That is about 125 pounds of lift in all.**

Now the load. Wet garden soil weighs about 100 to 125 pounds per cubic foot [6]. Four inches of it over 16 square feet is more than 5 cubic feet, about 610 pounds. With the soaked pallet, the raft carries **about 660 pounds**, more than five times its lift. Dry soil would float for a few minutes. Wet soil, after the first rain or the first splash, takes the raft straight down. Add a kneeling adult and the gap passes 800 pounds.

For a raft that floats, the lift has to come from something much lighter than water and much bigger than a few bottles. Closed-cell foam floats about 60 pounds per cubic foot [4]. A 4-foot square of 4-inch foam gives about 320 pounds of lift. Planted with light media and wetland plants, its load is under 100 pounds. That raft rides with about an inch below the waterline and lift to spare.

## Five ways it fails

1. **It sinks.** Soil soaks up water and gets heavier; bottles do not get more buoyant. The load outweighs the lift about five to one. The soil washes off into the pond as it goes.
2. **A raft is not a dock, and ponds drown children.** Kneeling on the raft dumps the person in the water. For adults that is a cold, muddy surprise. For small children a garden pond is deadly. In one British count, garden ponds caused 39 percent of home drownings of children under six. Children aged one to two are most at risk, and some drowned in less than a foot of water [3]. Drowning is quick and silent [8]. A floating raft only adds to a toddler's pull toward the water.
3. **Soil clouds the pond.** Garden soil leaches nutrients and fine silt into the water, feeding algae and choking fish. Real floating wetlands use mats of foam, mesh or coir and grow their plants hydroponically. Shoots stay above the water and roots hang down into it [1].
4. **The wrong plants.** Tomatoes and basil want deep, well-drained soil. On a raft their roots sit in water and rot. Wetland plants such as soft rush, pickerelweed and Japanese iris are made for wet feet [1]. They are also the ones that do the pond good.
5. **Bottles, pallets and drift.** Pallet wood soaks and rots in a season. Lashings loosen, bottles slip out, and an untethered raft drifts into the bank or over the pond pump. Floating wetlands are tethered so they can be pulled in [2]. The line needs slack so the raft rises and falls with the water level [1].

## The verdict

As filmed, the raft would sink and invites someone to climb onto open water: one out of ten. The idea at its heart is sound and old. Farmers in Bangladesh still build floating beds of water hyacinth and bamboo each monsoon and grow vegetables on them [5]. In ponds, floating wetlands are a proven tool. Built from wrapped foam, planted with wetland plants and tethered, the backyard version scores a seven. It shades the water, takes up nutrients and looks lovely.

## How to build a version that works

Floating wetland guides suggest covering 10 to 25 percent of a pond's surface to improve water quality [1]. One stormwater program sets 10 percent as the minimum and 50 percent as the most a pond should carry [2]. Leave the rest open; garden pond experts suggest keeping 30 to 40 percent of the surface clear in any case [7]. The kit below sizes the raft to your pond.

1. **Make the pond safe first.** If children under six live with you or visit, fix a rigid grid above the water line, strong enough to hold a child [3]. Chicken wire is not enough; it sags. Do this before anything else.
2. **Size it.** Measure the pond. Pick a raft that covers 10 to 25 percent, in water at least a foot deep so roots hang free.
3. **Use closed-cell foam.** Use XPS or marine EPS board, sized for at least twice the load. Wrap it in pond-safe mesh so birds and muskrats cannot chew it [4].
4. **Frame and plant.** Fix a cedar edge. Push plants through the mesh in coir or net pots with a light coir-perlite mix. Never use garden soil.
5. **Choose wetland plants.** Soft rush, pickerelweed, Japanese iris or other natives that suit your region [1]. Skip anything your state lists as invasive.
6. **Tether it.** Use a braided line to a small anchor, with enough slack for rising water [1]. Keep a rake or boat hook on the bank to pull the raft in.
7. **Never stand on it.** The raft's spare lift is for birds, rain and growth, not people. Work on it only after pulling it to the edge.
8. **Look after it.** Cut back the plant crowns once a year in late fall, and skip fertilizer and herbicide [1]. A good synthetic mat can last ten years or more [1].
