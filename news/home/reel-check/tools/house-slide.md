**The short answer: a slide in place of the stairs is not legal as the only way up, and laid at stair pitch it is steeper than any playground would allow.** A slide beside the stairs is another matter. Built at a playground slope with a guarded top and a soft landing, it is one of the more doable dream-house builds.

The clip type always lands. A family tears out its staircase, a gleaming steel chute goes in, and everyone from the kids to the grandparents rides it down laughing. Real homes do have them. Architects have run stainless tube slides between the floors of city apartments, and one homeowner spent about $150,000 on a custom mahogany slide [5]. We took a typical reel at its word and ran the numbers.

## The physics: 12 miles an hour into the front hall

A code stair rises no more than 7¾ inches per step over a 10-inch tread [3]. That is a pitch of about 38 degrees. The playground guidance from the federal safety agency says a slide for school-age children should average no more than 30 degrees, with no section steeper than 50. For toddlers it is 24 degrees [2].

Speed follows from slope and friction. A rider in jeans on a smooth steel chute has a sliding friction of about 0.3. Dropping 7.75 feet at 38 degrees, that rider reaches about **12 miles an hour at the exit, or nearly 14 in slick nylon.** As a second check, an energy balance gives the same answer: of the 7.75 feet of drop, friction eats about 39 percent, leaving 17.5 feet per second. These figures ignore air drag and the way friction rises with speed. A university study on a real slide found even a 210-pound rider, weights included, leveled off at about 6 miles an hour [6], so real riders on a shallow chute will be slower. The steep chute still sends them well past the bottom and onto the floor.

The other number is space. Bringing the slope down to 30 degrees from a 7.75-foot drop needs about 13.4 feet of run along the floor, plus a flat exit. At 27 degrees it is closer to 17 feet. **A safe slide takes more floor than the stair it is meant to replace.**

## Five ways it fails

1. **The house loses its stair.** The residential code expects a stair between floors: at least 36 inches wide, with risers no taller than 7¾ inches and 6 feet 8 inches of headroom [3]. Even a spiral stair, the tightest kind allowed, has its own minimums [3]. A wall ladder is not a code stair, so the upstairs bedrooms lose their legal way down in a fire. Moving furniture, groceries and anyone who can't climb has nowhere to go.
2. **The slope is too steep.** At stair pitch the chute is 8 degrees past the playground limit for school-age kids and 14 past the toddler limit [2]. Exit speed is higher, and riders tend to lift off at bends.
3. **The exit is a hard stop.** Playground slides end in a near-level exit run at least 11 inches long, set about a foot over a soft surface [2]. Protective surfacing should reach at least 6 feet past the equipment [1]. The reel's chute ends at hardwood, a door and a wall.
4. **The top is an open hole.** Falls cause about 80 percent of home playground injuries [1]. A slide entry at the top of a stairwell is a gap in the guard, waiting for a toddler.
5. **Snags and burns.** Clothes and drawstrings catch on gaps and bolt heads, and strangulation on a cord is one of the ways children die on home play equipment [1]. Bare metal in sun gets hot enough to burn [1], so a chute under a skylight needs shade.

The serious risks here are falls and entanglement, mostly to small children. The fixed version has to make the top hard to reach for a toddler and the bottom soft for everyone.

## The verdict

As filmed, a slide instead of stairs: three out of ten. It is fun, but it is illegal as the only route, too steep, and lands hard. Put it beside the stairs instead, at 24 to 30 degrees, with a gated top and a padded landing: seven out of ten. Houses that have slides nearly all keep a stair as well [5], and designers say to plan the slide early, because it takes up a lot of floor [4].

## How to build a version that works

**Keep the stair and add the slide.** Run the chute alongside the existing stair, or from a landing to a family room on a different line. If a straight run doesn't fit, a spiral slide fits in a far smaller footprint [4]. Ready-made stainless spirals come in modular sections that are built for tight indoor spaces [7].

1. **Measure the run.** Choose a slope from 24 to 30 degrees. Lower is gentler and better for small kids. Check that the run fits with 6 feet of clear floor past the exit [1]. The kit below gives the run for your floor height.
2. **Pick the chute.** A kit chute in stainless steel or fiberglass, with sides at least 4 inches high along its whole length [2], is the simplest route. Store-bought playground slides can work indoors [4].
3. **Engineer and permit.** If the stairwell opening has to grow, an engineer sizes the new header. Pull a permit for the framing.
4. **Frame the opening.** Install the header, trimmers and joist hangers, then patch the floor above.
5. **Hang the chute.** Bolt it to the floor at the top and to supports every 4 feet or so. Check every joint for gaps and bolt heads where clothing could catch [1].
6. **Guard the top.** Close the stairwell side with a guard and put a self-closing, self-latching gate at the slide entry. Add a bar or hood that makes riders sit before they go [2].
7. **Pad the landing.** Use a level exit run and a 6 by 6 foot foam mat. Keep doors, furniture and walls clear of where riders slide out.
8. **Test before riding.** Send a sandbag down first, then an adult. Make the rule feet first and one at a time.
