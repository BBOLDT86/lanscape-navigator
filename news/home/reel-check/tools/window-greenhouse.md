**The short answer: the walls in the reel are the good part; the glass roof, the old paint and the missing vents are not.** As filmed, the greenhouse would cook its plants by noon and leave old glass hanging over your head. Rebuilt with a plastic roof, real vents and a frame of its own, the salvaged windows make a fine one.

Old-window greenhouses are a favorite of the feed for good reason. They look like a storybook, they keep windows out of the landfill, and the clips make them look like a weekend. Some are filmed by real builders, and many lately are generated: faceless, golden-hour, and finished in seconds. We took a typical one at its word and checked what happens to the air, the glass and the paint.

## What the video shows

A pile of mismatched wooden sash windows sits on a lawn, white paint flaking. Hands screw the frames edge to edge into four walls on bare ground. More windows are laid across the top as a pitched roof. A salvaged door goes in one end. The last shots glow at sunset: shelves of seedlings, hanging ferns, a chair, string lights.

What never appears is telling. There is no foundation and no frame other than the windows themselves. Nothing opens. Nobody scrapes or tests the old paint, and nobody wears a mask. In generated versions the panes change size between cuts and the roof windows float without supports. Window muntins melt into the glass. We can't prove how a given clip was made, but the building in the last shot could not be built from the windows in the first.

For the numbers below we assume a house about 10 by 8 feet, walls about 6 feet high, single-pane glass on every face including the roof, and every window closed.

## The physics: 40 degrees too hot

Clear midday sun delivers roughly 250 BTU an hour to each square foot of ground. Glass lets most of it in, the floor and plants turn it to heat, and the heat has to leave through the walls. Single glass loses about 1.1 BTU an hour per square foot for each degree of difference [3]. Run that balance for our sealed 10 by 8 house and it settles **about 40°F above the outdoor air**. Air leaking through old frames trims that by only a few degrees. On an 80°F day that is 120°F inside. Extension engineers warn that without ventilation a greenhouse can pass 100°F within minutes on a hot day [2].

The cure is moving air. Holding this house within 15°F of outdoors takes about 900 cubic feet of air a minute. That is the whole volume of the house, and more, every minute. Engineers size greenhouse vents for one air change a minute in summer [2]. Without fans, that takes vents at the ridge and low on the walls, each about 15 to 20 percent of the floor area [2]. For an 80 square foot house that is 16 square feet at the top and 16 at the bottom. The reel has none.

Weight and wind matter too. A 90 mph design gust pushes about 20 pounds on each square foot of wall. On one 10-foot side that is about 1,300 pounds. Window frames screwed to each other at their edges are not built to carry that. With nothing pinning them to the ground, the whole box can rack, or lift and tip.

## Five ways it fails

1. **No vents, so it overheats.** As shown, every window is fixed shut. Even in spring, a sunny day can push a closed house well past what seedlings tolerate. The fix is at least one opening window near the peak and others low on the walls [1].
2. **Old glass overhead.** Old window panes are ordinary annealed glass. When annealed glass breaks it falls as long, sharp blades. Building codes treat glass sloped more than 15 degrees from vertical as overhead glazing. They require safety glass or a screen under it, though some codes exempt greenhouses used only for growing plants [5]. A backyard greenhouse is a place you stand, often with a hose and a bag of soil. Hail, a branch or a dropped tool can bring a roof pane down on your head.
3. **Glass near the floor.** Codes also flag large panes whose bottom edge sits less than 18 inches above a walking surface. That is where a stumble puts a knee or a hand through the glass [4]. Old windows set straight on the ground put the lowest pane right there.
4. **Lead paint.** Any window made before 1978 may carry lead paint, and paint from before 1960 is likely to [7]. Windows are among the worst sources, because the sash rubs the frame and grinds paint into dust [6]. Scraping or sanding dry, or burning off paint, spreads that dust where children play. Lead harms a child's brain at low doses.
5. **No frame and no base.** Sash windows are made to be held by a house. Screwed edge to edge, their thin wood and old joints carry wind and snow loads they were never built for. Without a base pinned to the ground, a strong gust can rack the box or tip it.

## The verdict

As filmed, this is a pretty box that overheats, sheds lead dust and hangs glass overhead: three out of ten. The core idea is good, though, and the fix is not exotic. Put the windows in the walls, put a plastic roof and vents on top, and give the whole thing a frame and a base. That is a seven: a cheap, lovely greenhouse that will last for years. It is also more work than the clip suggests, because every sash is a different size.

## How to build a version that works

The best published guide we found takes this approach. It uses a frame of 2x4 wall-and-rafter assemblies with plywood gussets, a knee wall at least a foot high, and windows fitted between the studs [1]. It recommends gathering the windows first and designing around them, and choosing sashes 32 inches wide or less [1]. The kit below follows the same plan with a twin-wall polycarbonate roof, which also holds heat better than single glass [3].

1. **Collect, then test.** Gather sashes of about the same height. Test the paint on every one with a lead kit. Set aside cracked panes.
2. **Strip lead paint safely.** If paint tests positive, work outdoors on plastic sheeting. Mist the surface and scrape it wet; never dry-sand, use a heat gun or burn it [7]. Bag the debris and keep children away [6]. Then prime and repaint so nothing flakes.
3. **Check the rules.** Many towns exempt detached one-story sheds and greenhouses under 200 square feet from a building permit, but not all [8]. Ask before you build, and ask about setbacks.
4. **Build a base.** Two courses of treated landscape timbers, corners overlapped, pinned with rebar every 3 feet [1]. Level within half an inch, with the diagonals equal.
5. **Frame it.** 2x4 frames every 2 feet, studs and rafters joined with plywood gussets and glue. Add hurricane ties at the base and purlins across the roof. Set the spacing by your real sash widths.
6. **Raise the glass.** Build an 18-inch plywood knee wall all round. That keeps the bottom pane above kick height and above the code's 18-inch line [4].
7. **Roof in polycarbonate.** Use 8 mm twin-wall sheets with closures on the ends, not salvaged glass. Fit wax-cylinder openers on the roof vents so they lift on their own as the house warms. A better-insulated house runs even hotter when shut, so the vents must work when nobody is home.
8. **Set the windows.** Hold each sash with stops and battens, flash between stacked rows, and caulk the gaps [1]. Make at least two low wall sashes open so air enters low and leaves high.
9. **Test on a sunny day.** At noon with the vents open, the inside should stay within about 15°F of outdoors. If not, add vent area or a shade cloth.
