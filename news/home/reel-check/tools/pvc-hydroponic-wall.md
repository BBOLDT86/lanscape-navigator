**The short answer: the plumbing in the reel is real, but its bucket would run dry in about a day, its strawberries would struggle, and its pump cord runs into water with no protection.** Lettuce is a different story. Lettuce is exactly what this kind of system was invented for, and a sloped, well-fed version will grow it all season.

PVC hydroponic walls are a staple of garden feeds. White pipes, perfect heads of lettuce, berries dangling over the edge, no soil, no weeds. Real growers post these builds, and so, increasingly, do AI video tools, which make them look effortless. We took a typical clip at its word and checked the water, the roots and the wiring.

## What the video shows

The clip starts with six lengths of 4-inch white PVC pipe, each about 8 feet long. A hole saw cuts 2-inch holes every 8 inches along the top. The ends are capped, and the pipes are strapped across a sunny garage wall in a zig-zag, joined by short elbows. A small pump sits in a 5-gallon bucket on the ground. Net cups of lettuce and strawberry plants go into every hole. Then a time-lapse: the wall fills with heads of lettuce, and ripe berries hang down over the white plastic.

What never appears is telling. Nobody tops up the bucket, mixes nutrients or checks pH. The pipes look dead level. The pump cord runs to an ordinary outlet strip on the ground. In generated versions, holes change spacing from one cut to the next, and strawberries ripen within days of planting. Pipe joints vanish into the wall. We can't prove how a given clip was made, but its plants grow without anyone feeding them.

For the numbers below we assume the clip's layout: six 8-foot pipes, 72 plants, half lettuce and half strawberries, fed from a 5-gallon bucket in full summer sun.

## The physics: 4 gallons a day from a 5-gallon bucket

Every plant in the wall drinks from the bucket. A mid-size lettuce in summer uses roughly 0.15 liters a day. Strawberry growers feed each plant about 200 to 400 milliliters a day [4]. Thirty-six of each comes to about 16 liters, a little over **4 gallons a day**. A 5-gallon bucket cannot be pumped dry, so in practice it gives out in less than a day. When it does, the pump runs dry and burns out, and the roots dry with it.

There is a second problem. Capped pipes with holes on top hold standing water until it reaches the holes. Each 4-inch pipe half full holds about a third of a gallon per foot. Six 8-foot pipes would take about 16 gallons just to fill, more than three buckets' worth. Either the pipes never fill, or the bucket empties into them.

Weight is fine. Half full, the six pipes and their water weigh a little over 200 pounds. That is a real load on a garage wall, but well within reach of straps lagged into the studs.

## Five ways it fails

1. **The reservoir is far too small.** A small tank also swings fast in temperature, strength and pH. The usual fix in these systems is a larger stock tank, which steadies all three [1].
2. **Level, capped pipes drown the roots.** The method behind these walls is the nutrient film technique. It runs a thin film of water down a sloped channel so roots get water and air at once. Channels need at least a 2 percent fall [1], and growers find a drop of 1 in 100 too flat [2]. A level pipe of still, warm water starves roots of oxygen. One extension guide puts lettuce roots at about 75°F and asks for at least 6 parts per million of dissolved oxygen. Warm water holds less oxygen and invites root rot [6].
3. **Strawberries are the wrong crop for a film channel.** Commercial hydroponic berries are grown mostly in pots or troughs of substrate, not film channels. A long-lived crop in a system with little buffer suffers badly when anything fails [4]. Even in a well-run university trial, stacked berries yielded about half a pound to three-quarters of a pound per plant over a season [5]. That is a real harvest, but nothing like the clip.
4. **When the pump stops, the plants wilt.** A film system holds almost no water around the roots. A power cut or a stuck pump can wilt plants almost at once [1]. A system that only works when everything works needs a daily check and a design that fails gently.
5. **A pump cord in water with no protection.** A pump sits in water, outdoors, beside a hose. The electrical code requires ground-fault protection on outdoor receptacles at homes [7]. Without it, a cracked pump housing or a frayed cord can energize the water and anyone touching it. That shock can kill. An outlet strip on wet ground is the setup to avoid.

## The verdict

As filmed, this wall would empty its bucket every day, rot its roots in summer and frustrate its berries: three out of ten. The parts are cheap and the method is proven, though. Give it a large tank, a slope, open ends that drain back to the tank, and a protected outlet. Grow lettuce and greens in the pipes and move the berries to pots on the same pump. That is a seven: a wall that turns out salad from spring to fall for about ten minutes of care a day.

## How to build a version that works

Lettuce, herbs, spinach, arugula and chard are the crops film channels suit best [1]. The kit below keeps them in the pipes. Six strawberries go in 2-gallon pots of perlite on a drip line from the same pump, the way commercial growers feed them [4].

1. **Pick the spot and the power.** Morning sun and afternoon shade, or a 30 percent shade cloth; shading cuts air temperature by several degrees [6]. A GFCI-protected exterior outlet within reach, so no extension cord runs across wet ground.
2. **Build a frame.** A freestanding 2x4 frame, or a ledger lagged into the wall studs. It must let each channel fall about 2 inches over 8 feet.
3. **Use potable pipe.** Choose PVC marked NSF-61 for drinking water. Drill 2-inch holes every 8 inches, deburr them, and rinse out the shavings.
4. **Leave the ends open.** Feed each channel at its high end. At the low end, drop an open pipe back into the tank. No channel should hold standing water. When the pump stops, everything drains home.
5. **Go big on the tank.** Use an opaque, lidded tank holding at least ten days of use: 20 gallons or more. A dark lid keeps algae out.
6. **Pump and air.** Size the pump for about a liter a minute per channel [3] at the height of the wall. Add an air stone in the tank to hold oxygen up.
7. **Run water first.** Run plain water for 48 hours. Every channel should carry a thin, even film with no drips.
8. **Feed and plant.** Mix a two-part hydroponic nutrient. Keep pH near 5.5 to 6.0, the range strawberry growers use [4]; lettuce does well there too. Transplant seedlings two to three weeks old with good roots [3].
9. **Check daily.** Pump on, tank topped up, roots white. Check pH and strength weekly; change the whole tank monthly.
