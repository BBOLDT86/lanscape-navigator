Three waves, one shape

In 1930, 2006 and 2026 the party that had just won the White House was swept in the next midterm. Laid over one another, the three decades look alike right up to the wave. What came two years later could hardly have been more different, and that is the question for 2028.

Every era of American politics ends the same way on paper: one party wins a presidential election comfortably, something breaks, and two years later voters punish it in the House. It happened in 1930, after the crash. It happened in 2006, after Iraq and Katrina. It happened six days ago. The interesting part is not the wave. It is the election after the wave, because history has given two opposite answers, and the third era is about to give its own.

Below, the three periods are drawn on the same axes and lined up on the wave midterm, so 1928, 2004 and 2024 — each the incumbent party’s last presidential win — sit in the same column, and 1930, 2006 and 2026 sit in the next. Tap or hover any column to read the three years side by side, or switch the alignment to start each era at its first election.

Part one: 1924–1932, the floor gives way

The 1920s belonged to the Republicans so completely that the decade’s House elections barely look like contests. They won the national House vote by 15.8 points in 1924, 17 in 1926 and 14.8 in 1928, the year they reached 270 seats1,2,3. Herbert Hoover carried the popular vote that November by 17.4 points and 444 of 531 electoral votes, on top of Calvin Coolidge’s 25-point, 382-elector landslide four years earlier8,14.

Then came October 1929. In the 1930 midterm the Republican lead in the House vote shrank to 8.5 points and the party lost 52 seats, leaving the chamber at 218 Republicans to 216 Democrats4. The 1930 wave is easy to misread: Republicans still won the national vote. What collapsed was the size of their lead, by 6.3 points in two years, and in a House that close that was enough — a string of deaths and special elections before the new Congress met handed Democrats the majority.

Two years later the ground gave way completely. Democrats won the 1932 House vote by about 12.4 points and took 313 seats, a net gain of 97, while Republicans lost 101, the first triple-digit loss by any party since 18945. Franklin Roosevelt won the presidency by 17.8 points and 472 electoral votes to Hoover’s 598,14. On the county map above, 2,943 of the 2,957 counties with returns moved toward the Democrats from 1928. Democrats then held the House for all but four of the next 62 years5. On the seat chart below, the 1920s line is the one that shoots past both modern eras.

Part two: 2004–2012, the wave that went back out

The second era starts the same way. George W. Bush won re-election by 2.5 points in 2004, and Republicans carried the House vote by 2.6 and held 232 seats6,7,8. By 2006 the war in Iraq, the response to Hurricane Katrina and a run of congressional scandals had turned the midterm into a referendum. Democrats won the House vote by 6.4 points and took 233 seats — a 9-point swing in two years6,7.

Then the financial system failed in the fall of 2008, and the 1930s pattern seemed to repeat: Democrats widened their House margin to 10.6 points, reached 257 seats, and Barack Obama won by 7.3 points and 365 electoral votes6,8,14. On the county map, 2,441 of 3,108 counties moved left from 2004 — but a red band through Appalachia and the upper South moved the other way. For two years the comparison to Roosevelt was everywhere.

It did not hold. In 2010 Republicans won the House vote by 6.6 points and took 242 seats, erasing the Democratic gains of both waves at once6,7. By 2012 Democrats narrowly won the House vote again, by 1.4 points, but Republicans kept 234 seats6,7. On the overlay the aqua line climbs alongside the 1920s line through the wave and the year after — and then falls back where the 1930s line kept rising.

Part three: 2016–2026, narrow, narrow, narrow, then not

The third era was, until last week, the closest decade in modern House history. Hillary Clinton won the 2016 popular vote by 2.1 points and lost the presidency; Republicans won the House vote by 0.96,8. Democrats’ 2018 wave (8.7 points, 235 seats) was followed by a 3-point margin in 2020 and Republican margins of about 2.5 points in both 2022 and 20246,7. Donald Trump won the 2024 popular vote by 1.5 points and 312 electoral votes, 2,787 of 3,107 counties moved right from 2020, and the House split 220–215, the narrowest majority in a quarter-century8,13.

Then 2026. On the unofficial count Democrats lead the national House vote by about 13.2 points, roughly 64.1 million votes to 49.0 million, with California and other states still counting mail ballots9. They have been called the winners of 254 seats, with 19 races still undecided10. Early analyses put it as the widest midterm House margin for either party since 1974, with seven Republican Senate seats flipped and Trump’s approval at 37 percent in the exit polls11.

Measured as a swing from the previous House vote, 2026 is the largest of the three waves by a distance: 15.7 points, against 9.0 from 2004 to 2006 and 6.3 from 1928 to 1930. In 1930 the wave started from a Republican lead of nearly 15 points, so even a big move left Republicans ahead; this one started from near even and went all the way through.

Two things separate the eras, and both matter for 2028. The first is the starting point: the 1920s Republican majority was enormous and the Depression destroyed it from the top, while the two modern eras began near even and swung around it. The second is what the winners of the wave did with it. In 1932 the crisis was still deepening on Election Day, and voters kept moving toward the party out of power. In 2008 the crisis hit during the campaign, carried Democrats one more step, and then was blamed on them by 2010.

The governors tell the same story one level down. Democrats held 18 of 48 governorships after 1928 and 38 after 193215. In the modern eras the swings are smaller but run in step with the House: 22 of 50 after 2004, 29 after 2008, back to 20 after 2010; 16 after 2016, the low point of either era, and at least 28 after this month’s vote, with Alaska and Kansas still uncalled15.

After the wave

The cleanest way to see the split is to look only at the wave midterms and the election that followed each, measured as the wave party’s own lead in the House vote. The two modern eras each contain two waves — 2006 and 2010, 2018 and 2026 — so there are four real cases and one open one.

Two of the four grew. 1930 to 1932 grew by 21 points, the realignment; 2006 to 2008 by a more modest 4.2, carried by the crash. The other two gave back most of the midterm lead, as presidential-year electorates arrived that were larger and less angry: 2018 to 2020 by 5.7 points, and the Republican wave of 2010 by 8 points in 20126.

2028: three paths

The Watch does not run a forecast model, and nothing below is one. What it does is take the 2026 starting point and apply what actually happened after each of the three modern waves in these eras. The 1932 path is shown for reference only: applied to today’s numbers it would mean a 34-point House margin, larger than anything since the 1930s, so it marks the ceiling of the analogy rather than a scenario.

All three modern paths keep Democrats ahead, which says more about how large the 2026 margin is than about 2028. The middle values — a House lead of about 7.5 points, roughly 246 seats, a presidential margin around 9 — are drawn on the charts above as the 2028 mark, inside a band from the weakest path to the strongest. Seat numbers carry an extra caveat: the mid-decade redistricting fights of 2025 changed several states’ maps, and more may change before 2028.

The prediction markets are, for now, somewhat more cautious than the paths: Kalshi’s traders price a Democratic presidential win at 66 percent and a Republican one at 35, with no single candidate above 20 percent — Jon Ossoff leads at 20, J.D. Vance at 1412. A party favored two to one is a long way from 1932.

The lesson of the overlay is narrow but real. Waves are common; what follows them is not settled by the wave itself. The 1930s line kept rising because the crisis outlasted the party that presided over it. The 2000s line fell because the crisis passed to the party that inherited it. Which of those happens next depends on events that have not happened yet, and on what a new Democratic Congress does with them.
