The receipts are fake. The prices are not.

A pair of Walmart receipts going around says the same six groceries cost $15.44 in 2002 and $57.93 today. The store on them does not exist. This is The Watch’s running ledger of receipt posts, a price database that only checked numbers can enter, and what staples have actually cost, month by month, since 1980.

The image is built to be believed. On the left, a yellowed Wal-Mart receipt in a plastic bag, dated April 6, 2002: milk, eggs, bread, chicken, bananas and coffee for $15.44. On the right, a crisp one from the same store on Oct. 1, 2026: the same six things for $57.93. A reader sent The Watch a repost of it on Sunday with one request: build something that tracks this.

The first finding is that the receipts are not real. Lead Stories traced the image to a TikTok upload on Oct. 13 that the platform itself had labelled as AI-generated, and found there is no town of Sunset Bay in Florida, no ZIP code 32987, no Walmart at 2847 Palm Grove Road and no store No. 4821. The product codes do not resolve to products. The arithmetic works, which is part of what makes it persuasive: the six lines on each slip are consistent with the printed totals. It was the first record entered in the ledger below, and it is graded F.

The second finding is that the fake was not needed. A real receipt from 2006, posted to X in May by a woman who found it in her mother’s things, shows 79 items for $161.87, and reporters at KTAL priced 17 of its lines at Walmart this year. Post cereal went from $1.86 to about $4.97. A gallon of whole milk went from $3.05 to $4.27 and up. A 10-pound bag of russets went from $3.96 to about $4.54, and a dozen eggs from $1.28 to $1.47. Real prices rose a great deal over twenty years. They did not rise the way the fake says they did, and the difference is the reason this section exists.

The eggs give it away fastest. The receipt has a dozen at $9.48. On Oct. 15, Lead Stories found a dozen Great Value eggs on Walmart’s own site for $1.67, and the national average that the Bureau of Labor Statistics published for September was $2.27. The fake is more than four times the government’s average and nearly six times the shelf price at the store it claims to come from.

How a price gets in

A receipt photo is a claim, not a measurement, and since image generators learned to print legible text last year, it costs nothing to manufacture one. So the database is built the other way round: nothing enters because it was posted. A price enters when it has a named source and survives the checks, and every price that does not is kept, visibly, with the reason.

  1. Record. Every price needs a date, an amount and the source it was read from. A malformed record stops the build; nothing is published until it is fixed.
  2. Source. Each post needs at least one linked source. One source earns a flag; the original document read by two or more outlets earns “corroborated.”
  3. Sum. When every line of a receipt has been read, the lines must add to the printed total.
  4. Store. The store number and address have to exist. The Sunset Bay receipts fail here.
  5. Codes. Product codes repeated across receipts years apart, or codes that match no product, count as evidence of fabrication.
  6. AI. A platform’s AI label or a detector result is recorded and counts as evidence of fabrication.
  7. Band. Each price is set against the official average for that item, month and region. More than 2.5 times the average fails; above 1.6 or below 0.4 is flagged. Cheap is never failed, because store brands and bulk bags are cheap.
  8. Pair. When a post shows the same item at two dates, its rise is set against the official rise for that item, or against grocery prices overall. More than double the official rise fails.
  9. Cart. A claimed change in a whole cart is set against official grocery inflation for the same months. Carts under $30 are only flagged, since a handful of items can honestly move far from the average.
  10. Feed. The official download is itself checked: series titles must match the item, values must be in range, and where a fact-checker has published an official figure, the feed has to agree with it to the cent or the build stops.

The grades follow from the evidence. A, verified: priced by reporters or fact-checkers, or a published receipt with an item-by-item method. B, corroborated: the full document is public and was read by at least two outlets, and nothing failed. C, unverified: one source, second hand, or totals only. D, doesn’t hold up. F, fabricated. Prices from A and B posts go in; C prices are held and drawn in grey; D and F prices stay out, except where a reporter separately priced an item, which goes in on the reporter’s authority.

The checker is tested every time it runs, on cases with known answers, before it is allowed to judge anything real:

  • a made-up receipt is graded F
  • eggs at 4x the official price fail the band check
  • a 7x rise against a 2x official rise fails the pair check
  • lines that add to the printed total pass
  • lines that do not add to the total fail
  • a real reporter-priced item passes and is graded A
  • a cart that triples while groceries rise 5% fails
  • a malformed record stops the build

What this cannot do. It cannot prove a receipt is authentic; B means corroborated, not authenticated. It reads what news outlets and fact-checkers reported from social posts, not the posts’ images directly, so most receipts are in the database only as the handful of lines someone published. And official averages are not Walmart’s prices: they are the yardstick, not the answer. Corrections: use the reader note at the foot of this page.
